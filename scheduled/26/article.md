*By Kaizen · 6 November 2026 · Monks Of Market*

Most tilt damage does not start with a bad trade. It starts with a session that should never have been traded at full size in the first place — a tired trader, an open urge to win yesterday's loss back, a mind already somewhere else, and a plan that was never written down before the open. By the time the loss arrives, the decision has already been made badly. This article gives you the check that comes first: a pre-trade gate you run before the first trade, so the conditions that produce tilt are caught while you can still do something about them.

## The short answer: a 7-check pre-trade gate controls tilt before the first trade

Before the first trade of the session, run seven checks on the conditions that produce tilt: body, recovery urge, outside weight, win-streak pull, no-trade acceptability, hard stops, and setup readiness. The gate — not your mood, not your profit and loss, and not the urgency you feel — decides the outcome. If all seven checks pass, trade at normal size. If exactly one non-critical check fails, trade reduced at half size on A+ setups only. If there is a recovery-urge fail or a hard-stop fail, or if two or more checks fail, the third branch applies: a no-trade day, or — only when the recovery urge is the sole fail — a circuit-breaker session, under the decision rule in the branch-rule section. After any break, trading resumes only when every item of the return-to-baseline test passes. That test is a practical self-check set by this article, not a scientifically validated diagnostic, and it is explained in full below.

This gate is for Indian retail traders trading intraday equities and derivatives who already know roughly what tilt is and now need a repeatable routine. It is a behaviour limit, not a profit promise: it cannot make a losing strategy win, and it cannot prevent losses. What it can do is stop you from starting a session already tilted and then paying for that start all day.

## What tilt is, in one paragraph

Tilt is a borrowed poker term for emotional decision-making after a loss or shock, where decision quality quietly drops while confidence does not drop to match it — sometimes it even rises. Palomäki, Laakasuo and Salmela (2013) studied this in online poker, describing detrimental, out-of-control decision-making driven by negative emotions, typically after a significant loss that feels unfair, followed by chasing behaviour in an attempt to restore a fair balance between wins and losses. That study is cited here only for the poker origin and that loss-feels-unfair-to-chasing sequence: it was a qualitative study of poker players, not a study of traders, and it proves nothing about any trader's profit and loss. For the full definition, the trigger list, and the complete sign catalogue, read [Tilt in Trading](/articles/tilt-in-trading/). This article does not re-teach that material. It owns only the gate that runs before the first trade.

### A 30-second sign scan, not a catalogue

Before you run the seven checks, take 30 seconds to scan for commonly reported signs — not as a diagnosis, but as cues to check honestly. Body cues commonly reported by practitioners include a clenched jaw, shallow or fast breathing, an elevated heart rate with no physical cause, raised or tense shoulders, leaning into the screen, and tunnel vision on one ticker or on the profit and loss. Behavioural cues visible in an honest trade log include sizing up right after a loss, re-entering seconds after a stop-out with no new information, skipping the normal analysis step, moving a stop wider or cancelling it, entering before the checklist is complete, thinking about size before finding a setup, and watching profit and loss instead of the chart. These are practitioner-observed self-report cues, not medical measurements, and they vary by person. If the scan lights up, that is information for the gate below, not a reason to push through. The full catalogue lives in [Tilt in Trading](/articles/tilt-in-trading/).

There is also a small physiological reason to take the body check seriously, stated within its limits. Lo and Repin (2002) measured physiological responses of professional securities traders during live trading and found statistically significant autonomic responses to market events, with response patterns that differed by experience in a small study of just 10 traders. The authors themselves called the results suggestive, not conclusive — so this article does not claim that less-experienced traders react more strongly, and it does not use the study to prove anything about trading outcomes. It is simply a reminder that the body is part of the trading decision, which is why the gate checks it first.

## The pre-trade gate at a glance: seven checks, one branch rule

The gate has seven checks, one branch rule, and three possible outcomes: trade at normal size, trade reduced, or do not trade. A no-trade day is a valid gate outcome, not a failure — in fact, it is often the gate working exactly as designed. The checks are scored pass or fail. There is no "mostly pass" and no negotiating a fail away after the open.

The gate matters more for Indian intraday traders for a simple structural reason: there is one session a day, so a tilted day cannot be repaired later the same day in a second session. That is exactly why the gate runs before the open, not after the damage. Every tilted re-entry also adds its own costs, which is another reason the cheapest tilt trade is the one the gate prevents.

## The seven checks

Run this gate before the chart opens, in order, and write the answers down. These seven scored checks, with the branch rule below, are this article's own original contribution, not attributed to any external source. The conditions it screens come from commonly listed pre-session questions; the scored, fill-in gate built from them, with explicit branches, is the new part.

### Check 1 — Body check (60 seconds): sleep, food, breathing, tension

Sixty seconds, four items: Did you sleep enough? Have you eaten? Is your breathing slow and normal? Is there tension you can actually feel — jaw, shoulders, hands? A fail here means you fix the body item before trading, not that you trade carefully. Drink water, eat, breathe slowly, stretch the tension out — and if the item cannot be fixed before the open, it stays a fail, not a "trade carefully" pass. The branch rule then decides what happens next: reduced at half size if it is the only fail, the third branch if it is not.

### Check 2 — Recovery-urge check: is there an open urge to make back a named number from a prior session?

Name the number. Is there a specific loss from a prior session that you are carrying into today with an urge to win it back? If the answer is yes, the session does not start on a fresh slate. It is a critical fail under the branch rule, and the third-branch decision rule below decides whether that means a no-trade day or a circuit-breaker session. A recovery urge is not a reason to trade bigger today. It is a reason the gate restricts today before a single trade is placed. Where this article touches post-loss rules, it adopts the staged companion article The Revenge Trading Circuit-Breaker's rule identically: session stop after 2 consecutive losses or at the daily loss limit, whichever comes first. This is the site's recommended protocol, not an exchange rule and not a SEBI rule.

### Check 3 — Outside-weight check: anything heavy from outside trading that will hijack attention today?

A family argument, a bill deadline, a health worry, an unresolved conflict — anything heavy enough that your attention will keep leaving the chart. Decide now, before the open, not mid-day when you are already in a position. Observable test: if you expect your attention to leave the chart more than once today for this weight, score it a fail; ordinary background noise you can set aside scores a pass. A heavy outside weight leads to a reduced session or a no-trade day under the branch rule. Trading through it and hoping attention holds is not discipline; it is skipping the gate — itself a signal, as explained later.

### Check 4 — Win-streak check: is a recent win quietly pulling toward bigger size?

Wins tilt traders too, just in the opposite direction: a recent win can quietly pull toward bigger size, looser entries, and the feeling that the market owes a continuation. Size is fixed before the session. Wins do not raise it. Observable test: write the pre-session size down unchanged; if the size you now want is larger than that written size, score it a fail. A win pulling toward more risk is a fail to be caught at the gate, not accommodated mid-session by quietly increasing size after a good morning.

### Check 5 — No-trade acceptability: would it genuinely be fine to take zero trades today?

This is the check that makes the no-trade day legitimate. If the honest answer is no — if a day with zero trades would feel wasted, frustrating, or like something to fix — then you are already chasing, and the gate fails. A trader who cannot accept a no-trade day will manufacture trades to fill the day, and manufactured trades are where tilt does its most expensive work. The right answer, on a day when nothing qualifies, is to close the platform and keep the capital. That is a successful gate outcome, not a wasted day.

### Check 6 — Hard stops written down before the first trade

Before the first trade, write down three things: your daily loss limit, your consecutive-loss tripwire, and your session end time. Then pre-commit, in writing, that hitting any one of them ends the session, with no renegotiation. The consecutive-loss tripwire follows the site's recommended protocol exactly: session stop after 2 consecutive losses or at the daily loss limit, whichever comes first. After a loss, the companion protocol governs the cooldown, return size, count reset and re-entry test — this gate only pre-commits you to follow it. For the full protocol, see the staged companion article The Revenge Trading Circuit-Breaker, pointed to here in plain text.

On the daily loss limit number itself, be honest about what kind of number it is. A daily loss limit of typically 2–3% of the account is a widely repeated practitioner convention. It is not a measured optimum — other practitioner sources give different figures — and it is not this site's prescription. The staged circuit-breaker article deliberately prescribes no daily-limit number, leaving it blank to fill in. So pick your own number before the session, write it down, and honour it. The gate's job is not to hand you the perfect number. It is to make sure you have a number, written down, before emotion gets a vote.

### Check 7 — Setup readiness: today's setups pre-listed with entry, stop, targets, and size

List today's setups before the open, each with an entry, a stop, targets, and a size. Anything not on the list is not traded. This check turns "only trade the plan" from a slogan into a gate item: if there is no pre-written setup, there is no trade, however tempting the move looks at midday. Fixing size before the session is also where position sizing does its quiet work — for the sizing maths itself, which this article does not re-teach, see [Position Sizing](/articles/position-sizing/) and the [risk of ruin guide](/articles/risk-of-ruin-trading/). Decide-before-the-session rules of the same kind, such as [the 3-5-7 rule](/articles/the-3-5-7-rule-in-trading/) and the [rules of thumb](/articles/trading-rules-of-thumb-ranked-by-evidence/), work the same way: the decision is made calm, before the open, so it does not have to be made under pressure.

## The branch rule: one rule, three outcomes

Here is the article's one branch rule, without hedging. There is no alternative branch and no "trade carefully" middle option beyond the one stated here. The fails fall into two groups, and the groups are what decide the outcome:

- **Critical fails: Check 2 (recovery urge) and Check 6 (hard stops).** A critical fail is one that removes the session's protection — either the trader is already chasing a prior loss, or the stops that would enforce a stop are not written down or not genuinely pre-committed.
- **Non-critical fails: Checks 1, 3, 4, 5 and 7.** A non-critical fail weakens the session but does not, on its own, remove its protection.

The outcomes follow from those groups:

- **All seven checks pass → trade at normal size.** Normal size means the size you fixed before the session, not the size your confidence suggests after the first win.
- **Exactly one non-critical fail → trade reduced at half size, A+ setups only.** Half size means half your normal risk per trade, and A+ means only your very best setups — the ones already on the pre-written list that meet your strictest criteria. The rule is half size on A+ setups only, not "slightly smaller on anything that moves." If no A+ setup appears, you take no trades — that is the reduced branch working, not a fourth option and not an excuse to widen the list.
- **Any critical fail, or two or more fails of any kind → third branch: no-trade day or circuit-breaker session only.** Two or more fails mean the conditions are stacked against you, and stacking is exactly what the gate exists to catch.

**Choosing inside the third branch.** A circuit-breaker session is available only when the recovery-urge fail is the sole fail and the other six checks pass. Before any loss has occurred in that session, a circuit-breaker session means a session traded from the first trade under restricted, pre-committed terms only: half size, A+ setups only, and the stop rule of session stop after 2 consecutive losses or at the daily loss limit, whichever comes first — with any loss handing the session to the companion post-loss protocol before any resumption. It is not a fresh-slate session with looser rules; it is a restricted session that starts under post-loss discipline. Every other third-branch case is a no-trade day: any hard-stop fail, any recovery-urge fail combined with any other fail, and any two-or-more-fail combination. When in doubt between the two, take the no-trade day.

Why one rule, with no hedging? Because a gate that can be renegotiated at the moment of temptation is not a gate. The trader who is already tilted is the worst person to decide whether the gate still applies. That decision was made before the open, when the trader was calm. The branch rule protects that calm decision from the later, louder one. If you want a different rule, change it tomorrow morning before the gate runs — never during the session it governs.

**Today's gate — fill in before the first trade**

1. Body (sleep, food, breathing, tension): Pass / Fail. If fail, what is the fix, and can it be done before the open? If not, it stays a fail and the branch rule decides.
2. Recovery urge (a named number from a prior session I want back): Pass / Fail. Any "yes" is a critical fail — see the third-branch decision rule below.
3. Outside weight (anything heavy that will hijack attention): Pass / Fail. If you expect attention to leave the chart more than once for it, score it a fail.
4. Win-streak pull (a recent win pulling toward bigger size): Pass / Fail. Write the pre-session size down unchanged; if the size you now want is larger, score it a fail.
5. No-trade acceptability (would zero trades today genuinely be fine?): Pass / Fail. A "no" is a fail — you are already chasing.
6. Hard stops written down (daily loss limit, consecutive-loss tripwire, session end time) with pre-commitment, no renegotiation: Pass / Fail. Any "no" is a critical fail.
7. Setup readiness (today's setups pre-listed with entry, stop, targets, and size): Pass / Fail. Anything not listed is not traded.

**Critical fails: Checks 2 and 6. Non-critical fails: Checks 1, 3, 4, 5 and 7.**

**Branch outcome (one only):** All pass → normal size. Exactly one non-critical fail → half size, A+ setups only (no A+ setup = no trade). Any critical fail or 2+ fails → third branch: circuit-breaker session only if the recovery-urge fail is the sole fail and the other six pass; otherwise a no-trade day.

Log the gate and its outcome in your journal. The journal is where a pattern becomes visible — for example, the same check failing three days in a row — and that pattern is information, not a verdict. For what to log and how, see the [trading journal guide](/articles/trading-journal-what-to-track-beyond-pnl/).

## Worked example: one filled-in gate for an Indian intraday trader

This is a concrete illustrative trader, not a real record. Every number below is recomputed from the research for this article, and no fees, return projections, or savings claims are included.

The illustrative trader has an account of ₹5,00,000 and trades intraday. Risk per trade is fixed at 1% of the account, which is ₹5,00,000 × 0.01 = ₹5,000. The daily loss limit in this illustration is 3% of the account, which is ₹5,00,000 × 0.03 = ₹15,000 — the same figure reached by multiplying the per-trade risk by three (3 × ₹5,000 = ₹15,000). The 3% figure here is an illustrative translation of the per-trade risk, not a prescription: as Check 6 explains, 2–3% is a practitioner convention, not a measured optimum. Half-size risk on a reduced branch is ₹5,000 ÷ 2 = ₹2,500 per trade.

On the morning in question, the gate runs like this. Body: fail — poor sleep, and the body item cannot be fixed before the open. Recovery urge: pass — no named number is being chased from a prior session. Outside weight: pass. Win-streak pull: pass. No-trade acceptability: pass — zero trades today would genuinely be fine. Hard stops: pass — the daily loss limit (₹15,000), the consecutive-loss tripwire, and the trader's own pre-written session end time of 1:00 PM are written down with pre-commitment. (1:00 PM is the trader's own chosen end time, not a market close time.) Setup readiness: pass — today's setups are pre-listed with entry, stop, targets, and size. One pre-listed A+ setup line reads: 100 shares of an NSE-listed stock — entry ₹2,450, stop ₹2,425, targets ₹2,475 and ₹2,500; ₹25 risk per share × 100 shares = ₹2,500 risk at the reduced size.

That is exactly one non-critical fail, so the branch rule gives one outcome: trade reduced at half size, A+ setups only. Risk per trade today is therefore ₹2,500, not ₹5,000, and only the pre-listed A+ setups qualify. Nothing else changes — the daily loss limit does not change on a reduced day: it stays ₹15,000, and the stops stay as written. Only the risk per trade drops, to ₹2,500.

Now suppose the reduced session goes badly, staying on the reduced branch throughout. Two consecutive losing trades at the reduced ₹2,500 risk total 2 × ₹2,500 = ₹5,000, which is ₹10,000 below the ₹15,000 daily loss limit (₹15,000 − ₹5,000 = ₹10,000 of headroom remaining). A trader watching only the daily limit might think there is room for another trade. Under the site's recommended protocol, there is not: session stop after 2 consecutive losses or at the daily loss limit, whichever comes first — so the 2-consecutive-loss stop trips first and the session stops, even though the daily limit has not been reached. That "whichever comes first" is the whole point. A single tripwire can always be rationalised away; two tripwires, with the earlier one binding, are much harder to talk yourself past. For the full post-loss protocol, see the staged companion article The Revenge Trading Circuit-Breaker, pointed to here in plain text.

## Mid-session re-check: when the gate runs again

The mid-session re-check is a scheduled procedure, not a repeat of the morning gate and not a way back in after a loss. Run it in four steps:

1. **Pick the fixed clock time before the session.** When you fill in the morning gate, write one clock time for the re-check beside your session end time and set an alarm for it. Pick a time roughly halfway through your planned trading window — a point where a bad morning can still be contained and a good morning can still be protected. Do not move it because the morning went well or badly; a movable re-check is no re-check.
2. **At that time, stop and re-score what can change mid-session.** Re-score Check 1 (body), Check 3 (outside weight), Check 4 (win-streak pull) and Check 5 (no-trade acceptability), and confirm Check 6 (hard stops) still stands — no tripwire hit, no stop quietly renegotiated. Do not add new setups: Check 7's list is fixed before the open, so a setup that was not pre-listed is still not traded. If a loss has occurred since the open, do not use this re-check to decide resumption — that decision belongs to the post-loss protocol and the return-to-baseline test below.
3. **Apply the outcome immediately.** All re-scored checks pass → continue at your current size. Exactly one non-critical fail while trading at normal size → drop to half size, A+ setups only, for the rest of the session. Already on the reduced branch and another non-critical fail appears → stop for the day; there is no second reduction. Any recovery-urge fail, any hard-stop breach, or two or more fails → stop for the day.
4. **Fixable in minutes, or not.** If the failing item can genuinely be fixed in a few minutes — water, food, a stretch, stepping away from the screen — fix it, re-score that check, and continue only if it now passes. If it cannot be fixed, the outcome in step 3 stands. Do not re-score your way out of a fail by lowering the standard.

This re-check is distinct from the return-to-baseline test that follows. The re-check decides whether a session already in progress continues at the same size, drops to half size, or ends. The return test decides whether trading may resume at all after a break or a cooldown. The re-check never authorises resuming after a loss or after a break; only the return test, together with the companion post-loss protocol, does that.

## The return-to-baseline test: all must pass before you trade again

After any break — a cooldown, a pause, or a no-trade morning reconsidered — trading resumes only when every item of this test passes. The test is practitioner advice and this article's own practical self-check rule. It is not an experimentally validated threshold, and it is not a diagnostic that proves tilt has ended. It is deliberately strict for a reason: a single calm signal must not be allowed to overrule the rest. All must pass, or you do not resume.

1. **Body back to baseline.** Breathing is slow, with no chest or hand tension you can feel. Not "calmer than during the loss" — back to your normal baseline.
2. **No urgency or compulsion to act.** No pull to get back in quickly, no feeling that the next minutes matter more than the plan.
3. **Normal trading logic back online.** You can evaluate setups calmly and you can accept missing a trade without frustration.
4. **Curiosity instead of fear or anger, as a self-check.** This is the least standardised item in the list, and it is worded as a self-check, not a measurement: are you interested in what the market is doing, rather than afraid of it or angry at it?
5. **Able to calmly write the next trade's plan with no pressure.** Entry, stop, targets, and size, written down, without rushing and without sizing the trade to recover anything.
6. **No need to recover losses.** The previous losses are acknowledged and left where they are. If any part of the next trade is about getting them back, the test fails.

If any item fails, you stay out. Take another break, re-run the gate, and test again. Resuming on five out of six is resuming on a technicality, and tilt is very good at technicalities.

### The 2-question test

Two questions sit alongside the return test, as the same kind of practical self-test — not validated research, and not a second, competing rule. First: "Am I acting on my plan or my emotions?" If the answer is not 100% clear, stop. Second: "Would I take this exact trade if I had zero prior trades today?" If the answer is no, or not sure, you are still on tilt. Question 2 here is the pre-trade and return-test form of the question. For post-loss re-entry, the staged companion article The Revenge Trading Circuit-Breaker uses its own winner-test — whether you would take the exact same trade at the exact same size had the previous trade been a winner — and that wording is kept intact for post-loss re-entry. The two forms are not two different rules. They are the same discipline applied at different moments: before the session, the clean comparison is zero prior trades today; after a loss, the clean comparison is a previous trade that had been a winner.

## When a check keeps failing: honest fixes, and why skipping the gate is itself a signal

A check that fails once is a bad morning. A check that fails repeatedly is information about your routine, and the fix usually lives outside trading. Repeated poor sleep is a sleep problem, not a trading problem: fix the sleep, and the gate stops failing on Check 1. A persistent outside weight — a stretch of weeks, not a day — may mean smaller sessions or fewer trading days until the weight lifts, decided at the gate, not discovered mid-position. A standing recovery urge, one that survives several gates, is the clearest case for the post-loss protocol: the staged companion article The Revenge Trading Circuit-Breaker owns that protocol, and this article points to it in plain text rather than rebuilding it. If the urge will not clear, the honest gate outcome is no-trade days until it does.

And one plain statement: skipping the gate is itself a tilt signal, not a shortcut. A trader who runs the gate on calm days and skips it on the days it would fail is not saving time. They are selecting exactly the days when the gate matters most and removing it. If you notice yourself skipping the gate, treat that as a fail — log it, and let the branch rule decide the session. The gate only works if it runs on the days you least want to run it.

## Risk and limitations: what the gate can and cannot do

The gate limits behaviour and loss sequences. It does not guarantee profit, prevent losses, or make any trade profitable. Trading can lose capital, and leveraged futures and options positions carry leverage and expiry and time-decay risk. A perfect gate score does not make a setup good, and a no-trade day does not protect capital already at risk elsewhere. Do not let a checklist create a false sense of safety around size, leverage, or a strategy that has not been validated.

The evidence limits matter too. The return-to-baseline test and the 2-question test are practitioner advice and this article's own practical self-checks, not experimentally validated diagnostics — no peer-reviewed validation of the exact all-must-pass list was found, and the "curiosity instead of fear or anger" item is a self-check, not a measurement. The 2–3% daily loss limit is a practitioner convention, not a measured optimum, and it is not this site's prescription. Palomäki and colleagues (2013) studied poker players, not traders, and their findings must not be presented as proof about traders' profit and loss. Lo and Repin (2002) studied just 10 professional traders, found response patterns that differed by experience, and called their own results suggestive, not conclusive. The gate is a disciplined routine built on practitioner consensus and clearly labelled judgement, not a scientifically proven instrument — and it is honest about that, because a trader who understands the limits of a tool uses it better than one who mistakes it for a guarantee.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is tilt in trading?

Tilt is emotional decision-making after a loss or shock, where decision quality drops while confidence does not drop to match it — full definition, triggers and sign catalogue: [Tilt in Trading](/articles/tilt-in-trading/).

### What are the signs of tilt in trading?

Commonly reported signs include body cues (clenched jaw, shallow breathing, tense shoulders) and behavioural cues (sizing up after a loss, re-entering seconds after a stop-out, watching profit and loss instead of the chart) — full catalogue: [Tilt in Trading](/articles/tilt-in-trading/).

### How do you stop tilting in trading?

Mostly, you do not stop it mid-session by willpower. You gate it before the session with the seven checks in this article, and you break it mechanically after a loss with the post-loss protocol. For the full post-loss protocol, see the staged companion article The Revenge Trading Circuit-Breaker, pointed to here in plain text.

### How do I know when tilt is over and I can trade again?

Use the all-must-pass return-to-baseline test in this article: body back to baseline, no urgency or compulsion, normal trading logic back online, curiosity instead of fear or anger as a self-check, able to calmly write the next trade's plan with no pressure, and no need to recover losses. It is practitioner advice and this article's own practical self-check, not a validated diagnostic — and every item must pass before you resume.

### Should I trade every day?

No. The no-trade day is a valid gate outcome, not a failure. Check 5 exists precisely to make that legitimate: if it would not genuinely be fine to take zero trades today, the gate has already caught you chasing. Some of the best gate outcomes are days when nothing is traded and the capital stays intact.

### What is a pre-trade checklist?

It is the gate itself: a set of conditions checked before the first trade — body, recovery urge, outside weight, win-streak pull, no-trade acceptability, hard stops, and setup readiness — with pass or fail branches that decide whether you trade at normal size, trade reduced, or do not trade at all.

### How much should I risk per trade to avoid tilt?

A fixed small percentage decided before the session, written into Check 6 and Check 7, so size is never decided under pressure. This article does not re-teach sizing maths: for the full method, see [Position Sizing](/articles/position-sizing/) and the [risk of ruin guide](/articles/risk-of-ruin-trading/). The illustrative figures in this article — 1% per trade and a 3% daily illustration — are teaching numbers for one example, not a prescription for your account.

### Is tilt the same as revenge trading?

No. Tilt is the state; revenge trading is one behaviour inside it. Revenge trading is trading to win back a loss immediately, with size and urgency set by the loss rather than by the plan. For the full mechanism, read [Revenge Trading](/articles/revenge-trading/), and for the state itself, read [Tilt in Trading](/articles/tilt-in-trading/).

## Conclusion: decide before the open — trade, trade reduced, or do not trade

The gate is a daily habit, not a one-time fix. Every morning, before the chart opens, seven checks and one branch rule decide the session: trade at normal size, trade reduced at half size on A+ setups only, or do not trade. After any break, the all-must-pass return test decides every resumption. None of that is decided mid-session, under pressure, after a loss, or during a winning streak — and that is the entire point. Run the gate tomorrow before the open, write the outcome down, and let the calm version of you make the decision the tilted version of you would regret.
