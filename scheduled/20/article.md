## The honest answer: there is no single "90%" — but the direction is consistent

Ask why most day traders fail and you will usually get one number back, quoted as if it were a law of nature. It is not. Measured loss rates differ by market, instrument and period — and that variation is itself the honest story.

What does not vary is the direction. In every large, measured population below, most day traders lost money. The question worth answering is not "what is the one true percentage?" but "why does the same failure repeat, in the same shape, across markets and decades?" The answer is a stack, not a single cause: the arithmetic is against the trader before psychology enters, the market structure is against the retail day trader, and the trader's own behaviour then multiplies both. No single layer explains failure. The layers compound.

Day trading means opening and closing positions within the same session. Intraday cash trading does that in cash shares, while F&O day trading does it in futures and options contracts — and each loss rate below belongs to whichever population is named.

### What the measured loss rates actually say

Start with India, because it has the largest and most recent measured evidence.

**SEBI, individual equity F&O traders, FY22–FY24 (study published September 2024).** 92.8% of more than 1 crore individual traders lost money over the three years, averaging about ₹2 lakh each, with aggregate net losses above ₹1.8 lakh crore, inclusive of transaction costs. Only 7.2% made any profit at all, and only about 1% earned profits above ₹1 lakh after costs over the three years. The losses were concentrated at the top: the top 3.5% of loss-makers — about 4 lakh traders — averaged about ₹28 lakh lost each. That ₹28 lakh figure is the average for that top slice of loss-makers, not the average for all traders.

**SEBI, individual equity-derivatives traders, FY25–FY26 (SEBI release PR No. 50/2026, August 2026).** On the basis used in that release, 90.9% of individual traders incurred a net loss in FY25, with aggregate net losses of about ₹1.12 lakh crore; in FY26, 87.7% incurred losses, with aggregate net losses of about ₹91,685 crore and an average loss of about ₹1.17 lakh per trader. Source note: the August 2026 release restates the FY25 comparator on its own basis (top 15 brokers, about 90% coverage), so FY25 and FY26 should be read together only on that revised basis. The lower FY26 loss rate also came with fewer traders — active individual traders fell about 20%, to 78.6 lakh from 98.1 lakh in FY25 — not with most traders suddenly becoming profitable. About 92% of FY26 aggregate individual losses arose from options trading, and individual traders incurred about ₹25,000 crore in transaction costs in FY26 alone.

**SEBI, individual intraday traders in cash equities, FY23.** 71% of individual intraday traders in the equity cash segment incurred a net loss in FY2022–23. Among traders making more than 500 trades a year, the loss-maker share rose to 80%. This is a different population from the F&O figures above — intraday cash, not derivatives — which is exactly why the number differs.

**Brazil, equity-futures day traders (Chague, De-Losso and Giovannetti, entrants in 2013–2015).** Of 19,646 people who began day trading mini-Ibovespa index futures, only 1,551 traded on more than 300 day-trading days — note: day-trading days, not calendar days. Of those 1,551 persisters, 97% lost money net of exchange and brokerage fees (before taxes and course costs). Only 8 of 1,551 persistent traders earned more than a bank teller's starting pay of about US$54 per day. Just 17 of 1,551 earned more than the Brazilian minimum wage of about US$16 per day. Persistence, in this cohort, did not turn into learning: the study found no evidence of learning by day trading for this group.

**Taiwan, day traders on the Taiwan Stock Exchange, 1992–2006 (Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean).** Less than 1% of the day trader population was able to predictably and reliably earn positive abnormal returns net of fees. That wording matters: it is about repeatable, predictable outperformance after fees, not about whether someone was profitable in any single year. A trader can have a profitable year by luck; very few could do it predictably, year after year, after fees.

**Europe, retail CFD accounts (ESMA summary, 2018).** Broker disclosures summarised by ESMA in 2018 showed 74–89% of retail CFD accounts losing money, with average losses per client of €1,600–29,000. This is a historical cross-jurisdiction disclosure range, not a single study and not a current figure — individual brokers' current warnings cover different periods and will differ.

### The "90-90-90" rhyme is a warning story, not a statistic

You may have heard that 90% of traders lose 90% of their capital in 90 days. No study produced that figure. It is a community warning story — a rhyme that survived because it feels true, not because it was measured — and it should never be attributed to SEBI, ESMA, or the Brazil or Taiwan studies. A companion article covers that rhyme in depth; here, treat it as folklore and use the measured figures above as the real evidence.

## Layer 1: Costs eat the edge first

Before psychology, before strategy, before the market even moves, the arithmetic is against the day trader. Every trade pays something: the bid-ask spread, brokerage, exchange charges, GST where applicable, and slippage — the difference between the price expected and the price actually filled. Each charge is small. Across dozens of trades a week, they are not.

The Indian cost stack is worth knowing in outline. The F&O figures that follow are context only: in F&O, STT is 0.15% on exercised/sell-side options (Budget 2026, effective 1 April 2026), and the NSE equity-options charge is 0.03553% per side all-in (₹3,553 per crore, comprising a ₹3,552.99 transaction charge and a ₹0.01 IPFT contribution, per NSE circular NSE/FA/73061, effective 1 March 2026). The detailed charges breakdown belongs to the article on [options trading charges in India](/articles/options-trading-charges-india/); the cost principle that follows applies identically to cash equities: costs are a line item that must be beaten before a single rupee of profit exists.

SEBI's intraday cash study makes the scale concrete. In FY23, loss-makers paid trading costs on top of trading losses equal to an additional 57% of those losses. For the heaviest traders — those making more than 500 trades a year — that figure rose to 72%. Read that carefully: the costs did not merely reduce profits. They sat on top of losses, deepening them by more than half again.

To total your own friction, take one contract note or broker statement, add the spread, brokerage, exchange charges, taxes and slippage across its trades, and divide that total by the number of trades to get your per-trade friction. Compare that figure with your average win to see how much your strategy must earn before costs are even covered.

### The breakeven point: beat your cost drag before you make a rupee

Consider this labelled illustration — a stated assumption, not a forecast: 0.10% friction on ₹50,000 capital per trading day = ₹50/day → ₹12,500 over 250 trading days. That ₹12,500 is gone before the trader's market view is even judged. A strategy that would have broken even before costs now needs to earn ₹12,500 a year just to stand still.

The same logic raises every breakeven win rate. Before costs, a strategy with 1:1 reward-to-risk needs a win rate above 50% to break even; at 1:2 it needs about 33.3%; at 1:3 it needs 25%. (See the article on [break-even win rate](/articles/break-even-win-rate/) and on [win rate versus risk-reward](/articles/win-rate-vs-risk-reward/) for the full maths.) After costs, every one of those breakevens rises — because the trader must now cover their cost drag as well as earn their edge. Many day-trading strategies that look viable on a gross basis are unviable net of costs, and the trader only discovers this after months of paying to find out.

## Layer 2: Recovery asymmetry turns normal losses into fatal ones

A loss is not symmetric. Losing 10% of an account needs an 11.1% gain to recover. Losing 50% needs a 100% gain — the account must double just to get back to where it started. The maths is simple: gain needed = loss ÷ (1 − loss). The consequences are not simple.

| Loss suffered | Gain needed to recover |
|---|---|
| 10% | ~11.1% |
| 20% | 25% |
| 26.3% | ~35.7% |
| 50% | 100% |

This is why a normal losing streak, at oversized risk, is enough to end a day-trading account — no bad strategy required. The companion article on [the mathematics of drawdown recovery](/articles/trading-drawdown-mathematics-of-recovery/) covers this in depth, with [maximum drawdown](/articles/maximum-drawdown/) and [recovery factor](/articles/recovery-factor/) for the measurement side; here, one short table is enough to see the trap. The deeper the hole, the steeper the climb out — and the more risk a desperate trader is tempted to take to climb faster, which deepens the hole further.

### Leverage and position size turn noise into ruin

Intraday moves are small. That is the structural temptation at the heart of day trading: to make a small move "worth it", the trader sizes up. A normal adverse move then costs a ruinous fraction of the account. Tight stops placed to "control" that risk get knocked out by normal market noise, producing death by a thousand small losses — each stop-out individually defensible, collectively fatal.

Oversizing is not greed alone. It is the rational-looking response to that small-move problem — which is exactly why it traps smart people. The numbers show how quickly it compounds. Ten consecutive losses at fixed-fractional risk, before costs, leave about 90.4% of the account at 1% risk per trade, about 81.7% at 2%, about 59.9% at 5%, and about 34.9% at 10%. (See [how losing streaks affect trading accounts](/articles/how-losing-streaks-affect-trading-accounts/) and [risk of ruin](/articles/risk-of-ruin-trading/) for the fuller picture.)

Put it in rupees. On a ₹2,00,000 account, 1% risk means a ₹2,000 maximum loss per trade. That ₹2,000 risk budget turns a normal intraday stop into a sensible share count. At 10% risk — ₹20,000 per trade — ten consecutive losses leave about 34.9% of the account before costs, well past halved, and the recovery maths above then takes over. Position size is not a detail of the strategy; for a day trader, it is the strategy's survival condition. See [position sizing](/articles/position-sizing/) for the method.

## Layer 3: No tested edge — the validation gap

Most day traders trade a setup they have never backtested, validated out-of-sample, or measured for expectancy. A few winning days feel like proof. They are not — a random strategy wins some days. Without a measured expectancy, the trader cannot know whether they have an edge, what their breakeven win rate after costs is, or how large a losing streak their edge should normally produce. So every losing streak feels like a broken strategy, and every winning streak feels like proof of skill.

The validation deep dive belongs to the articles on [why backtested strategies fail in live trading](/articles/why-backtested-strategies-fail-in-live-trading/), [how many trades it takes to validate a strategy](/articles/how-many-trades-to-validate-a-strategy/), [trading edge versus luck](/articles/trading-edge-vs-luck/) and [what trading expectancy is](/articles/what-is-trading-expectancy/). The point here is narrower and more damning: most day traders skip validation entirely, so they are not trading a tested edge with a bad patch — they are trading an unknown, and paying costs to discover what it is.

## Layer 4: Overtrading — frequency as a failure mode of its own

More trades mean more cost drag, more decisions under fatigue, and more exposure to noise. That alone would be enough. But overtrading has its own causes, and they are worth naming because they feel nothing like mistakes in the moment: boredom between setups, fear of missing out on a move, the thrill of participation itself, and the false belief that more trades mean more opportunity.

SEBI's intraday data anchors this layer. In FY23, the heaviest intraday cash traders — those making more than 500 trades a year — had the highest loss rate, 80%, against 71% for intraday traders overall. Frequency did not dilute the failure rate; it concentrated it. The deep dive on this behaviour is the article on [overtrading](/articles/overtrading/), with [FOMO trading](/articles/fomo-trading/) for the fear-of-missing-out driver specifically.

## Layer 5: Psychology under time pressure

Day trading compresses every bias into minutes. A swing trader has days to reconsider; a day trader has seconds, with the profit-and-loss flashing. That compression is the point: the same biases exist everywhere in trading, but day trading gives them the least time to be caught.

### Revenge trading and tilt

A loss triggers the urge to win it back immediately, with larger size — revenge trading. A winning streak breeds the opposite error: overconfidence sizing, where recent wins are read as a permanent increase in skill and position size creeps up just before the market reverts. The mechanisms are covered in the articles on [revenge trading](/articles/revenge-trading/) and [tilt in trading](/articles/tilt-in-trading/). Detailed circuit-breaker and tilt-control treatments are covered in companion articles on the revenge-trading circuit-breaker and tilt control (plain-text pointers only — neither is yet published).

### The disposition effect, outcome-based learning, and variable rewards

Two mechanisms complete the picture — overconfidence sizing after a winning streak is covered above, as the win-driven mirror of revenge sizing. First, the disposition effect: winners get sold early to lock in the good feeling, while losers are held in the hope they recover — cutting the gains that pay for the losses and letting the losses run. Second, outcome-based learning: a rule-breaking trade that wins is remembered as clever, and a well-executed trade that loses is remembered as a mistake, so the trader's memory systematically teaches the wrong lessons.

Underneath all of it sits variable, intermittent rewards — the same reinforcement schedule as a slot machine. Wins arrive unpredictably, which is precisely the schedule that keeps behaviour going through long losing stretches. That is a large part of why traders keep trading after losing, even when the measured evidence says they are losing: the occasional win is more persuasive than the cumulative trading statement. The pattern of giving profits back after a win is covered in [why traders give back profits](/articles/why-traders-give-back-profits/).

## Layer 6: The structural opponent — faster, better-capitalised participants

The opponent is not "the market" in the abstract. It is market makers, proprietary desks and algorithms — participants with better data, faster execution, larger inventory and lower effective costs. The retail day trader pays the spread to those participants, trade after trade.

SEBI's September 2024 study gives the scale, on gross trading profits before transaction costs: algorithmic trading accounted for 97% of FPI profits and 96% of proprietary trader profits in FY24. That split applies to that year and that basis only — not a blended figure, and not one to carry forward into other years. This is structural, not a complaint about fairness. It is why "try harder" is not a fix: effort does not buy lower latency, better data or a cheaper spread.

## Layer 7: Expectations and undercapitalisation — the small-account honesty

Small accounts are forced to risk large fractions to chase meaningful rupee gains. On a ₹20,000 account, a disciplined 1% risk is ₹200 per trade — barely enough to cover costs and a sensible stop on many instruments. So the trader risks 5% or 10%, not out of recklessness but because the disciplined number feels pointless. Then the recovery maths and the streak maths above take over, and a normal run of losses does the rest.

Layered on top is the income expectation. "Quit your job" framing makes every red day feel like failure, which pushes risk up further — the trader is not just trying to make money, but trying to prove that day trading can replace a salary, today. The context from SEBI's FY24 data is sobering: over 75% of individual F&O traders had declared annual income below ₹5 lakh, under-30s were 43% of individual F&O traders in FY24 (up from 31% in FY23), and about 93% of under-30 traders lost that year. The people most drawn to day trading as income are, on the evidence, the people least positioned to survive its maths.

Here is this article's most valuable unwelcome line: on a small account, the maths may say trade smaller, fewer, or not at all. That is not a motivational failure. It is the honest output of the cost, recovery and position-size arithmetic above.

## Layer 8: No feedback loop — why persistence without a journal repeats failure

Without a journal, traders remember dramatic wins and forget the full distribution of their trades. Without a review cadence, a normal losing streak looks like a broken strategy and triggers strategy-switching before any sample is meaningful. The trader changes setups, indicators and timeframes — not because the evidence said to, but because no evidence was ever collected.

Brazil is the proof point. Of 19,646 entrants, only 1,551 persisted beyond 300 day-trading days — and 97% of those persisters still lost. Persistence without feedback just repeats the same behaviour for longer. The study's finding of no evidence of learning by day trading for that cohort is the natural result: time in the market teaches nothing if nothing is recorded and reviewed. The journal method is covered in [what to track beyond P&L in a trading journal](/articles/trading-journal-what-to-track-beyond-pnl/), and the question of when persistence becomes stubbornness in [when to quit a strategy](/articles/when-to-quit-a-strategy/) and [the 3-day rule](/articles/the-3-day-rule/).

A companion article on the day trader failure funnel, the next article in this series, shows the sequence in which these layers typically unfold from first loss to blow-up; this article has explained why the layers exist, and that one shows the order they tend to strike in.

## What the few who survive do differently

No guru promises here — and no framing that you can simply choose to be the exception. The evidence says most people should not day-trade for income. But the minority who survive tend to share five behaviours, each of which directly answers one layer of the stack:

- **A tested edge at small, fixed risk per trade.** The 1% risk-budget rule is covered in depth in the companion article on trading rules of thumb, ranked by evidence (plain-text pointer only — it is not yet published). The point is the combination: tested, and small.
- **Costs treated as a line item.** Survivors know their per-trade friction and their breakeven win rate after costs — not approximately, but as numbers they can state. See [profit factor](/articles/profit-factor/) and [the Sharpe ratio for retail traders](/articles/sharpe-ratio-for-retail-traders/) for how performance is measured net of those costs.
- **A daily loss cap, and stopping for the day when it is hit.** The principle only, set before the session begins.
- **A journal reviewed on a schedule, judging process compliance over daily P&L.** Did you follow the plan? That is the question a review can answer. Daily profit cannot tell you whether you traded well.
- **The legitimate alternative, taken seriously.** Longer-horizon investing or swing trading under the same risk rules — tested edge, small risk, costs accounted for, journal kept — is not a consolation prize. It removes the time compression, the frequency-driven cost drag and much of the structural disadvantage, while keeping everything that actually works about disciplined trading.

## Frequently asked questions

### What percentage of day traders lose money?

There is no single measured percentage — the honest answer is a range by population and period. SEBI found 92.8% of individual equity F&O traders lost over FY22–FY24, 87.7% of individual equity-derivatives traders lost in FY26 (90.9% on the revised FY25 basis), and 71% of individual intraday cash-equity traders lost in FY23. In Brazil, 97% of equity-futures day traders who persisted beyond 300 day-trading days lost. European CFD disclosures summarised by ESMA in 2018 ranged from 74–89% of retail accounts losing money. The direction is consistent; the number depends on who was measured, in what market, and when.

### What percentage of day traders are profitable?

Distinguish two different questions. "Profitable at all" — SEBI found 7.2% of individual equity F&O traders made any profit over FY22–FY24, and only about 1% earned profits above ₹1 lakh after costs over those three years. "Profitably, predictably, after fees" is a much stricter test: in Taiwan from 1992–2006, less than 1% of day traders predictably and reliably earned positive abnormal returns net of fees. Making money in a given period and having a repeatable, predictable edge after fees are not the same thing, and conflating them is one of the most common errors in this debate.

### Can you make a living day trading?

A small minority does, and the evidence above shows how small. Making a living requires clearing every layer of the stack at once: an edge that survives costs, position sizing that survives normal losing streaks, a structural disadvantage that is somehow neutralised, and behaviour under time pressure that stays disciplined for years. That combination is rare, and nothing in this article promises it or offers a path that guarantees it. For most people, on the evidence, day trading is not a reliable income source — and the longer-horizon alternatives in the previous section carry the same risk discipline without the same structural headwinds.

### Is day trading gambling?

It depends on how it is done. Trading a tested edge, at small fixed risk, with costs accounted for and a journal reviewed on a schedule, is a probabilistic business with a measured expectancy — closer to a professional risk-taking activity than to a game of chance. Trading an untested setup, oversized, cost-blind, with no feedback loop, driven by variable rewards and revenge sizing, shares gambling's defining features: no edge, no control of risk, and a reinforcement schedule designed to keep you playing through losses. The gambling parallel holds exactly where the stack's layers are ignored, and breaks down exactly where they are respected.

### How long do most day traders last?

Most do not last long as active day traders. In Brazil, only 1,551 of 19,646 people who began day trading equity futures in 2013–2015 traded on more than 300 day-trading days — the large majority stopped well before that. At the same time, SEBI found that over 75% of loss-making F&O traders continued to trade F&O despite losses in FY22–FY24. Both facts can be true at once: most entrants quit, and many of those who stay keep trading while losing. Persistence is not the same as learning, as the Brazil cohort shows.

### Why do traders keep trading after losing?

The studies measure the persistence, not a single motive — so no single motive should be claimed. The mechanisms that plausibly sustain it are the ones this article has named: variable, intermittent rewards, where an occasional win outweighs a cumulative loss in memory; outcome-based learning, where rule-breaking winners are remembered as skill and well-executed losers as mistakes; and the absence of a feedback loop, where no journal or review ever forces a confrontation with the full distribution of results. Add the income expectation — admitting failure means abandoning the plan to quit a job or replace a salary — and continuing feels easier than stopping, even when stopping is the rational choice.

### Can a small account day-trade profitably?

The maths makes it very hard, and honesty requires saying so. A small account faces the same per-trade costs as a large one, needs the same minimum risk per trade to place a sensible stop, and therefore must risk a larger fraction of the account — which exposes it to the recovery asymmetry and streak maths above. The disciplined answer on a small account is often to trade smaller, trade fewer, or not day-trade at all — and to consider longer-horizon investing or swing trading, where the same risk rules apply but costs and time pressure weigh far less heavily. That answer is unwelcome, but it is the one the arithmetic supports.

### Do prop firms change the odds?

A funded account does not remove costs, the structural opponent, or any other layer of the stack. The trader still pays the spread, still faces faster counterparties, still needs a tested edge at survivable risk, and still faces the same psychology under time pressure — now with an external firm's rules and evaluation conditions added on top. Prop-firm mechanics are covered in their own companion articles (plain-text pointer only); they are not re-taught here, and they should not be read as a way around the failure stack. If the stack is not respected, changing who provides the capital does not change the outcome.

## Educational disclaimer: the risk, stated plainly

This article is for education only. It is not financial advice, and it contains no stock, contract or trade recommendations. Day trading can result in losses exceeding expectations, and it is not suitable as an income source for most people. Every statistic in this article is tied to the population, market and period it was measured in — do not read any single figure as a universal law, and do not use this article as a promise that any strategy, rule or behaviour will make you profitable. If you trade, size positions so that a normal losing streak cannot end your account, account for costs before judging any strategy, and keep a record you actually review.
