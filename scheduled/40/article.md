*By Kaizen · 14 November 2026 · Monks Of Market*

If you have heard both names — options and futures — and you are wondering which one to start with, you are asking the right question in the wrong order if you only ask which one is "better". For a beginner, the useful question is narrower: which first step gives you a defined worst case, a practice path you can actually pass, and a loss you can afford while you are still learning?

This article answers that question directly, then gives you the decision tree and the ordered practice ladder to follow. It does not teach either instrument in full, and it does not crown one instrument as safe. Read it as a risk-first starting guide for Indian retail traders who have not yet traded derivatives live.

A short disclosure first, because it shapes everything that follows: derivatives can lose money quickly, including more money than you put in as margin in the case of futures and sold options. Nothing here is a recommendation to trade, and nothing here promises a profit. Paper-trading success does not guarantee live results. The 1–2% sizing figure and the at-least-a-month practice period in this article are widely used conventions, not rules, regulations, or guarantees of safety.

## The short answer: start with bought options — only after paper-trading both, never with sold options or futures as your first live trade

Start with bought options — and only after you have paper-traded both options and futures for at least a month and passed the gates in the practice ladder below. Never start with sold options. Never start with futures as your very first live trade.

That order is deliberate. A bought option caps your loss on that single trade at the premium you paid. That defined worst case is the reason it is the least dangerous first live step, provided you size it so that losing the whole premium does not matter to you. It is not a promise that you will make money, and it is not safety over many trades: a bought option can expire worthless again and again, and those full-premium losses add up.

Futures come later, only after bought-option discipline and a margin and mark-to-market drill: their simpler payoff still carries obligation, daily cash settlement, and losses beyond the margin posted, demanding daily monitoring and a pre-set exit without exception.

Sold options are not a first step at all: the seller posts margin like a futures trader, takes on an obligation, and — uncovered on a call — faces theoretically unlimited risk.

If you cannot afford the one-trade worst case, cannot watch daily, or have not passed the gates, the answer is neither yet, with the ladder below as your assignment.

## The one distinction that drives the whole decision: obligation vs right

Almost every confusion beginners have about options versus futures clears up once you grasp one distinction: futures oblige, while an options buyer holds a right.

That single difference explains the upfront cost, the worst case, the daily workload, and why the two instruments feel so different even when they are based on the same underlying.

### Futures: both sides are obliged

A futures contract obligates both parties to transact at the agreed price at expiry, unless the position is closed beforehand. If you buy a futures contract, you are obliged to buy at that price. If you sell one, you are obliged to sell at that price.

There is no choice at expiry for a position still held. That is why futures exposure is direct: your profit or loss moves almost directly with the underlying, settled in cash every day while you hold the position. It is also why a futures position cannot simply be left alone when the market moves against you — the obligation, plus daily settlement, forces a decision every day.

This section covers the obligation only as far as the decision needs. The full futures guide — contract details, rollover, and related mechanics — is a separate topic, referred to here in plain text as the futures explainer, not taught in full.

### Options: the buyer chooses, the seller is obliged

An options contract splits the two roles. The buyer gets the right, but not the obligation, to transact at the strike price. A call buyer holds the right to buy at the strike; a put buyer holds the right to sell at the strike. The buyer pays a premium for that right.

The seller takes the other side of the bargain. The seller is paid the premium, and in return takes on the obligation if the buyer chooses to exercise. That is why "options" on their own do not have one risk profile: the buyer's risk and the seller's risk are opposites, and any sentence about options being safe or risky is meaningless until you say which side you mean.

For a beginner, that split is the whole game. Bought options, futures, and sold options are three different positions, not two instruments. Keep them separate in your head from here on.

### Indian settlement in one line: cash for index contracts, physical for stock contracts held to expiry

In India, index options are cash-settled and stock options are physically settled, while index futures are cash-settled and stock futures are physically settled when held to expiry. Indian equity options, both index and stock, are European-style, which means exercise only at expiry — there is no early-exercise story to plan around.

One qualification matters: only in-the-money options held to expiry are settled in this way. Out-of-the-money and at-the-money options expire worthless, and a stock option that expires worthless does not deliver shares. Do not plan as if every stock option ends in delivery.

## Margin vs premium, stated plainly

Futures margin and options premium sound like similar upfront costs. They are not the same kind of money, and confusing them is one of the costliest beginner mistakes.

Futures margin is a performance bond. You post it upfront, it is a fraction of the contract value, and it is returnable in principle — but it is topped up on adverse moves. While you hold a futures position, it is marked to market daily: each day's profit or loss is credited or debited in cash. If your margin falls below the required level, you face a margin call demanding additional funds immediately, and failure to meet it can lead to forced square-off by your broker.

An options buyer, by contrast, pays a premium upfront. That premium is non-refundable and is kept by the seller whatever happens — with one nuance: a buyer who sells the option before expiry may recover part of the premium at the then-current market price. The premium is not always lost in full on every trade, but it can go to zero, and that zero is the buyer's worst case on that trade.

An options seller posts margin, like a futures trader, rather than merely receiving premium. The seller collects the premium but must also fund the margin requirement and carry the obligation. That is why selling is a capital-heavy, high-risk position, not a low-cost way to collect income.

Above all, remember this sentence: margin is not a loss limit. A futures loss can exceed the initial margin posted. The margin is a deposit against performance, not a cap on what you can lose. The premium, for a buyer only, is a cap on that single trade — and that difference drives the starting order in this article.

## The three-way split you must keep visible: bought options, futures, sold options

Whenever you read a claim about options or futures, ask first: bought option, futures position, or sold option? If the answer is unclear, the claim is unsafe to act on. Here is the split, kept visible throughout this article.

### Bought options: low upfront capital, loss capped at the premium per trade — but easy to bleed to zero

A bought option needs only the premium per contract upfront, sometimes a small amount for a far out-of-the-money contract. On that single trade, your maximum loss is the premium paid. That defined worst case is genuinely valuable for a beginner: you can know, before you trade, the most you can lose on that trade.

The cost of that defined worst case is complexity and decay. An options buyer must be right about direction, timing, and volatility at once. A favourable direction alone is not enough if the move is too small or too late. Time decay erodes an option's premium every day, even if the underlying price stands still, and it accelerates near expiry. A buyer can therefore be right about direction and still lose money, and can lose 100% of the premium repeatedly — weekly, if they keep buying short-dated options that expire worthless.

A cheap premium is not a cheap lesson if it expires worthless every week. Low upfront capital makes it easy to start, and just as easy to bleed steadily without noticing the total until it is large.

Two further buyer costs sit beyond time decay, stated plainly. First, volatility: a premium also reflects how much movement the market expects. If the market calms down, the premium can fall even when the direction is right, because the expected movement priced into it has shrunk. Second, spreads and liquidity: you generally buy at the higher asking price and sell at the lower bid price, and that gap is wider — and costlier — in less liquid contracts and at the open. Check the spread before entry; a wide spread is a cost on both entry and exit, separate from decay.

### Futures: simple, direct exposure — but high capital, uncapped adverse loss, daily monitoring mandatory

A futures position is simpler to understand than an option. There is no strike or option-style time decay to model; expiry selection is a contract choice, not an option-pricing input — futures are listed in multiple expiries and the trader chooses one. Your profit or loss moves almost directly with the underlying, settled daily through mark-to-market. For a trader who wants direct directional exposure, that simplicity is appealing.

The cost is capital, obligation, and daily load. Futures need margin plus a buffer for daily mark-to-market debits — money that must be available to top up, not money you hope you will not need. Adverse losses are uncapped in the sense that they can exceed the margin posted, and they arrive daily as cash debits rather than waiting until expiry. Daily monitoring is mandatory, and a stop-loss is not optional. A futures trader who cannot watch the market, or who cannot exit at a pre-set point, is carrying a risk they have not priced.

Simpler does not mean safer. Futures are simpler to understand and harder to survive without discipline, because the loss does not stop at a premium and does not wait politely until expiry.

### Sold options: very high margin, very high risk — not a beginner's first step

Sold options combine the heavy parts of both worlds: margin like a futures trader, plus an obligation to the buyer. The seller's maximum reward is capped at the premium collected, while the risk is not capped in the same way — an uncovered call seller faces theoretically unlimited risk if the underlying rises sharply.

That asymmetry is the opposite of what a beginner needs. A beginner needs a first trade whose worst case they can state in rupees and afford without distress. A sold option's worst case cannot be stated that way for an uncovered position. Hedged or covered selling has different risk, but it is still not a first step: it requires understanding the hedge, the margin, and the exit before the trade, not during it.

The rule is plain: no selling options as a first step, however it is marketed as "income". Selling belongs later, after buyer-side discipline and the margin drill are proven — and the strategy spokes that teach multi-leg and selling-based approaches are pointers to later learning only, not first steps.

## The trap inside 'options are safer': a defined worst case on one trade is not safety over many trades

You will often hear that options are safer than futures for beginners. That sentence is only true with a qualification in the same sentence: bought options cap the loss at the premium per trade, while sold options and futures do not — but a capped loss per trade is not a capped loss over many trades.

Consider a frequent options buyer: each trade risks the full premium, decay eats it every day even on a flat day and accelerates near expiry, and a buyer right on direction but a week late on timing can still watch it shrink to nothing. Repeat that five or ten times and the total is five or ten premiums, each "defined" and each gone.

Futures trap the beginner differently: no option-style decay, so no flat-day bleed in the same way — but leverage and obligation mean losses arrive daily through mark-to-market debits, can exceed margin, and can force a trader out before a correct long-term view arrives if the daily debits cannot be funded.

The dangers differ in kind, not just size: slow, repeatable, total-per-trade loss disguised as small upfront cost, versus fast, daily, potentially-beyond-margin loss disguised as simplicity. "Start with options because they are safer" therefore fails if it stops there — it holds only for bought options, only per trade, and only with sizing that makes total premium loss immaterial plus paper-trading first.

Work through the five questions honestly, in order. Each one is designed to catch a different way beginners get hurt: unaffordable losses, unmonitored positions, underfunded margin, a mismatch between the safety they want and the safety they get, and a hedging need mistaken for a speculative trade.

### Question 1: How much can you lose on one trade without it changing your life?

This is the first question because it decides sizing before it decides instruments. Ask it in rupees, not in percentages of what you control. How much can you lose on one trade — entirely, in one go — without it changing your rent, your emergency fund, your sleep, or your next month's plans?

If that number is small, or if losing a single premium would sting enough to make you chase the loss, you are not ready for a live trade of any kind yet. Your verdict leans to neither yet, with a paper-trading assignment. If you can name a small, affordable worst case and stick to it, you can consider a bought option sized to that number — because only a bought option lets you state the worst case as the premium before you trade.

Never size by leverage — by asking "how much can I control?" — instead of by worst case: "how much can I lose?" The first question rewards bigger positions; the second question protects your capital.

**Branch outcome:** cannot name an affordable worst case → neither yet. Can name one and stick to it → continue to Question 2, with that rupee figure as your sizing anchor.

### Question 2: How much time can you watch the market daily?

Futures demand daily attention. Mark-to-market debits arrive daily, margin calls demand funds immediately, and a position left unmonitored can move beyond its margin before you notice. If you cannot watch the market daily, or cannot act on a pre-set exit during market hours, futures as a live first step are out — however simple the payoff looks.

Options buying demands a different kind of attention: expiry discipline. You must track time decay, expiry week, and whether the move you need is still plausible in the time left. That is less minute-by-minute than futures monitoring, but it is not passive. An option bought and forgotten until expiry week is a common way to donate a premium.

If you have almost no daily time, the verdict is neither yet. Time is not a soft preference here; it is a risk control. No instrument removes the need to monitor the risk you have taken.

**Branch outcome:** cannot watch daily during market hours → futures out. Bought options remain possible only if you can still do regular expiry checks; if you cannot do even that, the verdict is neither yet.

### Question 3: How much capital can sit as margin plus a mark-to-market buffer?

Futures capital is not just the initial margin. It is margin plus a buffer for daily top-ups that must sit available, untouched by other plans, while the position is open. Ask whether that combined amount can sit idle without stress, and whether you could top it up promptly on an adverse day without borrowing or raiding money you need.

If the answer is no, futures are not your first live step, even if the initial margin alone looks affordable. The initial margin is the entry ticket, not the total capital at risk. Options buying, by contrast, ties up only the premium per contract. That is why bought options fit smaller, learning-stage capital — provided the premium is sized so that losing it entirely does not matter, and provided you do not fund ten premiums in a row without noticing the total.

Keep this question structural. Lot sizes and margin requirements are contract-specific and change, so no single universal margin figure or single "amount to start" figure is quoted here. Decide on margin-plus-buffer versus premium-only, not on a number copied from a secondary page.

**Branch outcome:** cannot fund margin plus a buffer without stress → futures out; if Question 1 passed, the path narrows to bought options, otherwise neither yet.

### Question 4: Do you want a defined worst case, or direct directional exposure?

This question asks which kind of safety you can actually live with. A defined worst case means you know the most you can lose on that trade before you enter, and you accept paying for that definition through complexity, time decay, and the need to be right about timing and volatility as well as direction. That points to bought options.

Direct directional exposure means your profit or loss moves almost directly with the underlying, without option-style time decay — but without a cap either, and with daily settlement. That points to futures, but only after the drill, because direct exposure without a cap demands margin discipline and a pre-set exit.

There is no third option that gives you both a cap and direct, decay-free exposure for free. If you want both, you are asking for something neither instrument offers a beginner, and the honest verdict is to practise both on paper until you can state which trade-off you prefer from experience, not from a slogan.

**Branch outcome:** defined worst case → bought options, subject to Questions 1–3. Direct exposure → futures only after the drill, and only if Questions 2–3 passed; otherwise it falls back to bought options or neither yet.

### Question 5: Are you hedging something you already own, or speculating?

If you already own shares or an investment and you want to protect it, your decision changes. Hedging intent points you toward learning hedging approaches deliberately, not toward a speculative first trade that happens to mention protection. A beginner who owns an investment and wants to hedge should still start with the practice ladder, and treat hedging strategies as later learning — the protective and covered approaches are later, not first, pointers only here, not first trades to attempt live.

If you are speculating — taking a view on direction with money you can afford to lose — the earlier questions decide your first step, and the ladder below decides your order. Do not dress a speculative trade up as a hedge to justify skipping the gates. Hedging and speculating have different goals, different position sizes, and different exit rules, and confusing them is a fast way to hold a speculative loss while believing you are protected.

**Branch outcome:** hedging intent → same ladder, hedging approaches as later learning only; it never skips a gate. Speculating → Questions 1–4 decide, in order.

### Worked walk-through: when the answers conflict

**Conflict rule:** gating questions override preference. Questions 1–3 can block an instrument; Questions 4–5 can never unlock one that Questions 1–3 blocked. Affordability (Q1) overrides everything — if it fails, the verdict is neither yet whatever the other answers say.

Take a reader who can afford a small premium loss (Q1 passes), cannot watch the market daily during market hours (Q2 blocks futures), cannot fund margin plus a buffer without stress (Q3 blocks futures), prefers direct directional exposure (Q4 points to futures), and is speculating (Q5). The answers conflict: preference says futures, gating says no. Under the conflict rule, preference loses. The verdict is bought options after the practice ladder — because they can still do regular expiry checks — and futures stay blocked until daily monitoring, margin-plus-buffer, and Stage 2 discipline all pass. Wanting the simpler payoff does not override the two gates that make it survivable.

### The three verdicts: start with bought options, futures only after the drill, or neither yet

Every branch lands on one of three verdicts, each with its cost stated beside it.

Start with bought options, after paper-trading both for at least a month: your cost is premium bleed. You will pay a defined premium per trade, you may lose it entirely, and you must be right about direction, timing, and volatility at once while time decay works against you every day. Size each premium so that losing it does not matter, trade one bought option at a time, and never sell as your first step.

Start with futures only after the drill: your cost is the margin-call and daily-monitoring load. You must fund margin plus a mark-to-market buffer, watch the position daily, honour a pre-set stop-loss without exception, and accept that losses can exceed the margin posted. You earn this verdict only after Stage 2 discipline is shown and the margin and mark-to-market drill is passed — not by enthusiasm for the simpler payoff.

Neither yet: your cost is time, not money — and that is the cheapest cost on this list. Your assignment is the practice ladder below: learn the mechanics, paper-trade both instruments for at least a month with a log, and re-take the five questions when you can answer them from logged experience. Neither yet is not a rejection; it is the verdict that keeps your capital intact while you learn.

"Paper-trade first" is good advice and a useless slogan unless it has stages, single-position discipline, and gates you must pass. This ladder has all three. Failing a gate means staying at that stage, not trying harder live.

### Stage 0: Learn the mechanics — no live money

Learn the mechanics before any live money, and before paper-trading with real conviction. For options: calls and puts, strikes, premiums, expiry, and what exercise at expiry means for index versus stock contracts. For futures: margin as a performance bond, daily mark-to-market settlement, the obligation at expiry, lot size — the contract size that determines how many rupees each point of movement is worth — and what a margin call and forced square-off mean in practice.

Stage 0 is passed when you can explain, in your own words and without notes, the difference between margin and premium, why a bought option's worst case is the premium on that trade, why a futures loss can exceed margin, and why a seller's risk is different from a buyer's. If you cannot explain those four points, you are not ready for Stage 1 yet — you need more reading first, not positions, even paper ones treated casually.

### Stage 1: Paper-trade BOTH for at least a month, single contracts or positions only

Paper-trade both instruments, not just one, for at least a month, with single contracts or single positions only. Paper-trading is named here as a practice prerequisite in plain text, and the discipline matters more than the platform: log every trade, including the ones you would rather forget.

Log three things every day. First, what the premium did on a flat day — this is the lesson no reading teaches, because time decay is abstract until you watch a premium shrink while the underlying stands still. Second, what a mark-to-market debit felt like on a futures paper position — how fast it arrived, how large it was relative to the margin, and what you would have had to top up. Third, what expiry week did to an option — how the premium behaved as time ran out, and how spreads behaved at the open.

Use this minimal log template — five fields, one line per trade per day:

| Date | Position (instrument, strike/expiry or futures) | Premium or margin | What happened | What I noticed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Example: Day 1 | Paper bought call, strike near current price, 2 months to expiry | Premium ₹4,000 (illustrative) | Underlying flat | Premium fell slightly despite no move — time decay on a flat day |

The Stage 1 gate is a log, not a profit total. You pass when your log shows, over at least a month: premium behaviour on flat days, at least one mark-to-market debit you can explain in rupees, and expiry-week effects you noticed before they cost you. A profitable paper month without those observations is not a pass. An unprofitable paper month with those observations, honestly logged, teaches more than a lucky paper profit.

### Stage 2: First live step — one bought call or put at a time

If the Stage 1 gate passes, the first live step is one bought call or put at a time — never a sold option, never a multi-leg strategy, never a weekly expiry as a first trade. Choose a large, well-known underlying, a strike near the current price, and an expiry one to three months out. The longer expiry is deliberate: it gives your view time to work and slows the daily decay compared with a short-dated contract, though it does not remove decay.

Size it so that a total loss of the premium does not matter. That is the sizing rule for Stage 2, stated as a convention to work from, not a guarantee: many traders size first live trades at 1–2% of capital per trade, and the rupee translation should be worked on your own capital. On illustrative capital of ₹2,00,000, 1% is ₹2,000 and 2% is ₹4,000 — so a ₹4,000 premium is at the top of that convention for that capital, not a casual amount. For sizing help — treated here as a pointer only — see the [position-size tool](/tools/position-size/): it is a pointer for sizing discipline, not a re-teach of sizing here, and ruin is a separate pointer: the [risk-of-ruin article](/articles/risk-of-ruin-trading/) explains why repeated full-premium losses matter even when each single loss looks small.

For the first bought option, prefer an index contract over a stock contract on settlement grounds: an index option held to expiry is cash-settled, while an in-the-money stock option held to expiry is physically settled and can deliver shares — extra complexity and capital a first trade does not need.

**Exiting — decide before expiry week, not during it.** Your pre-written plan must name the exit, not just the entry. As expiry approaches and decay accelerates, holding to expiry is the default that bleeds fastest: exit early and keep whatever premium remains if the move has not arrived and the time left no longer justifies waiting, rather than holding by inertia into expiry week. A flat week is the trigger to re-check, not to add: if the premium has declined on a flat week and your original reason still holds with enough time left, holding is allowed only if your plan said so in advance; if the reason has weakened, or expiry week is near, exit early under the plan instead of hoping a late move rescues a decaying premium. Never let "it might come back" replace the exit you wrote before entry.

Stage 2 is passed by discipline, not by profit. Did you trade one option at a time? Did you honour the sizing rule? Did you exit or hold to expiry according to a plan you wrote before entry, rather than improvising? If you broke sizing to chase a loss, you stay at Stage 2 — with paper-trading again if needed — until discipline, not luck, is the pattern.

### Stage 3: Futures entry only after Stage 2 discipline is shown

Futures entry comes only after Stage 2 discipline is shown, and only after a margin and mark-to-market drill passed on paper. Before entry, state in writing: the maximum survivable adverse move in rupees, the exit point (your pre-set stop-loss), and the top-up you could fund promptly if the daily debit arrives. If you cannot state those three numbers before entry, you are not ready for the trade.

The first live futures step is a single futures position, with a pre-set stop-loss, sized to the 1–2% of capital per trade convention — meaning the loss at your stop-loss, not the margin, is sized to that convention. On illustrative capital of ₹2,00,000, that means planning the exit so the loss at the stop is around ₹2,000 to ₹4,000, not sizing by how large a position the margin allows you to control. Translate it this way: **loss at stop = stop distance in points × per-point value**. So, at the two-traps example's hypothetical ₹100 per point, a ₹2,000–₹4,000 loss band allows only a 20–40 point stop distance (₹2,000 ÷ ₹100 = 20; ₹4,000 ÷ ₹100 = 40). If your planned stop is wider than that at that per-point value, the position is too large for the convention — reduce the size or skip the trade; do not widen the loss band to fit the position. Daily monitoring is mandatory from day one, and the stop-loss is honoured without exception, including on days when "it might come back".

Failing the drill means staying at Stage 2. That is not a punishment; it is the ladder working. Futures reward the trader who can state the exit before entry and honour it after entry. Paper-trading both instruments first, then proving bought-option discipline, is how you earn the right to carry uncapped, daily-settled risk.

The two traps are easiest to feel in the same numbers. The example below is clearly labelled hypothetical: it is not a real NSE or BSE contract, it uses no real-contract lot size and no real margin percentage, and all numbers are recomputed inside this article. Both traders face the same underlying and the same adverse move. All figures are stated before brokerage, taxes and charges.

Hypothetical setup: an underlying, call it Index A, starts at 10,000 points. Over four trading days it falls 200 points a day, ending at 9,200 points — a total adverse fall of 800 points, or 8%. Two beginners take opposite kinds of exposure to the same fall. Neither figure below is a market quote.

Trader O, the options buyer, buys one hypothetical call option with a strike of 10,000, paying a total premium of ₹4,000. The call gives the right to buy at 10,000, but the market ends at 9,200, below the strike. At expiry the call is out of the money and expires worthless. Trader O's loss is the full premium: ₹4,000, which is 100% of the premium paid on that trade. The loss is defined — it cannot exceed ₹4,000 on that trade — but it is total, and it is repeatable: five such expiries in a row would cost 5 × ₹4,000 = ₹20,000, each loss "capped" and each premium gone.

Trader F, the futures trader, holds one hypothetical futures position on the same Index A, where each 1-point move is worth ₹100 in this made-up example. The trader posts a hypothetical initial margin of ₹20,000 — a made-up deposit for this example only, not a real margin requirement. The same 800-point fall produces a loss of 800 × ₹100 = ₹80,000. That loss is four times the ₹20,000 initial margin posted (₹80,000 ÷ ₹20,000 = 4). The margin was never a loss limit; it was a deposit, and the loss has run straight past it.

The daily path matters as much as the total, because futures settle daily:

| Day | Index A move that day | Daily mark-to-market debit | Cumulative futures loss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Day 1 | −200 points | 200 × ₹100 = ₹20,000 | ₹20,000 |
| Day 2 | −200 points | 200 × ₹100 = ₹20,000 | ₹40,000 |
| Day 3 | −200 points | 200 × ₹100 = ₹20,000 | ₹60,000 |
| Day 4 | −200 points | 200 × ₹100 = ₹20,000 | ₹80,000 |

After Day 1 alone, the ₹20,000 daily debit has already wiped out the ₹20,000 hypothetical initial margin. Trader F faces a margin call for additional funds immediately, and failure to meet it can lead to forced square-off. Trader F does not get to wait until expiry to see if the market recovers; the cash is debited day by day, and each day demands a decision and, if the position is kept, more funds.

Compare the two traps side by side, not by asking which number is bigger in this one example, but by asking which danger you can live with. Trader O's danger is defined but total and repeatable: ₹4,000 gone, and the same loss available again next week. Trader F's danger is daily, potentially beyond margin, and action-forcing: ₹80,000 against a ₹20,000 deposit, with a margin call after the first day. "Defined risk" and "simple" are different kinds of safety. A beginner must choose between a capped-per-trade loss that can repeat, and a simple payoff whose loss does not stop at the deposit and does not wait until expiry.

## Capital in structural terms: what each start actually ties up

There is no honest single "how much to start" figure, because futures margin and options premiums are contract-specific and change. The structure is what matters: futures tie up margin plus a buffer for daily mark-to-market debits (the money that funds the Day 1–3 debits above), plus daily attention; options buying ties up only the premium per contract, capped per trade — but budget it as a series, not a single cheap ticket, since a premium expiring worthless every week is not cheap over a quarter.

Charges are on different bases and are not interchangeable. For NSE equity options, the all-in transaction charge is 0.03553% per side on premium value — Rs 3,553 per crore of premium value per side, with the revised split from 1 March 2026 of Rs 3,552.99 transaction charge plus Rs 0.01 IPFT contribution. For NSE equity futures, the all-in transaction charge is 0.00183% per side on traded value — Rs 183 per crore of traded value per side, with the revised split from 1 March 2026 of Rs 182.99 plus Rs 0.01. The exchange charge alone is not the total cost: brokerage, taxes, and other charges sit on top, which is why the worked example above is stated before brokerage, taxes and charges.

In short: small learning capital and a small per-trade worst-case tolerance → bought options, sized so the premium loss does not matter. Capital that can sit as margin plus a buffer, daily monitoring, and a passed drill → futures later. Neither structure fitting comfortably → neither yet, on paper until it does.

## Your first 30 days: week by week

The ladder becomes concrete when you give it weeks. This 30-day plan assumes Stage 0 reading is underway and Stage 1 paper-trading starts in Week 2. Adjust the calendar to your schedule, but do not shorten the gates: a month of paper-trading with a log is a minimum, not a target to beat by trading live sooner.

### Week 1: Mechanics and the decision tree

Study the Stage 0 mechanics with no live money and no paper positions yet, then answer the five decision questions in writing, with rupee figures for Question 1 and a daily-time answer for Question 2. Write down your verdict and the cost you accept with it; the Stage 0 explain-it-back test in the ladder is the Week 1 pass mark.

### Week 2: Paper-trade both — dated log prompts

Start paper-trading both with single contracts or positions only, using the Stage 1 template above. Dated prompts, not restated rules: **Day 8 prompt — flat day:** log what the option premium did when the underlying barely moved, and the spread you saw at the open. **Day 11 prompt — normal futures day:** log what a normal daily move debited or credited, and how it compared with the margin.

### Week 3: Paper-trade through expiry-week effects — dated log prompts

**Day 17 prompt — expiry week:** log how the premium behaved as expiry approached versus two weeks out. **Day 20 prompt — sharp adverse day:** log the futures debit in rupees, the top-up it would have demanded, and whether your pre-written exit would have been honoured.

### Week 4: Pass or stay — back to the ladder gate

Week 4 adds no new pass conditions: take your full log back to the Stage 1 gate in the ladder above and check it against that gate. Pass → Stage 2 is earned. Thin log, casually sized paper trades, or improvised exits → stay on paper and repeat Weeks 2–3 with stricter logging, rather than going live to "learn faster" with real money.

## Do not do this yet: seven shortcuts that skip the ladder

Each item is a way beginners commonly skip the ladder:

- Do not sell options first — margin, obligation, and (uncovered call) theoretically unlimited risk; income stories are later learning.
- Do not start with multi-leg strategies — straddle, strangle, iron condor, butterfly, covered call, and protective put are later, not first, pointers only.
- Do not take futures as your very first live trade — only after Stage 2 discipline and the drill, with a pre-set stop and worst-case sizing.
- Do not trade with money you cannot afford to lose — if premium loss or a margin call would change your life, the trade is too big or too early.
- Do not size by leverage ("how much can I control"); size by worst case at the exit already set.
- Do not use a weekly expiry first — decay is fastest and time shortest; first bought options use one to three months out, near-current strike, large well-known underlying.
- Do not skip the log — a paper month without one is impressions, not a passed gate.

## Risk and limitations: what this decision does and does not protect you from

Bought options first protects you from one danger only: an uncapped loss on a single first trade. It does not stop repeated full-premium losses, decay on flat days, being right on direction but wrong on timing or volatility, or sizing up after a win or loss. Futures later protects you from entering an uncapped, daily-settled position unprepared. It does not stop margin calls, forced square-off, moves that gap past a stop, or the daily monitoring load — a stop-loss is a plan, not a guaranteed exit price.

Paper-trading limits apply throughout: paper fills may not match live spreads, paper emotions are weaker, and a paper profit can flatter a method that would not survive live brokerage, taxes and charges. The 1–2% sizing and at-least-a-month practice figures are conventions with pass conditions, not rules, regulations, or promises of safety or profit — see the [risk-of-ruin article](/articles/risk-of-ruin-trading/) for why repeated small losses still matter. This guide is not personal financial advice; if you cannot afford any loss or are unsure derivatives suit you, neither yet — with paper practice — preserves your capital without penalty.

## Frequently asked questions

### Options vs futures: which should a beginner start with?

Bought options, only after paper-trading both for at least a month and passing the Stage 1 gate — never sold options, never futures as the first live trade (see the short answer and ladder above).

### Are options safer than futures for beginners?

Only for bought options, and only per trade: the loss is capped at the premium, but that premium can be lost in full, repeatedly, with decay on flat days. Futures have a simpler payoff but uncapped, daily-settled losses that can exceed margin. Always say buyer, seller, or futures — and per trade versus over many trades.

### Can beginners trade futures?

Yes, but not first: only after the ladder — mechanics, a month paper-trading both, discipline with one bought option, and a margin/mark-to-market drill stating the survivable adverse move and exit in rupees before entry, with a pre-set stop and daily monitoring.

### How much capital does a beginner need for options vs futures?

No universal figure (see Capital in structural terms): options buying needs only the premium, sized so losing it does not matter; futures need margin plus a buffer for daily debits. On illustrative ₹2,00,000 capital, 1–2% is ₹2,000–₹4,000 of risk per trade — a translation for your own capital, not a required starting amount.

### Which is easier to learn: options or futures?

The futures payoff is easier — P&L moves almost directly with the underlying, with no option-style decay to model. Options are harder — strike, expiry, premium, volatility and decay together, with direction, timing and volatility all to get right. Easier to learn does not mean safer to trade.

### Should beginners sell options?

No as a first step. A seller posts margin, takes the obligation, and collects only the premium as maximum reward, while an uncovered call seller faces theoretically unlimited risk. Selling is later learning, after buyer-side discipline is proven.

### Should I paper-trade before trading options or futures, and for how long?

Yes — both instruments, single contracts or positions only, for at least a month, with a log showing flat-day premium behaviour, a mark-to-market debit in rupees, and expiry-week effects. Paper-trading and backtesting are named prerequisites here in plain text; failing the log gate means staying on paper.

### What should a beginner's first option trade look like?

One bought call or put, large well-known underlying (index preferred on settlement grounds, see Stage 2), strike near the current price, expiry one to three months out, sized so total premium loss does not matter — never sold, never multi-leg, never a weekly expiry first. Judge it on plan and sizing, not only on profit, and follow the Stage 2 exit guidance for flat weeks and expiry week.

### Can you lose more than your margin in futures trading?

Yes — margin is not a loss limit. In the hypothetical two-traps example, an ₹80,000 loss ran against a ₹20,000 hypothetical margin, with a margin call after Day 1. Unmet calls can lead to forced square-off, so futures capital must include a buffer and a pre-set stop.

### Is the maximum loss in options really limited to the premium?

For a bought option on one trade, yes — the premium can go to zero. For a seller, no. Over many trades, the per-trade cap does not cap the total. Always ask buyer or seller, and single trade or many trades.

### Do futures have time decay like options?

No option-style time decay: futures P&L moves almost directly with the underlying via daily mark-to-market. That is a simplicity advantage, not safety — margin, daily debits and obligation remain. Futures do have expiry selection as a contract choice (see the futures section).

### What is the difference between margin and premium?

Margin is a returnable performance bond for a futures position or sold option, topped up on adverse moves, and not a loss limit. Premium is the buyer's non-refundable payment for a right that can expire worthless, though selling before expiry may recover part of it.

### Options vs futures for hedging: which should a beginner use first?

Hedging intent changes the branch but not the ladder: same practice path, hedging approaches as later learning only. Do not open a hedge live until you can explain its margin, exit, and both-way outcomes; the first live step, if gates pass, remains a single bought option.

### When is the honest answer 'neither yet'?

When you cannot afford the one-trade worst case, cannot watch the market daily, cannot fund margin plus a buffer without stress, or have not passed the practice gates. The assignment is Stage 0, then at least a month paper-trading both with a daily log, then a re-take of the five questions from logged experience.
