*By Kaizen · 23 November 2026 · Monks Of Market*

Most traders collect options strategies the way people collect recipes — a covered call here, an iron condor there — and then hope the market agrees with whatever they picked. Guy Cohen's *The Bible of Options Strategies* reverses the order. You start by stating what you think the market will do — up, down, flat, or a big move in either direction — and the book hands you only the strategies built for that purpose. A strategy without a stated market view is a coin flip with a fee attached.

That single reversal is the whole book. Everything else — the chapters, the strategy table at the back, the way each strategy is explained — exists to make that reversal practical. This summary explains what the book teaches, how to actually use its strategy table, which strategies to learn first, which edition to buy, and where the book honestly stops being useful.

## What The Bible of Options Strategies actually teaches you

*The Bible of Options Strategies: The Definitive Guide for Practical Trading Strategies* is a strategy reference, not a trading system. It does not tell you when the market will move. It tells you, once you have a view, which tool fits that view — and what can go wrong with it.

The publisher describes the book as a guide to the 60 best options trading strategies, each explained step by step: why it works, when it is appropriate, when it is inappropriate, and how to use it. The book itself works through 58 strategies in detail. The difference between those two numbers is worth knowing, because it tells you something about the book's character: the publisher counts the full catalogue; the author counts the strategies he actually walks you through. Either way, the point is not the count. The point is the organisation.

The book groups every strategy by market purpose, not by alphabetical order. Its chapters cover income strategies, vertical spreads, volatility strategies, sideways (rangebound) strategies, leveraged strategies, and synthetic strategies. The second edition, published by FT Press in 2015, is updated throughout, with new material assessing the current options landscape, discussing margin collateral issues, and introducing Cohen's OVI indicator — his proprietary indicator for reading options activity.

Each strategy is explained the same consistent way: what it needs to be right about, what the worst case looks like before you enter, how time decay and volatility affect it, and how it behaves dynamically as price, time, and implied volatility change — not as a frozen payoff diagram. That dynamic behaviour is the book's professional edge. A static diagram tells you what a strategy looks like at expiry. Cohen shows you what it does while you are still holding it, which is when the real decisions happen.

The author is Guy Cohen, an options trader and educator and the author of the plain-English primer *Options Made Easy*. Reviewers treat his systematic organisation and clear language, not a trading record, as the book's authority. His tone is neutral and professional — disciplined thinking, probability awareness, and structural risk control, with no aggressive speculation and no unrealistic expectations.

## How to read the strategy table — the lookup tool at the back of the book

The most useful part of the book is not a chapter. It is the Strategy Table in the appendix — a summary table that lets you look a strategy up by purpose instead of reading the book cover to cover. Most summaries of this book list the chapter titles and stop. That misses the tool entirely. Here is how to use it.

**Step 1: State your market view in one sentence.** Not "I think this stock is good." Something testable: "I expect this stock to drift sideways for the next few weeks," or "I expect a sharp move, but I do not know which direction," or "I expect a slow grind higher." If you cannot state the view, you are not ready to pick a strategy — you are guessing.

**Step 2: Find your view in the table.** The table is organised by purpose, so your view maps to a family: directional views point to the basic and vertical-spread families; sideways views point to the rangebound family; a big-move-either-way view points to the volatility family; income goals point to the income family.

**Step 3: Read across and compare on the same fields.** For each candidate strategy, the table lets you compare what it needs to be right about, what the worst case is, and what time decay and volatility do to it. This is the step most traders skip. Two strategies can fit the same view and still be very different trades — one might need the move to happen quickly, the other might profit from time passing. The table shows you that difference before you commit money.

**Step 4: Read only the chapter for the strategy you chose.** The book is modular by design. You do not read it like a novel. You look up the strategy, read its section, check the worst case, and then decide.

A worked lookup, in plain terms: suppose you hold shares and expect them to drift sideways — no big move up, no crash. Your view is "flat." The table points you to the income and sideways families. Reading across, you find two candidates that fit: a covered call, which earns income from selling a call against shares you already own, and a long butterfly, which profits if the price stays near the middle strike. Both fit the same flat view, but they are different trades: the covered call's worst case is the shares falling, while the butterfly's worst case is the price moving away from the middle. The table makes that comparison visible in one glance. That is the whole skill the book is teaching — not memorising dozens of strategies, but comparing the two or three that fit your view and choosing on the basis of worst case and what has to go right.

## The four basic strategies — the atoms everything is built from

The book opens with the four basic options strategies, and this is the chapter to read first, whatever your level. Every later strategy in the book is a combination of these four, so learning them properly makes the rest of the book read as variations, not new mysteries.

- **Long call.** You buy the right to buy. It fits a view that the price will rise, and it needs the rise to be large enough and fast enough to overcome time decay. The maximum loss is known before you enter — the amount paid for the call.
- **Short call.** You sell the right to buy. It fits a view that the price will not rise above a level, and it earns from time passing. The risk is open-ended if the price rises sharply, which is why the book insists you know the worst case before entering.
- **Long put.** You buy the right to sell. It fits a view that the price will fall, and like the long call, it needs the move to be large enough and fast enough. The maximum loss is the amount paid for the put.
- **Short put.** You sell the right to sell. It fits a view that the price will not fall below a level. It earns from time passing, and its worst case is the price falling hard — a risk the book makes you face explicitly rather than discovering later.

Notice the pattern: each strategy is defined by what it needs to be right about — direction, timing, or volatility — and by its maximum loss before the trade is placed. That discipline, applied to every strategy in the book, is what separates a reference from a list of ideas. If you cannot state a strategy's worst case, you do not understand it well enough to trade it.

## Income and vertical spreads — the same view, different risk

The second rung of the book is where most readers should spend their time: income strategies and vertical spreads. These two families are best learned together, because they express the same market view in two different risk shapes.

Take a moderately bullish view — you expect a slow grind higher, not a sharp rally. The income family offers the covered call: you hold shares and sell a call against them, collecting income while giving up gains above the strike. The vertical-spread family offers the bull put spread: you sell a put and buy a further-out put below it, collecting income while defining your risk.

Same view, different trade. Follow the comparison through the book's own fields:

- **What the covered call buys you:** income from shares you already own, with no extra capital beyond the shares themselves. What it costs you: if the shares fall, you still own them and still carry the loss; the income only softens it.
- **What the bull put spread buys you:** defined risk — the long put caps the loss, so you know the worst case to the rupee before entering. What it costs you: the long put eats part of the income, so the reward is smaller, and the trade still loses if the price falls past the lower strike.

That is the lesson of the chapter: a spread is not a "better" version of an income play. It is the same view with the risk reshaped — defined loss in exchange for capped reward. The book's income chapter also covers the naked put, the bear call spread, iron butterflies and iron condors, and calendar and diagonal calls, but you do not need to learn them all at once. Learn the covered call and the bull put spread first, understand the trade-off between them, and the rest of the chapter reads as variations on the same idea.

## Volatility and sideways — the strategies beginners misuse most

This is the chapter pair where the purpose-first question matters most, because the same market can make one pair win and the other lose.

**The volatility pair: straddle and strangle.** These fit a view that a big move is coming, but you do not know the direction. You buy both a call and a put, and you profit if the move is large enough. The trap is that being right on the move is not enough: a bought straddle can be right about the move and still lose to time decay if the move comes too slowly, or to falling implied volatility if the expected event passes quietly. The book's dynamic treatment matters here — it shows how the position's value changes as time and volatility move, not just what it pays at expiry.

**The sideways pair: long butterfly and long condor.** These fit the opposite view: the price stays in a range. You profit when the price sits near the middle at expiry, and you lose when it moves away. Here the trap is reversed: a sold straddle can be right on direction and still lose to a volatility spike, because a sudden expansion in expected movement raises the value of the options you sold.

The contrast is the teaching. A straddle and a butterfly are not two unrelated strategies; they are opposite answers to the same question — "will this price move a lot or stay put?" If you get the question wrong, no amount of cleverness in the structure saves you. Before entering either, the book's method asks you to check both forces on the strategy table: what time decay does to the position, and what a change in volatility does to it. Most beginners check only direction. That is why these are the most misused strategies in the book.

## Leveraged and synthetic — the top of the ladder, honestly labelled

The final strategy chapters are the top of the ladder, and the honest way to read them is as knowledge, not as a first trade.

**Leveraged strategies** include ratio backspreads — structures that use more long options than short ones to create a position that benefits from a large move in one direction. The idea is worth understanding: leverage in options comes from structure, not from borrowing, and the payoff can be asymmetric. But the mechanics are fiddly, the worst case is easy to misjudge, and this is not where a reader should start. Know what a backspread is; do not trade one first.

**Synthetic strategies** are the proof that the four basic strategies combine into anything. A collar — holding shares, buying a put for protection, and selling a call to pay for it — turns a stock position into a defined-risk position. Synthetic calls, synthetic puts, synthetic straddles, combos, and box spreads all show the same idea from different angles: any payoff can be built from the four atoms. The collar is the one synthetic most readers will actually use, because it answers a real question — "how do I protect a position I already own without selling it?" — and the book treats it as a hedging tool, not a speculation.

Neither chapter needs a worked example from us. The book is the reference for the detail; this summary's job is to tell you where these chapters sit in the ladder and when to climb to them.

## How to actually use this book — the reading-and-practice plan

A reference book is only useful if you use it like one. Here is a practical plan, not a chapter-by-chapter summary.

**Week one: read the four basics and the strategy table.** Read chapter one and the appendix table together. For each of the four basic strategies, write down in your own words what it needs to be right about and what its worst case is. If you cannot do that without looking, read it again.

**Then: paper-trade one strategy from each purpose.** Pick one strategy for direction, one for income, one for volatility, and one for sideways movement, using the strategy table to choose them. Paper-trade them — no real money — and record each trade with its stated market view and its worst case. The point is not to make paper profits. The point is to practise the book's method: view first, strategy second, worst case stated before entry.

**Skip chapters until a specific view needs them.** You do not need the leveraged or synthetic chapters until you have a view that only those structures express. When that day comes, the book is on the shelf and the table tells you where to look. That is what a reference is for.

**The graduation signal.** This book has done its job when you can look at a market view, name the two or three strategies that fit it, and state each one's worst case without looking. At that point, the next rungs are pricing and depth: *Option Volatility and Pricing* by Natenberg for how options are priced, and *Options as a Strategic Investment* by McMillan for deeper strategy coverage. Both are covered in their own summaries in this book series — the Best Option Trading Books hub lists the full reading ladder.

**One honest limitation to carry with you.** The book's examples come from US stock options, and its tax material is US law. Indian market mechanics — expiries, contract structure, and tax treatment — differ, so translate the concepts rather than copying the trades. The book's decision structure — view, purpose, strategy, worst case — transfers completely. Its contract specifics do not.

## Which edition to buy — and the honest limits of the book

Buy the second edition. It is the updated version, published by FT Press in 2015, with new material on the current options landscape, margin collateral issues, and the OVI indicator. The first edition dates from 2005, and there is a second reason to avoid it: readers have reported errors in the first edition's worked examples and charts. That is stated here qualitatively and deliberately — no disputed figure is reproduced in this summary, and none should be trusted from memory. If you own the first edition, follow the book's decision structure and verify any worked number before trading it. If you are buying now, the second edition is the one to get.

The book's limits are as clearly stated by reviewers as its strengths, and they are worth knowing before you buy:

- **It is a reference, not a trading system.** It teaches you which strategy fits a view, not when the market will move. If you want a complete trading system, this book will not give you one — and it does not pretend to.
- **It is not for complete beginners.** Readers with no prior exposure to options fundamentals will struggle. If you have not yet read a primer — a book like *Options Made Easy* by the same author, or the beginner titles in this series — start there.
- **It is US-focused.** It does not cover futures options or foreign-currency options in depth, and its tax material is US law.
- **It will not make you an expert on any single strategy.** A reader who wants depth on one structure will outgrow this book and need a dedicated treatment. That is the nature of a reference: breadth first, depth elsewhere.

None of those limits is a reason not to own it. They are reasons to own it for the right job: as the book you reach for when you have a view and need to know which strategies fit it, what each one risks, and how to compare them.

## The expectancy tie-in: record the view, not just the trade

A strategy is only a lesson if it is recorded. When you paper-trade the ladder above, journal every trade with three things: the market view you stated before entering, the strategy you chose for that view, and the worst case you accepted. After twenty trades, review the journal — not for profit and loss, but for whether your stated views were right, and whether the strategies you picked actually matched those views. Most traders discover that their losses come from a mismatch between view and structure, not from a bad strategy. That is exactly what this book is designed to fix, and it is why recording the view matters as much as recording the result.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is The Bible of Options Strategies?

It is a strategy reference by Guy Cohen that organises options strategies by market purpose — direction, income, volatility, sideways movement, leverage, and synthetic structures — so a reader can start from what they think the market will do and find the strategies built for that view. Each strategy is explained step by step: why it works, when it is appropriate, when it is inappropriate, and how to use it.

### How many strategies are in The Bible of Options Strategies?

The publisher describes the book as a guide to the 60 best options trading strategies. The book itself works through 58 strategies in detail. The difference is between the publisher's catalogue count and the strategies the author actually walks through — either way, the book's value is its organisation by purpose, not the count.

### Who wrote The Bible of Options Strategies?

Guy Cohen, an options trader and educator and the author of the plain-English primer *Options Made Easy*. He is also the creator of the OVI indicator, which the second edition introduces.

### Is The Bible of Options Strategies good for beginners?

Not for complete beginners. Readers with no prior exposure to options fundamentals will struggle, because the book assumes you already know what a call and a put are. It is best for traders who have read a primer or a playbook and now want a reference for choosing between strategies.

### Is The Bible of Options Strategies worth reading?

Yes, for the right job. If you want one reference that answers "which strategy fits this market view?" and shows you each candidate's worst case, it is worth owning. If you want a complete trading system, a pricing theory, or depth on a single strategy, you will outgrow it — those are different books.

### What is the strategy table in The Bible of Options Strategies?

It is a summary table in the book's appendix that lets you look a strategy up by purpose. You state your market view, find the purpose family that matches it, and compare the candidate strategies on the same fields — what each needs to be right about, what its worst case is, and what time decay and volatility do to it. It turns the book from a catalogue into a lookup tool.

### The Bible of Options Strategies vs Options as a Strategic Investment — which should I read first?

Read Cohen's book first if your question is "which strategy fits my market view?" — it is organised for quick lookup and comparison. Read McMillan's *Options as a Strategic Investment* when you want deeper coverage of individual strategies and their mechanics. In this series' reading ladder, Cohen is the selection reference and McMillan is the depth reference.

### Which edition of The Bible of Options Strategies should I buy?

The second edition, published by FT Press in 2015. It is updated throughout, with new material on the current options landscape, margin collateral issues, and the OVI indicator. The first edition, from 2005, has reported errors in its worked examples, so the second edition is the one to buy.

### Does The Bible of Options Strategies cover taxes?

The first edition includes a chapter on taxation for stock and options traders, but it covers US tax law. Indian readers should treat it as background only — Indian tax treatment of options differs, and this book is not a guide to it.

### What is the OVI indicator in The Bible of Options Strategies?

The OVI is Guy Cohen's proprietary indicator, introduced in the second edition, which reads options activity to gauge market sentiment. The book summarises it in a dedicated section; it is presented as one of Cohen's tools, not as a standalone trading system, and the book does not promise results from it.

### Is The Bible of Options Strategies still relevant?

Yes, for its purpose. Strategy selection by market purpose does not go out of date, and the second edition's updates keep the framework current. What does not transfer is the book's US-specific detail — its stock-option examples and US tax chapter must be translated, not copied, by Indian readers.

### Can I trade options after reading The Bible of Options Strategies?

Reading it will teach you how to choose a strategy for a market view and what each strategy risks, but it will not by itself make you a profitable trader. Paper-trade the strategies first, journal every trade with its stated view and worst case, and review after twenty trades before risking real money. A strategy reference is one part of a trading education — expectancy, position sizing, and risk management are the others.
