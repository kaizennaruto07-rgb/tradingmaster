*By Kaizen · 2 November 2026 · Monks Of Market*

You have probably heard the sentence behind the 90-90-90 rule in trading before you ever placed a trade: 90% of traders lose 90% of their money within the first 90 days.

It is short, scary, and easy to remember. That is exactly why it survives. This article asks the question most pages skip past: is it true?

The short answer is no — not as a statistic. No published study ever produced the 90-90-90 triple. But that does not mean traders mostly win. The measured numbers behind the rhyme are nearly as harsh, and they point to something far more useful than fate: repeatable behaviour that can be changed.

Trading, especially leveraged derivatives and intraday trading, carries risk of loss of capital, including through leverage, expiry and time decay, and accumulated transaction costs. Nothing in this article is a profit promise, a stock pick, or a trade call.

## Is the 90-90-90 Rule True? No — It Is a Rhyme, Not a Measurement

The 90-90-90 rule is a folk saying wearing a statistic's clothes. It is a memorable rhyme, not a measurement.

That distinction matters in both directions. On one side, you should not accept the triple as a proven fact about your own chances. On the other side, you should not dismiss it as a pure scare story either. The absence of one exact triple does not mean most traders win — the studies below, each in its own market and period, find loss rates that are high by any honest standard.

So treat the rule for what it is: a warning, not a verdict. The rest of this article replaces the rhyme with measured data, shows how the losses actually happen in arithmetic you can check, and ends with a checklist for your first 90 days.

## What the Rule Actually Claims: The Three 90s, Unpacked

The triple makes three separate claims. They need to be tested separately, because the evidence treats each one differently.

### 90% of traders lose

This is a claim about how many traders lose money. It is the part closest to the real data — several large studies do find that a large majority of retail traders in the markets studied lost money — but no study puts the figure at exactly 90% for traders in general, across all markets and periods.

### They lose 90% of their money

This is a claim about how much is lost. The measured data does not support a fixed 90% loss figure. What the studies actually report are averages and ranges: some traders lose a little, some lose a great deal, and a small group at the worst end loses far more than the average. A single fixed percentage hides that spread.

### Within the first 90 days

This is a claim about how fast the losses happen. No study in the evidence below measures a 90-day triple. The studies measure windows of a year, three years, or hundreds of trading sessions. The speed in the rhyme is part of the mnemonic, not part of any published measurement.

## Where the Number Came From: The 1999 NASAA Finding and How 70% Became 90%

The closest traceable root of the "90%" idea is not a modern study of all trading. It is a 1999 review of United States day trading, commissioned by the North American Securities Administrators Association (NASAA).

Analyst Ronald L. Johnson, reviewing customer accounts for the NASAA Day Trading Project Group Report (August 1999), concluded that about 70% of public traders would "not only lose, but almost certainly lose everything they invest" — for US day traders in that 1999 sample, not for traders in general today. NASAA's own testimony summarised this as more than 70% losing money, with only about 12% showing the potential to be profitable.

The sample behind those figures was small and narrow, and that limitation must travel with the numbers. Johnson reviewed 26 randomly selected accounts at a single branch office of a single day-trading firm in Massachusetts, covering four months of account activity.

Of those 26 accounts, only 3 — 11.5% of the sample — showed the ability to conduct profitable short-term trading. Among the 17 accounts with statistically significant day trading, only 1 was successful, and even that account's returns were not commensurate with its risk.

Separately, as corroborating investigation evidence — not as part of the Johnson 26-account sample — NASAA cited evidence from another firm's branch office where 67 of 68 accounts lost money. That figure must not be merged into the Johnson sample, and neither figure is a market-wide rate.

The United States Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations discussed the NASAA report in its publication "Day Trading: Case Studies and Conclusions" (January 2000, with the hearing discussion dated February 2000). Senate staff found the industry's criticism valid on one point: the sample was too limited to draw broad conclusions about the day-trading industry as a whole, while still finding the report instructive and consistent with anecdotal evidence.

How did 70% become 90%, and where did the other two 90s come from? That part of the story is traced by researchers, with uncertainty stated: as the idea travelled through retail forex and CFD communities over two decades, the 70% appears to have been rounded up to 90% and to have picked up the triple-nine mnemonic along the way. This rounding story has no primary source documenting the transmission step, so it should be read as a plausible lineage account, not as a documented historical fact. Above all, 90% must never be attributed to NASAA — NASAA said about 70%, on that small 1999 sample.

### EU retail CFD accounts: 74–89% lost money

Analyses by national competent authorities across European Union jurisdictions, summarised by the European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) in March 2018, found that 74–89% of retail CFD accounts typically lost money. This is a cross-jurisdiction range for EU retail CFD accounts in those March 2018 analyses — not a single EU-wide rate, and not a rate for equities, futures, options, or Indian F&O.

In the same March 2018 analyses, average losses per client ranged from €1,600 to €29,000 across jurisdictions. That is a range, not a fixed loss figure, and certainly not a 90% loss figure. These findings prompted ESMA's March 2018 measures, which restricted the marketing, distribution, and sale of CFDs to retail clients, including leverage limits, and prohibited retail binary options.

### Brazil day trading: the longer traders persisted, the fewer profited

A working paper by Chague, De-Losso, and Giovannetti, "Day Trading for a Living?", followed all 19,646 individuals who began day trading Brazilian mini-Ibovespa equity index futures for the first time in 2013–2015, using data supplied by the Brazilian securities regulator. This is Brazilian equity index futures day trading only — not all trading, not Indian traders, and not equities generally.

The persistence curve is the paper's most counter-intuitive finding. The paper's net measure counts profit after exchange and brokerage fees, but excludes income taxes and expenses such as courses and platforms. On that measure, the share obtaining a positive net profit fell the longer people traded:

| Days traded | Traders in group | Share with positive net profit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 day | 1,111 | 29.8% |
| 2–50 days | 9,978 | 15.5% |
| 51–100 days | 3,100 | 8.9% |
| 101–200 days | 2,738 | 6.8% |
| 201–300 days | 1,168 | 5.4% |
| Beyond 300 days | 1,551 | 3.0% |

Only 1,551 of the 19,646 starters (7.9%) persisted beyond 300 trading sessions, and 97% of those persistent traders lost money — only 3.0% had a positive net result. The authors concluded it is virtually impossible for an individual to day trade for a living in that market. Note the caveat: the 3.0% figure is for traders persisting beyond 300 days, not for all 19,646 starters, whose profitable share at 1 day was 29.8%. And persistence itself is the warning here: simply lasting longer, without an edge and tight risk control, did not turn losses into profits.

### SEBI F&O: 89% in FY22, 93% over FY22–FY24, about 91% in FY25

For Indian readers, the home-market data comes from the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI). Keep the three windows strictly separate — they are different periods, and mixing them is how the real numbers get misquoted.

In FY22, SEBI's January 2023 study found that 89% of individual traders in the Indian equity F&O segment incurred losses, with an average loss of about ₹1.1 lakh among loss-makers. That population is Indian individual equity F&O traders in FY22 only, in a sample from top brokers.

Over the three-year window FY22–FY24, SEBI's September 2024 study found that 93% of over 1 crore individual F&O traders in India incurred losses, averaging around ₹2 lakh per trader. That ₹2 lakh figure is the three-year total per trader across FY22–FY24, inclusive of transaction costs — not a per-year figure. Publishing it as ₹2 lakh per year would overstate it threefold.

In the same FY22–FY24 study, aggregate losses of individual F&O traders exceeded ₹1.8 lakh crore over the three years. The top 3.5% of loss-makers — approximately 4 lakh traders — faced an average loss of about ₹28 lakh each over the three years, inclusive of transaction costs. That ₹28 lakh figure belongs to the worst 3.5% only; it is not the typical trader's loss. Only 7.2% of individual F&O traders made any profit over the three years, and only 1% managed to earn profits exceeding ₹1 lakh after adjusting for transaction costs. The 1% figure is for profits over ₹1 lakh, not for any profit at all.

Separately, for FY24 alone, the September 2024 study found that 91.1% of individual F&O traders in India lost money in FY24, with an average net loss of about ₹1.2 lakh per person in FY24.

The latest confirmed SEBI release is the FY25 study (July 2025). In FY25, nearly 91% of individual traders in the Indian equity derivatives segment incurred a net loss, similar to FY24. Net losses widened by 41% to ₹1,05,603 crore in FY25 from ₹74,812 crore in FY24, on the study's top-13-broker basis covering about 96 lakh traders.

The study also found that unique individual F&O traders fell from about 61.4 lakh in the first quarter of FY25 to about 42.7 lakh in the fourth quarter, after SEBI's late-2024 derivatives measures, and that losses eased in the fourth quarter at both aggregate and per-person level — but the full-year loss rate stayed near 91%. Fewer traders is not more traders winning.

### SEBI intraday cash: 7 in 10 lost in FY23

A different SEBI study, from July 2024, covers a different market and must not be merged into the F&O percentages. Among Indian individual intraday traders in the equity cash segment in FY23, 7 out of 10 — 71% — incurred a net loss. That population is individual intraday equity-cash traders in FY23, in a sample of individual clients of the top brokers accounting for about 86% of the individual client count in that segment in FY23. It is not F&O traders, and not delivery investors.

In the same FY23 intraday cash study, the loss-maker share rose to 80% for very frequent traders (more than 500 trades in a year), and was 76% for traders under 30. Loss-makers traded more on average than profit-makers. Frequency alone does not prove causation, but the pattern fits the cost-bleed mechanism below: more trades mean more costs, whether the trades win or lose.

## Why the Losses Happen: The Mechanism

The studies above describe how many traders lose. This section describes how — through five repeatable causes, each tied to a number from the data or a worked example. This is the good news hidden inside harsh numbers: the losses come from behaviour, not fate, so the escape is also repeatable.

### No written plan: reacting instead of executing

A trader without pre-decided entry, exit, and risk rules has to improvise every decision in real time, while prices are moving and emotions are running. That turns trading into reacting: entering because a move looks exciting, exiting because a dip looks frightening, and changing the plan mid-trade because the last trade went wrong.

A written plan does not guarantee a profitable strategy — an untested plan can still lose. But without one, there is nothing to review, nothing to improve, and no way to tell whether a loss came from the strategy or from breaking it. The checklist at the end starts here for that reason: the plan comes before the first trade, not after the first loss.

A filled example plan, in a few lines, looks like this: instrument type — a liquid large-cap equity for intraday cash trading; entry condition — buy only when the price breaks above the opening range with rising volume; stop — a live stop-loss order ₹20 below entry; target/exit rule — exit at twice the stop distance, or at the close of the session, whichever comes first; risk per trade — 1% of capital; daily stop condition — stop all trading for the day if the daily loss limit is hit. No stock is named, and no outcome is promised — the point is that every decision is written before money is at stake.

### Oversized risk: the position size that ends the game early

This is the mechanism that ends accounts fastest. Risking far more than 1–2% of capital per trade — including conviction sizing at 20–30% of capital on an "obvious" setup — means one normal losing streak does account-threatening damage. That damage happens before any question about the strategy's edge is even answered.

The 1–2% figure here is a widely used practitioner guideline for the survival checklist, not a regulator rule, a measured optimum, or a profit guarantee. Its value is shown in the streak arithmetic in the next section: the same 10-trade losing streak leaves a trader risking 10% per trade with about 35% of capital, and a trader risking 1% per trade with about 90.4%. Same streak, same strategy, different survival.

If you want to think about survival odds more formally, the companion article on [risk of ruin](/articles/risk-of-ruin-trading/) explains how the chance of ruin rises with risk per trade — and readers who want to run their own numbers can find the risk-of-ruin and position-size tools on this site.

### Emotional exits: one decision, before and after the rule

Consider one concrete decision. Before the rule: a trader has lost two trades in a row, feels the urge to win the money back immediately, and is about to double the size of the next trade and chase the move that has already happened. That is revenge trading in action — an emotional exit from the plan, not a new strategy.

After the rule: the same trader has a pre-decided rule that overrides the urge — no trade may exceed 1% risk, and the daily loss limit ends trading for the day once hit. The live stop-loss order on the open position and the daily limit, set before the session, enforce that rule whether the trader still feels the urge or not. The exit decision was made calmly, in writing, before the emotion arrived; the live order simply carries it out.

That single override matters because the pattern in the data points the same way: in SEBI's July 2024 intraday cash study for FY23, loss-makers traded more on average than profit-makers. Trading more, faster, after a loss is exactly how an emotional decision turns one bad day into a worse one. Related reading on this site covers revenge trading and tilt in more depth.

### Unrealistic expectations: underestimating how long consistency takes

Many beginners arrive expecting fast profits, and judge a strategy after a handful of trades. When the first weeks do not deliver, they switch strategies, increase size, or trade more often — which usually accelerates the losses rather than fixing them.

How long does consistency actually take? No study found in this article's research measures a standard time to profitability. The 18–36 month, or 1–3 year, learning timelines found in practitioner discussion are opinions, labelled as such here, not data. The Brazil curve above is a useful corrective to any timeline promise: in that market, more time spent trading did not by itself produce profitability. Time helps only if it is spent following rules, reviewing trades, and controlling risk — which is why the checklist measures a month of rule-following, not a month of profits.

### Cost bleed from overtrading: taxed on every trade, win or lose

Costs are a mechanism, not a footnote. In SEBI's September 2024 study, individual F&O traders in India spent around ₹50,000 crore on transaction costs over FY22–FY24 — about 28% of aggregate losses on that basis, with brokerage about 51% of those costs. Separately, in SEBI's July 2024 intraday cash study, loss-makers in the Indian equity cash segment in FY23 expended an additional 57% of their trading losses as trading costs, over and above their trading losses, while profit-makers spent 19% of their trading profits as costs.

Those two cost figures use different bases and must never be averaged or swapped: the F&O figure is costs as a share of aggregate losses, while the intraday-cash figure is additional costs relative to loss-makers' trading losses. The qualitative point, though, is the same in both markets: every trade pays costs whether it wins or loses.

Consider a short cost-bleed illustration, using only SEBI's cost shares and no invented per-trade cost figure: a trader doing 5 trades a day for 90 days places 450 cost-bearing trades. Even if the strategy itself breaks even before costs, 450 rounds of costs still tax the account. Overtrading does not need a bad strategy to lose money; costs alone can do it. Related reading on this site covers overtrading in more depth.

Taken together, the five causes describe one path: no plan, oversized risk, emotional exits, unrealistic expectations, and cost bleed. At the end of that path sits the outcome the rhyme warns about — an account reduced so far that recovery becomes arithmetically enormous. That endpoint is where the next section begins.

First, the recovery ladder. A loss and the gain needed to undo it are not symmetric, because the gain is earned on a smaller base:

| Loss | Capital after a ₹1,00,000 start | Gain needed to break even |
| --- | --- | --- |
| −10% | ₹90,000 | +11.1% |
| −50% | ₹50,000 | +100% |
| −90% | ₹10,000 | +900% |

A trader who starts with ₹1,00,000 and loses 90% is left with ₹10,000. Recovery is not +90%. It takes +900% — ₹10,000 growing back to ₹1,00,000 — to get back to the start. Losing 90% is not "almost out." It is a 10-times mountain. This is pure percentage recovery maths on a single capital base, before costs, taxes, and any further trading; it applies only to this one worked example.

Second, the streak arithmetic. Assume a fixed percentage of current capital is risked and lost on each of 10 consecutive trades, with no wins and no costs mid-sequence. A 10-trade losing streak is an illustrative normal losing streak — every strategy has one at some point — not a prediction that any strategy will have exactly 10 consecutive losses.

- Risking 10% per trade: 0.9^10 ≈ 0.349. About 35% of starting capital remains. The account is down about 65%.
- Risking 1% per trade: 0.99^10 ≈ 0.904. About 90.4% of starting capital remains. The account is down under 10%, and the trader is still in the game.

Same streak, same strategy, different survival. That is why position size, more than prediction, shapes survival in this illustration. For the related question of whether a strategy has an edge at all, rather than just a survivable size, see the companion article on [trading expectancy](/articles/what-is-trading-expectancy/).

## The First-90-Days Survival Checklist

These are survival rules, not a profit promise. No checklist item guarantees profit, and no named setup is presented here as profitable. Each item is one rule, plus one line of why, tied to the mechanism above.

Scope note: this checklist is for active, leveraged, or intraday trading of the kind the cited studies measure — not a prescription for long-term delivery investing.

1. **Write the plan before the first trade.** Rule: entry, exit, risk per trade, and the conditions under which you stop for the day — all written down before money is at stake. Why: without it, every move becomes an improvised, emotional decision.
2. **Risk 1–2% of capital per trade, as a guideline.** Rule: size every trade so that a loss costs no more than 1–2% of capital. Why: this is a practitioner guideline, not a guarantee, but the streak arithmetic shows why it matters — at 1% risk, a 10-loss streak costs under 10% of the account. Worked example: with ₹1,00,000 of capital, 1% risk is ₹1,000; if the stop-loss distance is ₹50 per share, the size is ₹1,000 ÷ ₹50 = 20 shares. That division — risk amount divided by stop distance — is how the percentage becomes an actual trade size.
3. **Use a stop loss as a live order, not a mental level.** Rule: place the stop when you place the trade. Why: a mental stop gets moved when fear or hope arrives; a live order executes the plan you made calmly.
4. **Set a daily loss limit — and honour it.** Rule: as a guideline, not data, set the daily loss limit at 3% of capital — three times a 1% per-trade risk — and stop trading when you hit it. Hitting the limit ends all trading for the day, with no re-entry. Why: it caps the damage from revenge trading and from the worst emotional days.
5. **Paper trade first.** Rule: practise on a simulator before trading live; 30+ days of paper trading is a practitioner suggestion, not a rule and not a regulator requirement. "Pass" means every trade in the period has a ticked plan checklist; any unticked trade breaks the count and the period restarts. Why: it lets you test whether you can follow the plan at all, without paying real costs for the lesson.
6. **Journal every trade.** Rule: record the setup, size, plan, exit, and reason for the exit — win or lose. One filled row looks like this: setup — opening-range breakout; size — 20 shares; plan — buy on breakout, stop ₹50 below entry; exit — stop-loss hit; reason for exit — the stop rule, not a change of mind. Review the journal weekly: if the plan was followed and the trade still lost, that points to a strategy error; if the plan was broken, that points to a discipline error. Why: without a journal, you cannot tell a strategy problem from a discipline problem, and you will repeat both.
7. **Do not scale up until a full month of rule-following is logged.** Rule: one full month of following the plan, not one month of profits, before increasing size — and "pass" again means every trade in that month has a ticked plan checklist, with any unticked trade breaking the count. Why: profits in a short window can be luck; a month of rule-following is evidence of process.
8. **No expiry-day lottery tickets without a view.** Rule: do not buy far out-of-the-money expiry contracts as a gamble, with no tested view behind them — that is, no written criteria checked over prior trades. Why: leveraged derivatives can lose capital quickly through expiry and time decay, and lottery sizing is oversized risk in its purest form.

Note on scope: the 90-90-90 rule is one rule of thumb among others — the 3-5-7 rule is a separate rule, not explained here — and this checklist deliberately stays with survival only.

## The Verdict: False as a Statistic, True as a Warning

The 90-90-90 rule is false as a statistic and true as a warning.

It is false because no study ever measured "90% lose 90% in 90 days." Its traceable root is a small 1999 NASAA sample in which about 70% of US day traders were found likely to lose everything they invested, with the Senate finding that sample too limited for broad conclusions — and the path from that 70% to the triple-nine rhyme is traced by researchers with uncertainty, not documented as fact.

It is true as a warning because the measured numbers are nearly as harsh: 93% of Indian individual F&O traders lost over FY22–FY24, about 91% lost in FY25, 74–89% of EU retail CFD accounts lost in the March 2018 analyses, and only 3.0% of Brazilian day traders persisting beyond 300 sessions came out ahead. Those are different markets, periods, and populations — and they all point the same way.

Your job is not to beat the 90%. It is to refuse the behaviours that create it: trading without a plan, sizing trades so large that a normal losing streak ends the account, exiting on emotion, expecting fast consistency, and overtrading into cost bleed.

If there is one decision that matters most, it is the size of your next trade. That decision is made before the trade, in calm, and it is the one decision the rhyme cannot make for you. Leveraged and derivatives trading can lose capital quickly, including through leverage, expiry and time decay, and accumulated costs — and no checklist, rule, or strategy in this article guarantees profit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is the 90-90-90 rule true?

No, not as a measured statistic — no published study produces the triple "90% lose 90% in 90 days." But the measured loss rates behind it are nearly as harsh, which is why it works as a warning rather than as a fact.

### What is the 90% rule in trading?

It is the folk saying that 90% of traders lose 90% of their money within the first 90 days — three separate claims about how many lose, how much they lose, and how fast. Each claim must be checked separately against the evidence, and none of them is a published measurement in that exact form.

### Why do 90% of traders lose money?

The repeatable causes are no written plan, oversized risk, emotional exits, unrealistic expectations, and cost bleed from overtrading — not intelligence, and not secret indicators. The data points at preparation, process, and risk control.

### How many traders actually lose money?

By market, period, and population: 89% of Indian individual equity F&O traders lost in FY22, 93% lost over FY22–FY24, and about 91% lost in FY25 (SEBI studies); 74–89% of EU retail CFD accounts typically lost money in the March 2018 analyses; in Brazil, the share of mini-Ibovespa futures day traders with a positive net profit fell from 29.8% at 1 day to 3.0% beyond 300 days (starters 2013–2015); and 7 in 10 Indian individual intraday equity-cash traders lost in FY23.

### What is the biggest mistake new traders make?

Oversized risk without a written plan — the combination that lets a normal losing streak end the account before any question about the strategy's edge is answered. The streak arithmetic above shows the mechanism: 10% risk per trade turns a 10-loss streak into a 65% drawdown, while 1% risk turns the same streak into under 10%.

### How long does it take to become a profitable trader?

No study measures a standard timeline. The 18–36 month, or 1–3 year, figures found in practitioner discussion are opinions, labelled as such, not data. The Brazil curve is a corrective: persistence alone, without an edge and tight risk control, did not produce profitability in that market.

### How do I stop losing money in trading?

Follow the survival checklist above: a written plan, the 1–2% risk guideline, a live stop loss, a daily loss limit, journaling, and no premature scaling up. That is loss control, not a profit guarantee — it is aimed at improving survival, it does not promise profits.

### Can beginners become profitable traders?

The data shows most do not, in the markets and periods studied, and no guarantee is offered here. The minority outcome in the evidence is tied to preparation, process, and risk control rather than intelligence or secret indicators — which is why the checklist focuses on behaviour you can control.

### Is trading just gambling?

One way to think about it — offered here as interpretive framing, not as a researched finding — is that gambling has a fixed negative edge that cannot be changed, whereas a trading edge is unproven until tested, so the high loss rates reflect untested trading, not a rigged game. Risk disclosure still applies: leveraged and derivatives trading can lose capital quickly, including through expiry and time decay and accumulated costs.
