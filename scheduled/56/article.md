*By Kaizen · 22 November 2026 · Monks Of Market*

If you have never traded an option, the subject can feel locked behind jargon. Strike prices, premiums, assignment, Greeks — the words arrive faster than the understanding. Michael Sincere's *Understanding Options* exists for exactly that reader. It is a plain-English primer that explains what an option is, what the four basic trades are, and how to approach them in a sensible order, without assuming you already know the language.

This summary does something most summaries of the book do not: it turns the book into a ladder you can actually climb. Each of the four basic trades is a rung, with its maximum loss stated before you step onto it. Then comes the part beginners worry about most — assignment — followed by the three pricing drivers, the Greeks tied to one carried example, and a reading-and-paper-trading plan that tells you when this book has done its job.

A note before we start: this article paraphrases the book's ideas in our own words throughout. No passage from the book is quoted here.

## Is Understanding Options a good first book for a complete beginner?

Our verdict — and it is a judgement based on reviewer consensus, not a proven fact — is yes, for the reader this book was written for: someone who has never traded an option and wants a first book that explains the subject from the ground up.

**Who it is for:**

- A complete beginner who wants to understand what an option actually is before risking any money.
- A reader who wants the four basic trades explained in a clear order, with the risks stated plainly.
- A more experienced trader who wants a refresher in plain language.

**Who it is not for:**

- An intermediate trader who already understands options and wants depth, advanced strategy analysis, or a complete trading system. Reviewers agree this book is too introductory for that reader.
- Anyone looking for a ready-made system to follow trade by trade. The book teaches the trades; it does not hand you a complete trading system.

That split is the honest frame for everything that follows. If you are starting from zero, this is a strong first rung. If you already know the basics, you will outgrow it quickly — and the later sections of this article explain what to read next when that happens.

### What the book is at a glance

Michael Sincere is a full-time columnist and author of several trading books, including *Understanding Stocks* and *All About Market Indicators*. His authority in this book, as reviewers describe it, is his plain-language teaching style rather than any trading record.

The edition this summary follows is the 2nd edition, published by McGraw-Hill on January 6, 2014, running to 320 pages (ISBN-13 978-0071817844). The 1st edition dates to 2006. According to the author and publisher, the book has sold over 100,000 copies — that is their own claim, not an independently audited sales figure.

The book is organised in six parts:

- **Part 1 — What You Need to Know First**
- **Part 2 — Selling Covered Calls**
- **Part 3 — How to Buy Calls**
- **Part 4 — How to Buy Puts**
- **Part 5 — Advanced Options Strategies**
- **Part 6 — Uncommon Advice**, including an interview with Sheldon Natenberg, described in the book as a bestselling author and recognised options expert

Parts 2 to 4 are organised the way you would actually trade, moving from beginner-level strategies to more advanced ones. The book itself is a ladder — and this article follows its order.

The 2nd edition adds new material on overcoming assignment anxiety, LEAPS, the Greeks, protective and married puts, the collar, credit and debit spreads, straddles, the strangle, iron condors, calendar spreads, butterfly spreads, Weeklys, and mini-options. The later chapters are previews of where the ladder leads; they are not the place to start.

## The one idea that reframes options: a right, not an obligation

An option gives its holder the right, not the obligation, to buy or sell something at a set price within a set period. That single sentence is the whole subject in miniature — and it is also why options confuse beginners. Most things you buy give you the thing itself. An option gives you a right, and rights have a price and an expiry date.

Sincere illustrates the idea with a property analogy, which we paraphrase here in our own words. Imagine you pay a small amount for the right to buy a house at an agreed price for, say, the next three months. If the house rises in value, your right is worth having — you can buy at the old, lower price. If the house falls in value, you simply walk away. You lose only the amount you paid for the right itself. That payment is the premium, and the agreed price is the strike price.

A call option works the same way for buying: it gives the buyer the right to buy an asset at a predetermined price. A put option gives the buyer the right to sell an asset at a predetermined price. In both cases, the buyer pays a premium for the right, and the right expires.

Now comes the part the analogy makes obvious, and the part beginners most often miss: **the right decays every day you hold it.** Each day that passes without the move you need is a day of value quietly leaving the option. Time decay is the silent tax every bought option pays. That is why the first trade must be small and defined-risk — you are paying for time, and time is always running out.

Hold that idea while you read the ladder below. Every rung is easier to understand once you see that you are either paying for a right or being paid to take on an obligation — and that the two sides of that exchange carry very different risks.

Climb the ladder in order. Do not skip to the top rung because it looks more profitable — the top rung is where the real downside lives.

### Rung 1: The covered call — income from stock you already own

A covered call means selling a call option on stock you already own, to generate income. You collect the premium. In exchange, if the stock rises above the strike price and the option is exercised, you sell your shares at the strike price and give up any further upside above it.

This is the gentlest first rung for a reason: you already own the shares, so you are not borrowing trouble you do not understand yet. The trade needs the stock to stay roughly flat or rise modestly. If the stock falls, the premium you collected softens the loss slightly, but it does not protect you — you still own a falling stock, and that is the real risk of this rung.

**Maximum loss:** there is no fixed, small maximum loss on this rung in the way there is for a bought option. Your loss is the fall in the stock you own, minus the premium you received. As a worked illustration: if you own a stock at ₹1,000, collect a ₹20 premium, and the stock falls by ₹100 to ₹900, your loss is ₹100 minus the ₹20 premium received — ₹80 per share.

**What beginners get wrong:** treating the premium as free income. It is not free. You are being paid to give up upside, and you keep the downside of owning the stock.

### Rung 2: Buying a call — paying for upside with a capped loss

Buying a call lets you profit from a rise in the underlying with a smaller outlay than buying the shares themselves. You pay the premium, and that premium is the most you can lose on the bought call itself. If the stock does not rise enough, or does not rise in time, the option can expire worthless — and you lose the whole premium.

This rung is where the book's central lesson bites hardest. The trade must be right on direction, timing, and magnitude — not direction alone. A stock that rises too little, or too late, still leaves you with a loss. A bought call can lose the entire premium you paid — the percentage swing can be large, but the rupee loss is capped at the premium.

**Maximum loss:** the premium paid for the bought call itself.

**What beginners get wrong:** buying a call because a stock "will go up" and forgetting the clock. Being right eventually is not the same as being right before expiry.

### Rung 3: Buying a put — paying for downside protection or a bearish view

Buying a put lets you profit from a fall in the underlying, or hedge a stock position against a drop. A protective put — also called a married put — protects a stock position against a fall, in the same way you might insure something valuable: you pay a premium, and if the worst happens, the put softens the blow.

The same max-loss discipline applies as on Rung 2. The premium paid is the most that can be lost on the bought put itself, but the put also needs the fall to be large enough and soon enough to be worth more than the premium you paid.

**Maximum loss:** the premium paid for the bought put itself.

**What beginners get wrong:** buying puts as a cheap way to bet against everything, and discovering that a market that drifts sideways — not down — quietly eats the premium through time decay.

### Rung 4: Selling a put — collecting premium with real downside exposure

Selling a put collects premium, but it carries real downside exposure, and this rung should only be attempted after the first three are understood. The seller may be obliged to buy the underlying at the strike price if the buyer exercises. What this rung needs you to be right about is the underlying staying above the strike: if it stays above the strike, the put expires worthless and you keep the premium; if it falls below the strike, you are on the hook to buy at the old, higher price.

The book covers both cash-secured puts, where the seller holds enough cash to buy the shares if assigned, and naked puts, where the seller does not. The per-share loss bound is the same in both cases, but the funding position is not: in the worked illustration below, a ₹1,000-strike put sold for a ₹20 premium has a maximum loss of ₹980 per share if the underlying falls to zero (₹1,000 minus the ₹20 premium received). With a cash-secured put, that cash is already set aside, so the worst case is a loss you planned for and can fund. With a naked put, no cash is set aside, so the same fall leaves you owing the full strike price at assignment without the money reserved to pay for it — the loss is the same size, but the cash shortfall is the added danger. Either way, this must never be presented as low-risk. Collecting premium feels like income; the obligation behind it is the real trade.

**Maximum loss:** ₹980 per share in the illustration above — the strike minus the premium received, if the underlying falls to zero.

**What beginners get wrong:** seeing only the premium coming in and not the obligation going out. The premium is the pay; the obligation to buy is the job.

## Assignment anxiety, handled honestly

The 2nd edition's most human chapter deals with assignment anxiety — the fear of being assigned on a short option. It deserves a plain explanation, because fear shrinks once you know what the word actually means.

Assignment is the seller's obligation. On a covered call, assignment means you are obliged to sell the stock you own at the strike price. That is it. It is a normal outcome of selling options, not an exceptional disaster. You sold someone the right to buy your shares at a set price; if they choose to use that right, the deal you agreed to simply happens. And assignment can happen before expiry, not only on the expiry date — a short option can be assigned at any point while it is live, so the worst case must be affordable from the day you enter, not only at expiry.

The anxiety comes from surprise, not from the event itself. The cure is procedure, not courage. Before entering any short option trade, run this max-loss-first checklist:

1. **Know the worst case before entry.** Write down, in plain words, what happens if you are assigned. On a covered call: you sell your shares at the strike. On a short put: you buy the shares at the strike. If you cannot write the worst case in one sentence, you do not understand the trade yet.
2. **Size so the worst case is affordable.** The position must be small enough that the worst case is a bad day, not a blown-up account. Position sizing is not a detail to sort out later; it is the decision that determines whether you survive your first twenty trades. See our guide to [position sizing](/articles/position-sizing/) and, if your account is small, [position sizing for a small account](/articles/position-sizing-for-small-accounts/).
3. **Have a written exit rule.** Decide before entry what you will do if the trade moves against you, and at what point. A rule written while you are calm is worth far more than a decision made while you are frightened. One concrete example for a short option trade: if the underlying falls to the strike price on a short put, close the trade and take the loss rather than waiting to be assigned — the trigger is written down before entry, so there is nothing to decide in the moment.

**After assignment actually happens:** on a covered call, the shares leave your account and the strike price arrives in their place — you have sold at the strike, as agreed, and the trade is over.

On a short put, the shares arrive in your account and the strike price leaves it — you now own the stock at the strike price, and you must decide what to do with a position you did not choose to buy at that moment: hold it as a stock position you are willing to own, or sell it and take the loss. Either way, assignment is not a penalty or a mistake to fix in a panic; it is the trade doing exactly what you agreed it would do, and the plan you wrote before entry tells you which of those two outcomes you accept.

With those steps done, assignment becomes boring — a known outcome of a known trade, handled by a plan you wrote in advance. That is the whole point: the fear does not disappear because you are brave. It disappears because you already know the worst case, you can afford it, and you know what you will do.

## Time, volatility, probability: what each pricing driver does to your first trade

Three drivers decide what an option is worth: time to expiration, volatility, and probability. This section shows all three acting on one carried trade, so you can see how each driver changes the same position.

**The carried example — simple illustrative numbers, not real market data.** Imagine a stock trading at ₹1,000. You buy a call option with a strike price of ₹1,000, paying a premium of ₹20 per share. At expiry, the option is only worth something if the stock is above ₹1,000 — and you only profit if it is above ₹1,020, your breakeven (the ₹1,000 strike plus the ₹20 premium you paid). Keep those three numbers — ₹1,000, ₹20, ₹1,020 — in mind through the three sections below. They are invented for illustration only, and they stay the same throughout so the drivers can be compared fairly.

One warning before the numbers: trading costs eat into a small premium. A ₹20 premium leaves little room — costs come out of the same small amount, so a first trade must clear its costs as well as its ₹1,020 breakeven before it makes anything at all. That is a qualitative warning, not a figure: the smaller the premium, the more the costs matter.

### Time decay: the option loses value even when the stock stands still

Time decay works against the buyer. In our carried example, suppose the stock stays exactly at ₹1,000. Ten days pass, and the same call that cost ₹20 is now worth only about ₹14 — these figures are invented illustrative values, not modelled prices. Nothing about the stock changed, but ten days of the right have been used up. Ten more days pass, still at ₹1,000, and it is worth about ₹7 — again, an invented illustrative value, not a modelled price. At expiry, with the stock still at ₹1,000, the option is worth nothing: the right to buy at ₹1,000 is worthless when you can buy at ₹1,000 anyway.

This decay path illustrates direction, not a prediction: it shows that a bought option loses value as time passes, not that any real option would follow these exact figures. Holding and waiting is itself a cost. A beginner who buys a call and waits patiently for the stock to move is paying for every day of that patience. This is why the book's ladder starts with the covered call, where time decay works for the seller, before asking you to buy options, where it works against you.

### Volatility: the premium can be expensive or cheap before direction matters

Volatility inflates or deflates option premiums. Higher volatility makes options more expensive; lower volatility makes them cheaper — before direction ever matters.

Take the same carried example. If the market suddenly expects bigger swings in the stock, that same ₹1,000-strike call might cost ₹32 instead of ₹20 — an invented illustrative value, not a modelled price, and no statement is made here about how large a volatility change would produce it. If the market goes quiet and expects almost no movement, the same call might cost only ₹12 — likewise an invented illustrative value, not a modelled price. Your view on the stock has not changed in either case. Only the price of the right has changed — and with it, your breakeven. At a ₹32 premium, the stock must clear ₹1,032 before you profit. At a ₹12 premium, it must clear only ₹1,012.

The lesson is practical: the entry price is part of the trade, not a detail. Buying an expensive option needs a bigger move to win than buying a cheap one, even with the identical view on the same stock.

### Probability: right on direction is not enough

An option trade must be right on direction and timing and magnitude. This trio is our article's own framing of the book's point: being right on direction but wrong on timing, or on how far the underlying moves, can still lose money.

Return to the carried example one last time. Suppose the stock does rise — your direction was right — but only to ₹1,010 by expiry. The option is worth ₹10 at expiry (the stock is ₹10 above the strike), but you paid ₹20 for it, so you still lose ₹10 per share. Right direction, wrong magnitude. Or suppose the stock rises to ₹1,030 — comfortably above your ₹1,020 breakeven — but only the week after your option expires. Right direction, right magnitude, wrong timing — and the option still expires worthless.

That is the discipline the whole book is built around. A view is not a trade. A trade is a view plus a deadline plus a size big enough to matter and small enough to survive being wrong.

### Choosing a strike, an expiry, and an underlying for a first bought option

The ladder tells you the order; it does not tell you how to pick the first contract. This short chooser is the article's own practical guidance for a first paper trade, not a claim about the book.

- **Strike:** start with a strike close to the current price — in the carried example, the ₹1,000 strike on a ₹1,000 stock. A strike far above the price needs a very large move to win; a strike far below it costs a much larger premium for the same view.
- **Expiry:** give the trade time. A first bought option should expire weeks away, not days away, so a small delay in the move does not end the trade — and so time decay does not eat the premium before the view has had a chance.
- **Premium:** keep it small enough that losing the whole premium is a lesson, not a blow. If the premium is so small that costs would eat most of it, the trade is too small to bother with; if losing it would hurt, it is too big.
- **Underlying:** pick a stock you already follow and understand, one whose price moves in steps you can make sense of, and paper-trade it on that one stock until the rung is passed. Avoid a stock you chose only because someone else tipped it, or one whose price jumps around in ways you cannot explain — a first paper trade should teach you about the option, not about a stock you do not know.

## The Greeks for beginners: the dials, tied to the carried example

The book has a chapter on delta and the other Greeks. The Greeks are simply dials that describe how an option's price responds to changes around it. Rather than defining them in the abstract, here is what each dial does to the carried example — a stock at ₹1,000, a call bought for a ₹20 premium — with invented illustrative numbers, not modelled prices, so you can see the dials move a trade you already understand.

- **Delta — the dial that matters most on Rungs 2 and 3.** As an illustration only, if delta were 0.50, a ₹10 rise in the stock would lift the ₹20 premium by about ₹5, to about ₹25. Delta is how closely your bought option follows the stock.
- **Theta — the dial that matters most on Rung 1, and the one a buyer must watch.** Theta is the time-decay dial from the section above: it is the daily fall in the premium while the stock stands still, which is why the ₹20 premium became about ₹14 and then about ₹7 in the illustration. On a covered call, time decay works for you as the seller; on a bought call or put, it works against you.
- **Gamma — how fast delta itself changes as the stock moves.** On Rung 2, gamma is what makes a bought call's sensitivity to the stock grow as the stock rises towards the strike — the dial moves faster the closer the trade gets to the money.
- **Vega — how much the premium moves when expected volatility changes.** This is the dial behind the volatility illustration above: the same call costing ₹32 when bigger swings are expected and ₹12 when the market goes quiet. It matters most on Rung 4, where a short put seller is exposed to a rise in expected volatility, and on any bought option held while the market's mood changes.
- **Rho — how much the premium moves when interest rates change.** For most beginners holding short-dated options, this is the least important dial — but it is worth knowing it exists.

Read the Greeks as a dashboard, not as a test. If you can say, in plain words, which dial pushes your trade which way on the rung you are on, you understand enough to start. The deeper mathematics can wait until the graduation signal in the plan below is met.

## Discipline: position sizing, maximum loss, and exit rules before entry

The book's most overlooked material is its section on trading psychology — discipline and managing emotions — which reviewers note is often missing from other beginner books. The framing in this article is our own discipline framework applied to the book's teaching: position sizing, maximum loss, and exit rules decided before entry.

Options can produce large percentage gains or losses on the premium, so a plan comes first. Before any trade, you should be able to answer three questions in writing:

1. **How much am I risking?** Your [position sizing](/articles/position-sizing/) decides this, not your confidence. One concrete rule: decide the maximum rupee loss first, then size the trade so it cannot exceed it. For a first paper trade, set that maximum loss at an amount you could lose in full without changing your month — for example ₹1,000 — and then choose the trade so the premium paid on a bought option, or the fall in the stock minus the premium on a covered call, stays within it. A trade you are certain about and a trade you are unsure about get sized by the same rule — the amount you can afford to lose — not by how strongly you feel.
2. **What is the maximum loss?** For a bought call or put, it is the premium paid. For a covered call, it is the fall in the stock minus the premium received. For a short put, it is tied to how far the stock can fall below the strike. If you cannot state the maximum loss, do not enter.
3. **When do I exit?** Decide the exit before entry — the point at which you take a loss, and the point at which you take a profit. A trade without an exit rule is a hope with a premium attached. Two concrete examples: on a bought call, exit if the premium halves — in the carried example, if the ₹20 premium falls to ₹10 — rather than holding it to zero in hope; on a covered call, accept assignment if the stock rises above the strike instead of buying the option back in a panic, because selling at the strike was the deal you agreed to.

Then comes the psychology: staying disciplined when a trade moves against you. The urge to hold a losing option "just a little longer" is time decay charging you rent while you decide. The urge to double a position after a loss is how a bad week becomes a blown-up account. If you have ever felt that pull, read our guides on [how to size positions after a losing streak](/articles/how-to-size-positions-after-a-losing-streak/) and the [risk of ruin](/articles/risk-of-ruin-trading/) — the mathematics of why survival comes before profit.

Discipline is the difference between the reader who survives their first twenty option trades and the one who blows up on the third. The strategies in this book are simple. Sticking to the plan when the trade moves against you is the hard part, and it is the part the book treats seriously.

## The advanced chapters, in one line each: where the ladder leads

Part 5 and the later chapters are a preview of where the ladder leads — not a second article, and not material to master before the first four rungs. Each topic below gets one line only, as the book's later chapters present them:

- **Protective and married puts:** buying a put to protect a stock position against a fall.
- **The collar:** owning the stock with a protective put and a sold call, trading away some upside for downside protection.
- **Credit and debit spreads:** buying and selling options together to create a protected position with reduced exposure.
- **Straddles and strangles:** strategies that profit from a large price move in either direction.
- **LEAPS:** long-dated options, for views that need far more time than a standard expiry allows.
- **Weeklys:** short-dated weekly options.

## How to actually use this book: the reading-and-paper-trading plan

### What are the four basic option trades?

The covered call, buying a call, buying a put, and selling a put — in that ladder order. The covered call sells a call on stock you own for income, with capped upside. Buying a call pays a premium for upside, with the maximum loss limited to the premium paid. Buying a put pays a premium for downside protection or a bearish view, with the maximum loss limited to the premium paid. Selling a put collects premium in exchange for the obligation to buy at the strike, with real downside exposure.

### What is assignment anxiety in options trading?

Assignment anxiety is the fear of being assigned on a short option — the worry that the buyer will exercise and you will have to honour your side of the deal. Assignment itself is the seller's normal obligation: on a covered call, it means selling the stock you own at the strike price, and on a short put, it means buying the shares at the strike price — and it can happen before expiry. It is made boring, rather than frightening, by the max-loss-first checklist: know the worst case before entry, size the position so the worst case is affordable, and have a written exit rule.
