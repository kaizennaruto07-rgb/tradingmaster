*By Kaizen · 4 November 2026 · Monks Of Market*

Most articles about day trader failure give you a single number. "Most fail." That number is useless, because it hides the only question that matters to a trader who is still in the game: **when** do traders leave, **what** kills them at that point, and **which stage are you at right now?**

This article answers that question with a funnel. Imagine 100 new day traders walking in on day one. Then watch the cohort thin — gate by gate — and learn what the survivors at each gate do differently from the ones who left there.

One warning before the numbers: this funnel is built from published attrition points, not from following one single group of 100 real people for five years. The individual points are measured; the smooth path between them is illustrative. Every figure below carries its population and period, because a number without those two labels is a slogan, not a statistic.

## The funnel in one table: 100 traders walk in

The attrition points come from research on day traders on the Taiwan Stock Exchange between 1992 and 2006 (Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean), the most complete day-trader survival study ever published. The study counts a trader as having quit when no day trading is observed for 12 consecutive months.

| Gate | Still standing (of 100) | Left at this gate | What kills them here |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Start | 100 | — | — |
| After month 1 | ~60 | ~40 | Expectations vs reality, no system, leverage blow-ups |
| After 2 years | ~20 | ~40 | Cost drag, strategy-switching, revenge trading |
| After 3 years | ~13 | ~7 | No feedback loop, slow bleed |
| After 5 years | ~7 | ~6 | Rule drift, boredom, life attrition |
| Predictably profitable | ~1–2 | — | — |

The gate-to-gate subtractions in the "Left at this gate" column (40, 40, 7, 6) are arithmetic on the published cumulative points — 100 → 60 → 20 → 13 → 7 — not separately measured stage-exit rates. No source measured "40 traders leave exactly between month 1 and year 2" as its own statistic; that figure is what the cumulative points imply.

Read the table twice. The first thing it tells you is how fast the thinning happens: most of the attrition is over by year 2. The second thing it tells you is how misleading survival alone is: of the 7 still standing at year 5, only about 1–2 are predictably profitable. The rest are still trading. That distinction — still standing versus predictably profitable — is the whole article.

## Gate 1 — Month 1: the expectations gate

**~40 of 100 leave here.** Nearly 40% of day traders in the Taiwan study day-traded for only one month.

This is not primarily a skill gate. Most of the traders who leave in month 1 never had a tested process to fail with. They arrived with an expectation — income, quickly — met their first real losses, and discovered that the gap between expectation and reality was wider than their account could absorb. Leverage turns a few bad trades into a blown small account, and with no system and no risk plan, there is nothing to fall back on when the first drawdown arrives.

**What the survivors do differently:**

- **A written system before live size.** The rules for entry, exit, and position size exist on paper before real money tests them. If you cannot state your system in a paragraph, you do not have one yet.
- **Fixed risk per trade.** The 1% risk-budget rule — risking roughly 1% of the account on any single trade — is the standard starting point discussed in the companion piece on trading rules of thumb (staged, not yet published). It does not make a bad system good; it makes a bad month survivable.
- **An expectation reset.** The first month's job is survival and data, not income. A trader who ends month 1 with a small account intact and a journal full of real trades has done the job. A trader who ends month 1 hunting for income has usually already blown up.

**Exit criterion for this gate:** if you cannot describe your system, your risk per trade, and your daily loss limit without looking them up, you are still at Gate 1 — whatever the calendar says.

## Gate 2 — Year 2: the cost and switching gate

**Only ~20 of 100 remain.** About 80% of all day traders in the study's lineage quit within the first two years.

Gate 2 is where the mathematics of trading does its quiet work. Costs — brokerage, taxes, and charges — take a slice of every trade, so a strategy must clear its cost hurdle before it makes a single rupee of profit. Across hundreds of trades, that drag compounds. So does recovery asymmetry: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain just to get back to breakeven, which is why a single bad stretch can end a year even when the win rate looks respectable. (The full recovery maths is covered in [the mathematics of drawdown recovery](/articles/trading-drawdown-mathematics-of-recovery/).)

The behavioural killers at this gate are just as systematic. Traders switch strategies before any sample is large enough to judge — ten trades tell you nothing, and neither do thirty. And after the first serious drawdown, many start overtrading and revenge trading: trading more, and bigger, to win back what was lost, which is how a normal drawdown becomes an account-ending one (see [overtrading](/articles/overtrading/) and [revenge trading](/articles/revenge-trading/)).

**What the survivors do differently:**

- **Cost accounting.** They know their per-trade friction and the breakeven win rate after costs. A strategy that looks profitable before costs and loses after them is not a strategy; it is a donation.
- **A fixed sample size before judging a strategy.** The sample is decided in advance and honoured. Switching mid-sample restarts the clock and guarantees that no strategy ever gets a fair trial — a pattern examined in detail in [why backtested strategies fail in live trading](/articles/why-backtested-strategies-fail-in-live-trading/).
- **A daily loss cap that ends the session.** When the cap is hit, trading stops for the day. This is a principle, not a gadget: the detailed circuit-breaker method is covered in a companion piece (staged, not yet published). The point is structural — the decision to stop is made before the losing day, not during it.

**The uncomfortable truth about Gate 2 survivors:** they are not yet profitable. They are just still standing. Surviving Gate 2 means you have stopped the bleeding; it does not mean you have found an edge. Confusing the two is how traders celebrate survival as if it were success, then size up and walk straight into Gate 3's trap.

## Gate 3 — Year 3: the no-feedback gate

**~13 of 100 remain.** Only about 13% of day traders in the Taiwan study continued day trading after three years.

Gate 3 kills differently from the first two gates. The traders who reach it have survived the early blow-ups. They have a system, more or less, and they have stopped switching every week. What they have not built is a feedback loop: no journal, no weekly review, no way to distinguish a strategy that is failing from a strategy going through a normal losing run. So they repeat the same behaviour for years. That is the slow bleed. Persistence without feedback is this gate's signature failure, and it is crueller than a blow-up because it feels like discipline. Showing up every day is not the same as improving.

**What the survivors do differently:**

- **A journal and a weekly review cadence.** The review judges process compliance — did you follow your rules? — not daily profit and loss. A week of rule-following losses is a different problem from a week of rule-breaking wins, and only a journal tells them apart. (See [what a trading journal should track beyond profit and loss](/articles/trading-journal-what-to-track-beyond-pnl/).)
- **One strategy, one sample, no switching mid-sample.** The sample-size discipline from Gate 2 becomes permanent here. The question is no longer "does this strategy work?" but "am I executing it well enough to know?"

**Exit criterion for this gate:** if you cannot point to a specific change you made to your trading in the last month because your review told you to, you do not have a feedback loop yet — you have a diary, or nothing at all.

## Gate 4 — Year 5: the boredom and drift gate

**~7 of 100 remain.** Only about 7% of day traders in the Taiwan study remained after five years.

Gate 4's killer is the strangest in the funnel: success at following rules, followed by abandoning them. Traders who can follow a system get bored of following it. They start making judgement calls where the system used to decide, sizing up on confidence or after a winning streak, and giving back quarters of gains in stretches that look, from the outside, like carelessness. They are not careless. They are bored — and boredom is the most underestimated risk in a multi-year trading career.

Gate 4 also takes traders for reasons that have nothing to do with skill. Capital needs, time pressure, family obligations — life attrition. Saying so honestly matters, because a funnel that attributes every exit to a trading mistake is a fable, not data. Some of the 6 who leave between year 3 and year 5 leave because life changed, not because trading beat them.

**What the survivors do differently:**

- **Rules treated as permanent, not training wheels.** The system is not something you graduate from once you are experienced; it is the thing experience taught you to keep.
- **Boredom accepted as the job.** A well-run trading system is mostly uneventful. Traders who need excitement from their trading will manufacture it, and manufacturing excitement is how accounts get given back.
- **Position size unchanged by confidence or by a winning streak.** Size follows the risk rule, not the mood. A winning streak changes nothing about the next trade's risk budget — and a trader who sizes up because they feel hot has already left the system, even if the account has not noticed yet.

## The profitable core: why surviving is not enough

Here is the funnel's punchline, and the reason this article exists. Of the 7 traders still standing at year 5, only about 1–2 are predictably profitable.

The Taiwan research lineage finds that less than 1% of day traders demonstrate predictably, reliably positive abnormal returns after fees — and roughly 1.6% are predictably profitable net of fees in an average year, yet that small cohort accounts for about 12% of all day-trading activity. Note the distinction the study itself insists on: about 13% of day traders earn profits net of fees in a typical year, but a single profitable year is not predictability. Predictability means doing it year after year, after costs, in a way that cannot be explained by luck. That is a far smaller group.

The activity share matters for a subtle reason: the profitable few trade far more than the average trader. That means the market, seen from inside — from chat rooms, social feeds, and leaderboard-style success stories — looks more winnable than it is. You are disproportionately likely to hear from, and about, the traders in the profitable core, because they generate a disproportionate share of the activity. The funnel's base rate is the antidote to that sampling bias: for every visible winner, there are roughly 98 other starters who are gone, still losing, or still unproven.

Risk disclosure, stated plainly: day trading can lose your entire trading capital — the [risk of ruin](/articles/risk-of-ruin-trading/) maths shows how quickly a small edge deficit compounds, leverage magnifies losses as well as gains, and nothing in this article is a promise that following the survivor behaviours will make you profitable. They are what the survivors did. They are not a guarantee.

## The Indian anchor: what SEBI's loss rates do — and do not — say

India's own numbers tell a loss-rate story, not an attrition story, and the two must never be spliced together.

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) found that about 93% of individual traders in equity futures and options lost money over FY22–FY24, with aggregate losses above ₹1.8 lakh crore and an average loss of roughly ₹2 lakh each. Its FY25 follow-up found about 91% of individual traders in equity derivatives made a net loss, with aggregate net losses of about ₹1,05,603 crore.

Those are loss rates — the share of traders who ended a period down — measured on a different population (Indian equity derivatives traders) over a different window. They are not attrition rates, they do not say how many traders quit after a month or a year, and they cannot be inserted into the Taiwan funnel as if they were the same measurement. Their honest use is as an anchor: in the world's most active retail derivatives market, the same harsh base rate shows up. The funnel above explains the timeline; SEBI's figures confirm the destination is no kinder in India.

One sentence of housekeeping: the catchy "90-90-90" rhyme sometimes quoted about traders is not a statistic and not a funnel stage. It is a piece of trading folklore, covered properly in its own companion piece (staged, not yet published), and it should never be quoted as data.

## Which gate are you at? A five-question self-diagnosis

Answer honestly. Each question lands you at a gate, with the single highest-leverage action for that gate.

1. **How long have you been day trading?** Less than a month, and still finding your feet: you are at Gate 1. Your action: write the system down before your next live trade.
2. **Are you still switching strategies?** If yes, you are at Gate 2 regardless of how long you have traded. Your action: pick one strategy and fix its sample size in advance — then honour it.
3. **Do you keep a journal and review it weekly?** If no, you are at Gate 3. Your action: start the journal this week and hold your first review on a fixed day, judging process compliance, not daily profit.
4. **Do you honour a daily loss cap every session?** If the cap is negotiable on bad days, you are back at Gate 2. Your action: make the cap mechanical — hit it, stop, no exceptions.
5. **Have you sized up on confidence or after a winning streak?** If yes, you are at Gate 4. Your action: return position size to the risk rule and treat any urge to size up as a signal to re-read your own rules, not to trade bigger.

If you answered "no" to more than one question, start with the earliest gate you failed. Gates are passed in order; you cannot fix Gate 4 drift while Gate 2 switching is still going on.

## The honest exit

At any gate, quitting day trading is a legitimate outcome, not a failure verdict. The funnel's numbers are not a moral judgement on the 93 who leave or stall — they are a description of a brutally selective activity. A trader who reads this funnel, recognises their gate, and decides that the expected value of their time is better spent elsewhere has made a rational decision, not a cowardly one.

The evidence-backed alternatives keep the same risk rules and change the time horizon: longer-horizon investing, or swing trading with the same fixed risk per trade, the same journal, and the same review cadence. What changes is the frequency — fewer decisions, less cost drag, less exposure to the intraday noise that makes Gate 2 so expensive. What does not change is the discipline. If the discipline was the problem, a longer horizon will not fix it; if the frequency was the problem, it might.

The funnel does not ask you to believe you will be the exception. It asks you to locate yourself honestly, fix the one thing your gate demands, and let the numbers — not hope — decide whether you keep going.

## Frequently asked questions

**What percentage of day traders quit?**
About 80% of all day traders quit within the first two years (Taiwan Stock Exchange, 1992–2006, Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean lineage), and only about 7% remain after five years. These are attrition rates for that population and period — not a universal "failure rate" for all traders everywhere.

**How many day traders quit in the first month?**
Nearly 40% of day traders in the Taiwan study day-traded for only one month. Month 1 is the expectations gate: unrealistic expectations meeting first real losses, with no system and no risk plan.

**How long do most day traders last?**
Most do not last two years. The funnel runs: ~60 of 100 after month 1, ~20 after two years, ~13 after three years, ~7 after five years (Taiwan, 1992–2006). Traders who trade heavily (20+ days) survive at much higher rates — a different population, not a contradiction.

**What percentage of day traders are profitable?**
About 13% earn profits net of fees in a typical year, but less than 1% demonstrate predictably, reliably positive returns after fees, and roughly 1.6% are predictably profitable net of fees in an average year (Taiwan research lineage). A single profitable year is not predictability.

**Why do day traders quit?**
At different gates, for different reasons: expectations and leverage blow-ups in month 1; cost drag, strategy-switching, and revenge trading by year 2; no feedback loop by year 3; rule drift, boredom, and life changes by year 5. There is no single reason — that is why a funnel, not a list, is the right shape for the answer.

**Can you survive as a day trader?**
Survival is possible — about 7 in 100 are still trading after five years in the Taiwan data — but survival is not profitability. Only about 1–2 of the original 100 are predictably profitable. The survivor behaviours in this article (written system, fixed risk, cost accounting, fixed samples, journal and review, unchanging position size) are what the survivors did; they are not a guarantee.

**How many years does it take to become a profitable day trader?**
The data does not give a "years to profitability" number, and any article that does is inventing one. What the data shows is a selection process: most traders leave within two years, and predictability — not a single good year — is rare even among five-year survivors. Judge yourself by process compliance over a fixed sample, not by a calendar target.
