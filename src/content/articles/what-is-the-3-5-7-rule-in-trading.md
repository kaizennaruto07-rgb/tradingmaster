The 3-5-7 rule caps your loss on any single trade at 3% of your account, your total risk across all open trades at 5%, and demands winners roughly 2.3 times your risk. That three-number leash is the difference between a bad week and a blown account.

Most beginners lose money not because their strategy is wrong, but because no number anywhere told them to stop. The 3-5-7 rule is that number, written before the trade, when you are still thinking clearly.

## What is the 3-5-7 rule in trading?

The 3-5-7 rule is a risk management framework built on three caps. Each number controls a different way an account dies.

| Number | Cap | What it controls |
|---|---|---|
| **3** | 3% of account equity | Maximum loss on any single trade, if the stop-loss is hit |
| **5** | 5% of account equity | Maximum total risk across all open trades combined |
| **7** | ~7% reward target | Minimum size of a winner, roughly 2.3 times the 3% risk |

Read the first line carefully: 3% is the amount you are willing to **lose**, not the amount you invest. On a ₹1,00,000 account, 3% means a ₹3,000 loss at the stop.

The position itself can be far larger — ₹75,000 in the example later in this article — because the stop distance, not the position value, sets the loss.

The rule answers three questions before every trade:

- How much can I lose on this one trade? → 3%.
- How much risk am I carrying across everything open? → 5%.
- How large must my winners be to make the losses worthwhile? → 7%.

It tells you how much to risk, never when to enter. It is a position-sizing and exposure framework, not a trading strategy.

## The honest truth: sources disagree on the 7

Here is something most articles about the 3-5-7 rule will not tell you: there is no official version. No regulator, broker, or exchange issues this rule.

It is a trader-created guideline that spread through retail trading education, and different sources define the numbers differently.

| Version | What 3, 5, and 7 mean | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| Exposure framework (used in this article) | 3% per trade, 5% total open risk, 7% reward target | Most recent retail-education sources |
| Monthly circuit breaker | 3% per trade, 5% portfolio risk, stop trading for the month at 7% monthly loss | Some stock-trading guides |
| Price-based exits | 3% stop below entry, 5% partial profit, 7% full target | Some swing-trading guides |
| Concentration tiers | 3% per trade, 5% per sector, 7% whole portfolio | Some portfolio-risk guides |
| Moving averages | 3, 5, and 7-period exponential moving averages | A separate, unrelated technical setup |

This article uses the exposure framework because it is the most widely cited version, and because all three of its numbers run on the same logic: budgeting account risk. The monthly circuit breaker is still a good idea — it simply is not what most sources mean by 3-5-7, so this article treats it as a companion rule, not as the definition.

If another article gives you a different version, neither of you is necessarily wrong. The rule's value was never in the exact digits.

It is in having a cap at all — a point this article returns to at the end.

## Why the 3-5-7 rule works: the survival math

Losing streaks are not a possibility in trading. They are a certainty.

Every trader, good or bad, eventually takes 10 losses in a row. The only question is what that streak costs.

| Risk per trade | Loss after 10 straight losses | Capital left | Gain needed to recover |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1% | 9.6% | 90.4% | 10.6% |
| **3%** | **26.3%** | **73.7%** | **35.7%** |
| 10% | 65.1% | 34.9% | 186.8% |

Read that table twice. At 3% risk, the worst normal streak in a trader's year costs about a quarter of the account, and a 35.7% gain restores it.

At 10% risk, the same streak takes nearly two-thirds of the account, and recovery needs a 186.8% gain — a mountain most traders never climb.

That is the entire argument for the rule. It does not make you profitable.

It keeps you alive long enough for profitability to matter.

The 7% target does the other half of the job. A winner of 7% against a risk of 3% is a reward-to-risk ratio of about 2.3 to 1.

At that ratio, the breakeven win rate is just 30%:

| Reward-to-risk | Win rate needed to break even |
|---|---|
| 1:1 | 50.0% |
| 1.5:1 | 40.0% |
| 2:1 | 33.3% |
| **2.33:1 (3-5-7)** | **30.0%** |

A trader who is right only one time in three still breaks even under 3-5-7, before costs. Costs — brokerage, STT, exchange charges, and GST on every trade — reduce net gains in practice, so judge the rule on net results, not gross ones.

## How to apply the 3-5-7 rule tomorrow morning

Follow these steps in order, before you place any trade. Do not reorder them — sizing comes from the stop, never the other way around.

**Step 1: Write down your account equity.** Use the actual balance available for trading, not money you hope to add. On a ₹1,00,000 account, your numbers are: 3% = ₹3,000 per trade, 5% = ₹5,000 total open risk.

**Step 2: Pick the trade, then pick the stop.** Find your entry and your stop-loss from your setup first — a level where the trade idea is proven wrong, not a random percentage. Suppose you buy at ₹250 with a stop at ₹240.

**Step 3: Compute your risk budget.** Multiply account equity by 3%. On ₹1,00,000, that is ₹3,000.

This is the maximum this trade is allowed to lose.

**Step 4: Size the position from the stop.** Divide the risk budget by the risk per share. Risk per share is entry minus stop: ₹250 − ₹240 = ₹10.

Shares = ₹3,000 ÷ ₹10 = 300 shares. Position value is 300 × ₹250 = ₹75,000.

Notice the position is 75% of the account, yet the risk is only 3% — because the stop, not the size, sets the loss.

**Step 5: Check the 5% cap before entering.** Add this trade's ₹3,000 to the risk of every open trade. If the total crosses ₹5,000, you do not take the trade at full size.

Either reduce it or skip it.

**Step 6: Set the target at roughly 2.3 times your risk.** Risk per share is ₹10, so the target sits about ₹23.30 above entry, at ₹273.30. If hit, the gain is 300 × ₹23.30 = ₹6,990 — about 7% of the account.

If your setup cannot honestly reach that target, the trade fails the rule. Skip it or re-plan it.

**Step 7: Log all three numbers before you click buy.** Entry, stop, target, share count, risk in rupees. A rule you do not write down is a suggestion, and suggestions evaporate the moment a trade goes red.

### The same math on a ₹50,000 account

The formula does not change for a smaller account. Only the rupee amounts do.

- Risk budget: 3% of ₹50,000 = ₹1,500.
- Setup: buy at ₹100, stop at ₹97. Risk per share = ₹3.
- Shares: ₹1,500 ÷ ₹3 = 500 shares. Position value = ₹50,000.
- Target: about 2.3 × ₹3 = ₹7 above entry, at ₹107. Gain if hit: 500 × ₹7 = ₹3,500, which is 7% of the account.
- Always round down to whole shares. A result of 333.33 shares becomes 333 shares, because rounding up would breach the risk budget. If the result is zero shares at a valid stop, the trade cannot be taken — skip it rather than widening the budget to force it.

Run your own numbers through the [position size calculator](/tools/position-size/) before trusting mental arithmetic on a live order.

## The 5% cap everyone ignores

Most articles explain the 3 and the 7, then wave past the 5. The 5 is the rule's teeth.

On a ₹1,00,000 account, total open risk may never exceed ₹5,000. One full 3% trade uses ₹3,000 of that budget.

That leaves just ₹2,000 for every other open trade combined. In practice, 3-5-7 lets you hold one full-size position and one smaller one — not five positions at 3% each.

That is deliberate. Five positions at 3% each is 15% of the account riding on one bad afternoon, which is exactly the blow-up the rule exists to prevent.

The 5% cap also forces a question beginners skip: are these positions correlated?

- Two stocks in the same sector reacting to the same news are effectively one bet, not two.
- Three positions all exposed to the same market move fail together.
- If one headline could plausibly hurt all your open trades at once, count their risk together against the 5% cap.

When in doubt, treat uncertain positions as correlated. Overestimating correlation costs you a trade.

Underestimating it costs you an account.

## 3-5-7 vs the 1% rule: which should a beginner use?

The 1% rule is the older, stricter cousin: risk no more than 1% of your account on any single trade. Here is how the two compare.

| | 1% rule | 3-5-7 rule |
|---|---|---|
| Risk per trade | 1% | 3% |
| Total open-risk cap | None built in | 5% |
| Reward target | None built in | ~7% (≈2.3:1) |
| Loss after 10 straight losses | 9.6% | 26.3% |
| Best for | Beginners, small accounts, learning phase | Traders with a tested edge and proven discipline |

The honest recommendation: start at 1%. The 1% rule's job is to make your education cheap — 10 straight losses cost under a tenth of the account while you learn.

Graduate to 3% only after months of consistent, rule-following trading, not after one good week.

What the 1% rule lacks is the rest of the system. A trader risking 1% per trade can still hold 12 positions at once, or take profits at 0.5:1 and slowly bleed.

The 3-5-7 rule's real contribution is not the 3 — it is the 5 and the 7 wrapped around it.

The ideal beginner setup borrows all three: 1% per trade, a 5% total cap, and a 2:1 or better target. That hybrid is stricter than either rule alone, and strictness is the point.

## The mistakes that break the 3-5-7 rule

**Mistake 1: Sizing by feel, then naming it 3-5-7.** Buying a round 100 shares and placing a stop "about 7% away" caps nothing. If the risk budget was never computed, the rule was never applied.

The formula — shares = risk budget ÷ risk per share — is the rule.

**Mistake 2: Widening the stop mid-trade.** Moving a stop further away to "give it room" increases the risk per share after the size is already fixed. The loss is no longer 3% of anything.

Set the stop from the setup, size from the stop, and leave both alone unless the plan itself says to trail.

**Mistake 3: Adding to a losing trade.** Averaging down adds fresh risk to a position already moving against you, and it blows straight through the 5% cap. A second entry is a second trade: it needs its own risk budget, and the cap usually has no room for it.

**Mistake 4: Ignoring correlation.** Five "different" trades in one sector, or five trades all long the same market direction, are one concentrated bet wearing a disguise. The 5% cap exists precisely for this case.

**Mistake 5: Treating the 7 as a promise.** A 7% target is an aspiration, not a payout. Markets do not owe you 2.3:1 because you wrote it down.

If a setup cannot realistically reach its target, the correct move is to skip the trade — not to shrink the target until the trade looks acceptable.

## The contrarian truth about the 3-5-7 rule

Here is the insight generic articles miss: the numbers 3, 5, and 7 are not magic. There is no mathematical law that makes 3% safer than 2% or 4% in any absolute sense.

A trader using a disciplined 2-4-6, or even a strict 1-3-5, with honest position sizing will outperform a trader using 3-5-7 as a slogan.

The rule's power is that it is a hard cap decided in advance. What destroys accounts is not the absence of the perfect percentage — it is the absence of any percentage, replaced in the moment by hope, frustration, or the urge to win it back.

A mediocre cap you obey beats a perfect framework you negotiate with mid-trade.

That is also why the rule alone is not enough. It must be paired with position sizing on every single trade, a written log, and a session stop for the days when you are trading emotionally rather than systematically.

The digits get you into the discipline. The discipline is what saves the account.

## Your 3-5-7 daily checklist

Copy this into your trading journal and run through it before the market opens:

- [ ] Today's account equity written down; 3% and 5% budgets computed in rupees.
- [ ] Maximum 3 planned setups for the day — no impulsive additions.
- [ ] Every trade has entry, stop, and target written down before entry.
- [ ] Share count computed as risk budget ÷ risk per share, rounded down.
- [ ] Total open risk checked against the 5% cap before each new entry.
- [ ] Target is at least 2 times the risk; otherwise the trade is skipped.
- [ ] Session stop set: stop trading after 3 consecutive losses, or at the daily loss limit, whichever comes first.
- [ ] No averaging down, no stop-widening, no revenge trades — all three are rule violations, not judgment calls.

## Walk through a bad day: how the caps work together

Numbers on a page are easy to agree with. A red day is where they get tested, so walk through one in advance.

Consider a trader with ₹1,00,000 who follows 3-5-7 plus the session stop. Trade one is a planned setup that stops out.

The loss is 3% of ₹1,00,000, or ₹3,000, leaving ₹97,000.

Trade two is another planned setup, sized at 3% of the new balance: 3% of ₹97,000 is ₹2,910. It also stops out.

The balance is now ₹94,090.

Trade three follows the same rule: 3% of ₹94,090 is ₹2,822.70. It stops out too.

The balance is ₹91,267.30.

Three consecutive losses, and the session stop now triggers. The day ends down ₹8,732.70 — about 8.7% of the starting account.

That hurts, and it is supposed to hurt enough to teach, not enough to kill.

Now compare the same day without the caps. The trader, frustrated after trade two, doubles size on trade three to win it back.

Trade three is a normal stop-out, but at double size it costs about ₹5,645 instead of ₹2,822.70. Then comes the revenge trade — a setup that was never in the plan — and it costs another ₹5,000.

The uncapped day ends down roughly ₹16,500, nearly double the capped day, and the trader is now trading on tilt with a damaged account. The difference was never the strategy.

Both days had the same first two losses.

This is why the session stop matters as much as the percentages. The 3% cap limits each loss, the 5% cap limits concurrent risk, and the 3-loss stop limits the day.

Together they convert a bad day from an account event into a journal entry.

Review the day the next morning, not the same evening. Ask three questions: did each trade follow the plan, did the stops come from the setups or from fear, and did any trade breach the 5% cap?

If the answers are honest, a capped losing day becomes the cheapest education trading offers.

The gap between the two days is ₹7,822.70. That is not a rounding error — it is the price of one undisciplined afternoon, and over a year of trading, that gap compounds into the difference between an account that survives and one that does not.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the 3-5-7 rule in trading?

The 3-5-7 rule is a risk management framework with three caps: risk at most 3% of your account on any single trade, never more than 5% of your account at risk across all open trades, and aim for winners of roughly 7% — about 2.3 times the 3% risk. It is an informal, trader-created guideline, not a rule issued by any regulator or exchange, and it governs position sizing and exposure rather than telling you when to enter a trade.

### What is the 3-trade rule?

The 3-trade rule is not a standardized or universally accepted rule, so the term needs unpacking. Its most common meaning is the 3-strike rule: stop trading for the session after three consecutive losses, because three straight losses usually signal either a market out of sync with your system or an emotional state that will make the next trade worse. Some day traders also use it as a cap of three trades per day to control overtrading. Either way, it is a separate guardrail from 3-5-7, though the two pair well together.

### How many trades should I take per day?

The 3-5-7 rule itself sets no trade count — it caps risk, not frequency. As a practical guardrail, beginners should plan a maximum of three quality setups per day and take only those, because overtrading is one of the fastest ways to convert a good strategy into losses. Stop the session after three consecutive losses or when you hit your daily loss limit, whichever comes first. Fewer, planned trades beat many impulsive ones.

### What is the 1% rule in trading?

The 1% rule means risking no more than 1% of your account on any single trade — so a ₹1,00,000 account risks at most ₹1,000 per trade. It is more conservative than the 3-5-7 rule's 3%: ten straight losses at 1% compound to about a 9.6% drawdown, versus about 26.3% at 3%. Beginners should start with the 1% rule and graduate to 3% only after months of consistent, rule-following trading, not after one good week.

### What is a good risk-to-reward ratio?

A good risk-to-reward ratio is 2:1 or better — you aim to make at least twice what you risk. The 3-5-7 rule implies about 2.33:1, since a 7% target against a 3% risk is 2.33 times the risk, and at that ratio you only need a 30% win rate to break even before costs. Ratios below 1:1 demand a win rate above 50% just to survive, which is a hard way to trade. If a setup cannot realistically reach at least 2:1, skipping it is usually the correct decision.

### Should I stop trading after three losses?

Yes — as a session guardrail, stop trading after three consecutive losses. Three straight losses mean either the market is against your system today or you are trading emotionally, and both are reasons to walk away rather than force a fourth trade. Note the distinction: three planned stop-outs within a valid strategy are normal statistics, while three emotional mistakes are a warning sign — but the action is the same either way, because the risk being managed is tilt, not bad luck. Review the trades the next day with a clear head.

### How can beginners manage trading risk?

Beginners manage risk with a short, non-negotiable routine: size every position from a fixed risk budget (start at 1% per trade), set the stop-loss before calculating size, keep total open risk under the 5% cap, set a daily loss limit and stop at it, log every trade, never average down into a losing position, and never take a revenge trade after a loss. Risk management is not one big decision — it is these small rules, applied on every trade without exception.

---

Three numbers will not make you a profitable trader. But they will decide whether you survive long enough to become one. Write your 3% and 5% budgets in rupees before tomorrow's open, size every trade from its stop, and let the 7% target filter out the trades that were never worth taking. The market rewards the trader who is still standing — and standing is a risk management achievement before it is anything else.
