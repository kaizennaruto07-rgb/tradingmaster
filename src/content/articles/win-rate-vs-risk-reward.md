*Educational content only — not financial advice. Trading involves risk of loss.*

Ask ten traders which matters more — winning often or winning big — and you'll get a fight, not an answer. The "win rate is king" camp will show you their 75% green screenshots. The "risk-reward is everything" camp will show you one monster 1:5 trade. Both are showing you half an equation and calling it the whole thing.

So here's the short answer, and we'll prove it line by line: **neither win rate nor risk-reward wins on its own. What makes you money is how the two combine.** A 90% win rate can bleed an account dry. A 30% win rate can build one. The difference is the other half of the pair.

Don't believe it yet? Take this hypothetical illustration: same risk, same 100 trades, same Rs 100 on the line every trade:

- **70% win rate at 1:1** risk-reward → **+Rs 4,000**
- **40% win rate at 1:3** risk-reward → **+Rs 6,000**
- **60% win rate at 1:0.5** risk-reward → **−Rs 1,000**

The trader who was "right" most often made Rs 4,000. The trader right least often made Rs 6,000. And a 60% win rate — the number most beginners would celebrate — *lost* money. This article unpacks exactly why, shows you what each number actually measures, and gives you a framework to evaluate your own trading from your journal.

---

## The short answer: neither one wins alone — what matters is how they combine

Every trade you take has two independent properties: **how often** you're right, and **how much** each win or loss is worth. Your final P&L is the product of both, not a prize for maximizing either one.

Think of it like a shop. Win rate is the number of customers walking in; risk-reward is the profit margin on each sale. A shop with a thousand customers buying at a loss goes bankrupt faster than a shop with a hundred customers buying at a healthy margin. Volume without margin is just expensive noise.

Traders resist this because the two numbers *feel* like they should trade off cleanly — "if I win less often, I just need bigger winners." Sometimes that's true. But it isn't automatic, and the math has strict thresholds. A 40% win rate needs at least a 1:1.5 ratio just to survive. A 60% win rate at 1:0.5 is already dead on arrival. The rest of this article gives you the tools to check any pairing you actually run — before costs, after costs, and in the mirror of your own psychology.

## What win rate actually measures — and what it hides

**Win rate = (winning trades ÷ total trades) × 100.** If you took 50 trades and 30 were green, your win rate is 60%. Simple, clean, and dangerously incomplete.

Here's what it hides:

1. **It says nothing about how much winners make versus losers lose.** A 70% win rate where each win makes Rs 50 and each loss loses Rs 200 is a bonfire, not a strategy.
2. **It says nothing about costs.** Brokerage, STT, GST, and slippage get deducted from the real money you keep, never from the headline percentage.
3. **It says nothing about how wins and losses cluster.** Ten losses in a row and ten wins in a row are identical in the win-rate column. They are not identical to your psychology, your drawdown, or your margin account. (The streak math coming up will sting a little.)

Traders overvalue being right because being right *feels* good. Every green tick is a small hit of validation; every red trade is a small verdict on your judgment. A high win rate strategy gives you that hit constantly, even while the account drains quietly in the background. Low win rate strategies do the opposite: they punish your ego on 60–70% of your days and pay you for it. Most people can't choose the second path — which is precisely why understanding it is an edge.

**Bottom line:** win rate measures frequency. Frequency without payoff size is a vanity metric.

## What risk-reward actually measures — and the dishonest ratio

**Risk-reward ratio = (Target − Entry) ÷ (Entry − Stop Loss)** for a long trade. If you buy a Nifty option at Rs 100, place your stop at Rs 90 and your target at Rs 120, you're risking Rs 10 to make Rs 20 — a 1:2 ratio. Standard, correct, and widely misunderstood.

Because here's the thing nobody tells beginners: **the ratio is geometry, not a forecast.** Drawing a 1:3 plan on a chart says absolutely nothing about whether price actually reaches that target. The geometry is a plan for *how you'll get paid if you're right*. It is not a claim about *how often you'll be right*. Every time you widen a target to get a prettier ratio, you're implicitly claiming you'll be right less often — whether you admit it or not.

Which brings us to the **dishonest ratio**: the targets pasted absurdly far away so the trade *looks* like a genius 1:4. We've all seen these chart posts — entry marked here, stop tucked tight underneath, and a target two resistance levels above that price has never touched in this expiry. The ratio is real arithmetic. The numerator is invented. A ratio you will never actually collect is decoration, not risk management.

Honest R:R has three requirements: the stop is where your idea is *wrong*, the target is where the idea has *actually been proven* before (a real level, not a round number with ambition), and both survive slippage and spread on your instrument. Anything else is a ratio that exists only on the screenshot.

**Bottom line:** risk-reward measures payoff structure. Payoff structure without a realistic probability of reaching the target is fantasy accounting.

## The same strategy, six outfits: 100 trades, Rs 100 risk, before costs

Now the proof. Same risk per trade (Rs 100), same 100 trades, no costs yet. Only the pairing changes. Net P&L = (wins × average win) − (losses × average loss):

| Win rate | R:R | Wins × avg win | Losses × avg loss | Net P&L |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 70% | 1:1 | 70 × Rs 100 = Rs 7,000 | 30 × Rs 100 = Rs 3,000 | **+Rs 4,000** |
| 40% | 1:3 | 40 × Rs 300 = Rs 12,000 | 60 × Rs 100 = Rs 6,000 | **+Rs 6,000** |
| 60% | 1:0.5 | 60 × Rs 50 = Rs 3,000 | 40 × Rs 100 = Rs 4,000 | **−Rs 1,000** |
| 90% | 1:0.3 | 90 × Rs 30 = Rs 2,700 | 10 × Rs 100 = Rs 1,000 | **+Rs 1,700** |
| 30% | 1:3 | 30 × Rs 300 = Rs 9,000 | 70 × Rs 100 = Rs 7,000 | **+Rs 2,000** |
| 50% | 1:1 | 50 × Rs 100 = Rs 5,000 | 50 × Rs 100 = Rs 5,000 | **Rs 0** |

Read that table twice. The 40% trader — wrong more often than not — makes the most money. The 60% trader — right most of the time — loses. The 90% trader, the one your group chat would crown as a genius, makes a thin Rs 1,700 on 100 trades, a margin so fragile that real-world costs would erase it entirely (more on that in the costs section).

These are illustrative figures, not anyone's actual results — the point is the structure, not the numbers. The structure says: **net outcome is the product of the pair, never either input alone.** There is no universal "good" win rate and no universal "good" ratio. There are only pairings that pay and pairings that don't.

## Yes, a 90% win rate can lose money — and a 30% win rate can win

Two composite, illustrative profiles — common archetypes in Indian markets — both realistic in structure:

**The 90% scalper** (illustrative composite, not a real person). He trades Bank Nifty options intraday, grabs Rs 20–30 premiums, books fast, feels like a machine. His wins are tiny; his stops, on the days the market gaps or a position goes against him, are full-sized. Nine Rs 30 wins barely cover two-and-a-half Rs 100 losses — and once brokerage, STT, and slippage join, they don't cover even that. His equity curve is a slow staircase down interrupted by confident screenshots. He doesn't have a strategy problem. He has a *pairing* problem: his ratio can't pay for his (rare but full-sized) losses.

**The 30% trend follower** (illustrative composite, not a real person). She loses most trades — small, planned, boring losses. Then one trend pays 3R and erases a week of red. Her equity curve looks like a saw blade that climbs. She has survived what the scalper hasn't: the boredom and self-doubt of losing most of the time.

And surviving is the operative word, because low-win-rate trading comes with a brutal companion: **losing streaks**. Assuming trade outcomes are independent with a fixed win rate (a simplification — real markets have regimes and correlations, so treat this as a planning figure, not a forecast), probability says that at a **30% win rate**, a 10-loss streak across 100 trades has roughly a **58% chance** of happening — better than even odds. (Computed by exact dynamic programming over all possible outcome sequences, cross-checked by Monte Carlo simulation, October 2026.)

Let that land: if you run a 30%-win-rate strategy, you are more likely than not to hit a 10-loss streak in any 100-trade stretch — and near-certain to face one across a trading career. Your position sizing, your capital, and your mental health all have to survive that before the math ever gets to pay you. Many traders pick a low-win-rate/high-R strategy on paper and abandon it at the bottom of exactly this streak — locking in the pain and missing the payoff. The pairing demands resilience as a skill, not just as a virtue. For the full probability treatment — how streak length scales with win rate, trade count, and what it means for survival math — see our guide to [risk of ruin](/articles/risk-of-ruin-trading/).

## The one-line breakeven check: flip the question around

Every ratio has a minimum win rate below which it cannot make money. The full breakeven table lives in our [expectancy guide](/articles/what-is-trading-expectancy/) — but for day-to-day decisions, the flipped form is handier: **minimum R:R = (1 ÷ win rate) − 1.** A 30% win rate needs at least 1:2.33 to break even (1 ÷ 0.30 − 1 = 2.33). Run your win rate through it, check your ratio clears it with margin for costs, and you're done. The deep derivation and edge cases are coming next in this series (Article #4, Break-Even Win Rate).

## Why traders obsess over win rate: the psychology of being right

If the math is this clear, why does every beginner ask "what's your win rate?" before anything else? Because the brain is not a spreadsheet.

**Losses hurt more than equivalent wins feel good.** A red trade isn't just minus Rs 100 — it's a small verdict: *you were wrong.* Stack ten of those (and remember, the 30%-win-rate trader is more likely than not to), and the pain compounds into something the math can't price. A high win rate strategy anesthetizes this: constant small wins keep the verdict away, even when the account is quietly bleeding.

**Consecutive losses feel like a pattern, even when they're probability.** After six red trades, the trader doesn't think "I'm inside the expected distribution." He thinks "something is broken" — and starts revenge trading to get it back, or overtrading to force a win that proves he's still good. Both impulses are the psychology of being right overriding the math of the pairing.

**"Feels good" beats "pays well" in every selection process.** Given a choice between a strategy that wins 65% of the time and slowly loses, and one that wins 35% and makes money, most traders pick the first — then spend a year wondering why the account shrinks. The comfortable strategy isn't chosen because it's profitable. It's chosen because it's *comfortable*.

None of this is a character flaw; it's wiring. The practical takeaway is to choose your pairing with your psychology, not against it. If you know you cannot sit through a 10-loss streak without changing everything, don't run a 30%-win-rate strategy — run a higher-win-rate pairing with an honest ratio and tight costs. The best pairing on paper that you abandon in month two is worth exactly zero.

## Three trader archetypes, three pairings

There is no universally correct pairing — only pairings that fit different temperaments, instruments, and cost structures. Three common ones in Indian markets:

**1. The scalper — high win rate, low R:R.**
Lives on Nifty/Bank Nifty options or liquid futures, in and out in minutes. Typical pairing: 65–80% win rate at 1:0.5 to 1:1. This profile **lives or dies on costs and execution** — brokerage, STT, and slippage are a large fraction of each small win, so the textbook breakeven numbers lie to him more than to anyone. One sloppy stop wipes out a morning. Needs: razor execution, low-cost broker, iron discipline on stops.

**2. The swing trader — mid win rate, balanced R:R.**
Holds equity or F&O positions for days to weeks, targeting 1:1.5 to 1:2 with a 40–55% win rate. The balanced pairing — neither half has to be heroic. Costs matter less per trade because targets are wider. Needs: patience through multi-day drawdowns, genuine levels for targets (not the dishonest ratio), and sizing that survives 5–6 loss streaks.

**3. The trend follower — low win rate, high R:R.**
Waits for the big move, accepts being wrong most of the time: 25–40% win rate at 1:2.5 to 1:4. Needs: deep enough capital and small enough size to survive the better-than-even 10-loss streaks, plus the psychological wiring to keep executing a strategy that loses on most days. This is the profile that most beginners romanticize and few can actually run.

Notice what's *not* on the list: a correct answer. The scalper isn't "worse" than the trend follower — his costs and temperament just demand a different pairing. The question was never "which number is better." It's "which pairing can *you* execute for 100 trades without breaking?"

## The single number that combines both: expectancy

Win rate and risk-reward are two halves; **expectancy** is the whole — the average profit or loss per trade in units of risk (R), which is exactly the two halves multiplied together. Positive means the pairing pays; zero or negative means stop and redesign. For the formula, worked examples, and realistic ranges, see our complete guide to [trading expectancy](/articles/what-is-trading-expectancy/).

## Costs and position sizing work on net numbers, not paper ratios

Everything so far was **before costs**. Reality charges admission: brokerage, STT, exchange charges, GST, stamp duty, and slippage come out of your actual money. Qualitatively, the effect is simple — **a fixed cost per round trip behaves like a small extra loss on every trade**, which pushes your true breakeven win rate above every textbook number. Costs hit the small-target profiles disproportionately: the scalper at 1:0.3 feels them first and hardest; the trend follower with wide targets feels them least. This is why sizing and strategy decisions must be made on **net R:R after costs**, never on the paper ratio you drew on the chart.

Here's what that degradation looks like as illustrative arithmetic (*not* an Indian-market cost figure — your actual round-trip cost depends on your broker, instrument, and size). Say you plan a 1:2 trade with Rs 100 risk and pay Rs 20 in round-trip costs: your net win is 200 − 20 = Rs 180, your net loss is 100 + 20 = Rs 120, and your realized ratio is 180 ÷ 120 = **1:1.5, not 1:2**. The breakeven win rate quietly climbs from 33.3% to 40%. Nothing about your strategy changed — the costs just moved the goalposts. Now run that same arithmetic on the scalper's Rs 30 wins and you'll see why his profile lives or dies on execution costs.

The Indian context makes this concrete. SEBI's F&O profitability study (released August 2026, covering FY26) found that individual traders' aggregate net losses were **Rs 91,685 crore**, with **87.7% of individual traders** ending the year as net loss-makers and the average net loss per trader at **Rs 1.17 lakh**. Losses skew young: **88.5% of traders under 30** lost money. And the cost line in that study is the quiet killer: transaction costs consumed **35% of gross losses among loss-making traders**, versus 21% of gross profits among the profitable. (Source: SEBI study "Profitability of Individual Traders in the Equity Derivatives Segment (FY25-FY26)", via sarkaritel, thehindubusinessline, and outlookmoney, August 2026.)

Two practical consequences:

1. **Compute your pairing on net numbers.** For options traders, our [options trading charges guide](/articles/options-trading-charges-india/) breaks down the Indian cost stack; futures traders should account for funding and fees similarly using the [cost calculators on our tools page](/tools/).
2. **Size your rupee risk to survive the streak math.** The 30%-win-rate trader who risks 5% per trade and hits the better-than-even 10-loss streak is down ~40% before the strategy ever gets to work (1 − 0.95^10 = 0.4013). Your per-trade rupee risk must be small enough that the expected worst streak is an annoyance, not an account-ender. The math of surviving drawdowns is its own subject — see our guide to [risk of ruin](/articles/risk-of-ruin-trading/).

## Risks and limitations: when the pairing breaks down

The win-rate/R:R framework is powerful, but it has four honest failure modes. Know them before you trust it.

**1. Funded and prop accounts can punish the "right" pairing.**
Some prop firms enforce consistency rules — for example, no single day contributing more than roughly 30–50% of total profit. A low-win-rate/high-R strategy *violates that cap by construction*: its profits arrive concentrated in a few big winners. The strategy can be genuinely profitable and still fail the account's rules, while a high-win-rate/small-payoff profile glides through. This is a documented industry argument (FXStreet, August 2026) rather than a universal rule — check your specific firm's terms — but it proves an important point: **there are real environments where the "risk-reward matters more" ranking flips.** Context decides, not dogma.

**2. All the streak math assumes independent trades. Real trading isn't.**
The 58% figure for a 10-loss streak assumes each trade is an independent coin flip with a fixed win rate — and it comes from exact computation, not the crude streak-probability approximation: the formula and online calculators built on it materially overstate the true probability, which is why the naive figure looks near-certain when the real one is 58%. Real trading violates this constantly: regime shifts (a trend strategy keeps losing in chop), correlated positions (three Nifty longs are one bet, not three), and your own behavior changing after losses. Treat streak probabilities as a *lower bound on the pain to prepare for*, not a precise forecast.

**3. Paper R:R is not realized R:R.**
Slippage, partial fills, and — the big one — **exiting early** degrade the actual pairing. The trader who plans 1:3 but books at 1:1.5 "to be safe" and lets stops run to full −1R is not running 1:3. He's running something closer to 1:1.2, and his breakeven requirement just moved. Your journal measures the *realized* pairing; the plan measures intentions.

**4. Backtested pairings are not guaranteed forward.**
A pairing that worked over the last 200 trades describes the past regime. Markets change, edges decay, and costs change (as the SEBI data shows, STT burdens move year to year). Re-verify periodically; never assume permanence.

And the standing disclosure for everything in this section: leveraged F&O can lose more than your margin, and SEBI's data shows roughly **9 in 10** Indian retail F&O traders lose money. No pairing in this article is a promise of profit — they're frameworks for measuring whether you have an edge, not for manufacturing one.

## Your decision framework: evaluate the pair in your own journal

Enough theory. Here's the practical close — how to judge your own trading:

**Step 1: Journal 30–50 real trades.** Not backtests, not paper trades — real ones, with entry, stop, target, exit price, and costs. Thirty is the minimum for the numbers to mean anything; fifty is better.

**Step 2: Compute your *realized* pair.** Win rate = winners ÷ total. Realized R:R = average win in rupees ÷ average loss in rupees. Use realized, after-cost numbers — not what you planned.

**Step 3: Check the pair against breakeven.** Run your win rate through the flipped check — **minimum R:R = (1 ÷ win rate) − 1** — and confirm your realized ratio clears it with margin for costs. Is your win rate above the breakeven line for your realized ratio? If yes, you have a candidate edge. If no, one of the two halves is broken. The full ratio-by-ratio breakeven table is in our [expectancy guide](/articles/what-is-trading-expectancy/).

**Step 4: Fix the weaker half first.** The decision rule:
- **Win rate high but net flat or negative?** Your ratio is too small — your winners can't pay for your losers. Widen targets to real levels, stop exiting early, or cut the strategy's costs.
- **Ratio big but net flat or negative?** Your win rate is below the streak-survivable level — you're either picking targets price never reaches (the dishonest ratio) or trading a style your psychology can't sustain. Bring targets to honest levels, or switch to a higher-win-rate pairing you can actually execute.

**Step 5: Re-check every 50 trades.** Pairings drift. Costs change. You change. The journal is the instrument panel — fly by it, not by how trading felt last week.

**Worked example — the framework in action.** A swing trader journals 40 real trades: 17 winners, 23 losers. Win rate = 17 ÷ 40 = **42.5%**. Average win Rs 150, average loss Rs 100 (after costs), so realized R:R = **1:1.5**.

Step 3: minimum R:R = (1 ÷ 0.425) − 1 = **1:1.35**. The realized 1:1.5 clears it — but barely. Expectancy per trade: (0.425 × 1.5R) − (0.575 × 1R) = 0.6375 − 0.575 = **+0.0625R**. Positive, but razor-thin — one bad month of slippage or a few early exits and it's negative. Per the Step 4 rule, this is a "win rate fine, ratio too small" case: the verdict is **fix the ratio half** — hold winners to honest target levels instead of booking early, or trim the cost stack — before thinking about sizing up. Re-check at 50 more trades (Step 5) to see if the fix stuck.

The question you started with — "which one actually makes me money?" — now has a working answer: **the pair, measured on your own realized numbers, with expectancy positive after costs, in a combination you can execute through its worst streak.** Everything else is commentary.

### Frequently asked questions

**Should I focus on win rate or risk-reward?**
Neither in isolation. Focus on their product — expectancy per trade. A high win rate with a tiny ratio can lose money (60% at 1:0.5 loses), and a big ratio with too low a win rate can't survive its losing streaks. Evaluate the pair together from your journal, and fix whichever half is weaker.

**Can you be profitable with a low win rate?**
Yes — if the ratio compensates. A 30% win rate at 1:3 nets +Rs 2,000 per 100 trades at Rs 100 risk (before costs). The catch is survival: at 30%, a 10-loss streak across 100 trades is ~58% likely (assuming independent outcomes), so your position sizing and psychology must withstand long dry spells. Low win rate strategies pay you for enduring discomfort most traders won't.

**What is a good risk-reward ratio for day trading?**
There is no universal number — start from the breakeven logic instead. Pick the ratio your setups honestly reach, then ask what win rate it demands: at 1:1 you need above 50%, at 1:1.5 above 40%, at 1:2 above 33.3% — all before costs, so real trading needs a margin above those lines (the full table is in our [expectancy guide](/articles/what-is-trading-expectancy/)). The honest answer is the pairing *you* can execute: intraday scalpers typically run 1:0.5 to 1:1 with high win rates, while intraday swing-style traders target 1:1.5 to 1:2. Whatever you choose, compute the ratio on net, after-cost numbers — costs hit small intraday targets hardest — and beware of pasting far-away targets just to claim a big ratio (the dishonest ratio).

**Why isn't a bigger risk-reward ratio automatically a better trade?**
Because the ratio is geometry, not a forecast — a 1:5 plan says nothing about whether price reaches the target. Widening the target lowers the probability of reaching it, which usually lowers your win rate by more than the ratio improves. A ratio only has value if the target is a level price realistically reaches *and* your realized win rate clears the breakeven threshold for it.
