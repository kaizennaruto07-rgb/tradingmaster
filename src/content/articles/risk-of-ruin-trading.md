## Risk of Ruin: The Probability Your Account Dies Before Your Edge Pays

Risk of ruin is the probability that your trading account hits zero — or a loss threshold you define as ruin — before a winning strategy has enough trades for its edge to pay off.

Here is the headline takeaway — the entire point of this article: **ruin is controlled by position size, not luck.** Two traders can run the exact same strategy, with the exact same win rate and the exact same entries, and one survives for years while the other blows up in a month. The difference is never the market. It is how much each of them risked per trade.

Risk of ruin turns that intuition into a number. It answers one question: *given my win rate, my average win versus my average loss, and the fraction of my account I risk on each trade — what are the odds this account eventually dies?* Once you can compute that number, every sizing decision stops being a feeling and becomes arithmetic.

### A simple analogy: the coin-flip gambler with a finite wallet

Imagine two gamblers flipping the same biased coin. It lands heads 55% of the time, and every correct call pays 1:1. Both gamblers have an edge — over enough flips, both should win money.

The first gambler bets 1% of his wallet on every flip. The second bets 25%.

The second gambler's wallet will, with near certainty, hit zero on an ordinary losing streak — and losing streaks are ordinary. Ten losses in a row at 45% loss probability is not freak bad luck; it is a scheduled event. At 25% per flip, a 10-loss streak leaves the wallet at roughly 5.6% of its starting size (0.75^10), deep in territory no recovery can fix. The first gambler, betting 1%, loses about 9.6% on the same streak and keeps playing.

Same coin. Same edge. Same streak. One wallet survives, one dies. That is risk of ruin: **the edge was never the problem — the bet size was.**

## The Math, Explained Gently: Win Rate, Reward-to-Risk, and Risk Per Trade

Risk of ruin is computed from three inputs. Label each one before you touch the formula:

- **W — win rate.** The fraction of your trades that win, as a decimal. 45% win rate means W = 0.45.
- **L — loss rate.** Simply 1 − W. At 45% wins, L = 0.55.
- **Rp — payoff ratio.** Your average win divided by your average loss. A strategy that wins ₹2,000 on average and loses ₹1,000 on average has Rp = 2 (a 2:1 reward-to-risk).
- **Risk fraction — the share of your account risked per trade.** Risking 2% per trade means a risk fraction of 0.02, and U = 1 / 0.02 = 50. Think of U as "how many consecutive losses it takes to lose the account" — the number of units your account is divided into.

The formula — the one variant used consistently throughout this article:

**A = (W × Rp − L) / (W × Rp + L)**

**Risk of Ruin = ((1 − A) / (1 + A))^U**

A is your edge compressed into a single number between −1 and 1. When A is positive, you have a mathematical edge; the bigger A, the stronger it is. When A is zero or negative, the strategy has no edge, and ruin is not a probability — it is a certainty (more on that in the next section).

One useful companion number: the **break-even win rate = 1 / (1 + R)**, where R is your reward-to-risk ratio. At 2:1, you break even winning just 1/(1+2) = 33.3% of trades. Anything above that, before costs, is edge.

Notice what is missing from the formula: entries, indicators, timeframes, markets. Risk of ruin does not care *how* you generate your edge. It only cares that the edge exists and how aggressively you bet on it.

### Expectancy comes first: negative expectancy cannot be sized away

Before computing ruin, compute expectancy:

**Expectancy = (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss)**

This is your expected profit per trade, in rupees. If it is positive, the strategy earns money per trade on average. If it is negative, the strategy loses money per trade on average — and **no position size on earth can fix that.** Look at the formula: with negative expectancy, the numerator of A (W × Rp − L) goes negative, and the whole ruin calculation collapses to 100%. Small bets do not save a losing strategy; they only make it die slower.

This is the order of operations most traders get backwards. They ask "how much should I risk?" before asking "does this make money per trade?" The formula is blunt: positive expectancy is the entry ticket. Position sizing decides whether you survive long enough to collect.

## Risk of Ruin vs Drawdown vs Going Broke in Real Life

Traders mix up three different things. Keep them separate:

- **Risk of ruin** is a *probability about the future*, computed before you trade. "Given my stats, I have a 13% chance of eventually losing this account." It is a forecast, and like all forecasts, it is only as good as its inputs.
- **Drawdown** is a *measurement of the past or present*: the peak-to-trough decline of your account, in percent. "I am down 20% from my high" is a drawdown. It is a fact, not a forecast — and a 20% drawdown does not mean your risk of ruin is 20%. The two numbers answer different questions.
- **Going broke in real life** is the *event itself*, and it is messier than any formula. Margin calls, forced liquidation by your broker, topping up the account mid-drawdown (which the formula does not model), quitting in frustration at −40% — real ruin includes psychology and plumbing the math never sees.

Risk of ruin is the cleanest of the three, which is exactly why you must remember what it leaves out. We will return to that in the limitations section.

### Why a 50% drawdown is almost ruin: the recovery asymmetry

Losses and gains are not symmetric. Losing 50% requires a 100% gain just to get back to where you started — and a 100% gain is a far rarer event than a 50% loss. The deeper the hole, the steeper the walls:

| Drawdown | Gain needed to recover |
|---|---|
| 10% | 11.1% |
| 20% | 25% |
| 30% | 42.9% |
| 50% | 100% |
| 90% | 900% |

The formula for the right column is drawdown ÷ (1 − drawdown). Read the last row twice: after a 90% drawdown, you need to multiply your remaining capital *ten times* just to break even. This is why professionals treat a 50% drawdown as near-death. It is not that recovery is impossible — it is that the mathematics of recovery has quietly turned against you, and every further loss makes it exponentially worse. Survival is not about avoiding all losses. It is about never letting a loss get big enough to trigger this asymmetry.

## How to Calculate Risk of Ruin: Three Worked Examples

We will run the same formula three times. Follow the arithmetic once and you can do it for your own trading forever.

### Example 1: Intraday NIFTY options trader (illustrative example)

*All inputs below are an illustrative example, not a real strategy's statistics.*

- W = 0.45 (45% win rate), L = 0.55
- Rp = 2 (2:1 reward-to-risk: average win twice the average loss)
- Risk per trade = 2% of the account → U = 1 / 0.02 = 50

Step 1 — compute A:

A = (0.45 × 2 − 0.55) / (0.45 × 2 + 0.55) = (0.90 − 0.55) / (0.90 + 0.55) = 0.35 / 1.45 ≈ **0.2414**

Step 2 — compute the base:

(1 − A) / (1 + A) = 0.7586 / 1.2414 ≈ **0.6111**

Step 3 — raise to U:

Risk of Ruin = 0.6111^50 ≈ 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ % — **effectively zero.**

A disciplined intraday options trader with a genuine 45% win rate and a 2:1 edge, risking 2% per trade, faces essentially no mathematical risk of ruin. The account will still suffer ugly losing streaks and drawdowns — ruin math says nothing about pain — but the account itself is not in danger. This is what correct sizing buys you.

### Example 2: Crypto futures trader (Delta Exchange context)

Same drill, different market. A crypto perps trader on an exchange like Delta Exchange, trading with leverage:

- W = 0.40, L = 0.60
- Rp = 2.5 (2.5:1 — wider targets are common in trending crypto moves)
- Risk per trade = 5% → U = 20

A = (0.40 × 2.5 − 0.60) / (0.40 × 2.5 + 0.60) = (1.00 − 0.60) / (1.00 + 0.60) = 0.40 / 1.60 = **0.25**

Base = 0.75 / 1.25 = **0.60**

Risk of Ruin = 0.60^20 ≈ **0.0037%**

At 5% risk per trade, this trader's ruin probability is 0.0037% — about 1 in 27,000. Tiny, but not zero. Now watch what happens when the same trader drops to 2% risk (U = 50): 0.60^50 is effectively zero. Same strategy, same market, same leverage — the extra 3% of risk per trade was buying essentially all of the ruin.

One crypto-specific note: leverage does not change the formula; it changes your risk fraction, because the fraction is computed on what the stop-loss costs you as a share of equity, not on notional size — which is exactly why leveraged traders must be stricter about sizing, not looser.

### Example 3: The caution case — same edge, destroyed by sizing

- W = 0.45, L = 0.55
- Rp = 1.3 (1.3:1 — a thinner edge, but still positive expectancy: 0.45 × 1.3 − 0.55 = +0.035 per unit risked)
- Risk per trade = 10% → U = 10

A = (0.45 × 1.3 − 0.55) / (0.45 × 1.3 + 0.55) = (0.585 − 0.55) / (0.585 + 0.55) = 0.035 / 1.135 ≈ **0.0308**

Base = 0.9692 / 1.0308 ≈ **0.9402**

Risk of Ruin = 0.9402^10 ≈ **54%**

Read that again: a strategy with a genuine positive edge has a **~54% chance of killing the account** — a coin flip on survival — purely because of 10% position sizing. Now run the *identical* strategy at 2% risk (U = 50): 0.9402^50 ≈ 4.6%. Same edge, same trader, same market. The only thing that changed was the bet size, and ruin fell from a coin flip to under 5%.

This is the whole article in one example. **Edge is necessary. Sizing is decisive.**

## Why Position Size Is the Whole Game: The Exponential Effect of Risk Per Trade

Take one fixed strategy — 55% win rate at 1:1 reward-to-risk (Rp = 1) — and change nothing but the risk per trade:

| Risk per trade | U | Risk of ruin |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | 10 | ~13.4% |
| 5% | 20 | ~1.8% |
| 2.5% | 40 | ~0.03% |
| 1% | 100 | ~0% |

(For this strategy, A = (0.55 − 0.45)/(0.55 + 0.45) = 0.10, so each row is 0.8182^U.)

Look at the shape of that table. It is not linear — it is exponential. Halving your risk per trade doesn't halve your ruin — it crushes it, and each successive halving crushes it harder: the first halving cuts ruin roughly sevenfold (13.4% to 1.8%), the next more than fiftyfold (1.8% to 0.03%), the next effectively to zero. Going from 10% risk to 1% — a tenfold reduction in size — collapses ruin by more than four orders of magnitude.

Once risk per trade drops into the low single digits, ruin does not get a little smaller — it falls off a cliff. The expensive direction is the other one: every extra percent of risk buys disproportionately more ruin.

### Losing streaks are guaranteed; ruin from them is optional

A 55% win-rate strategy loses 45% of its trades. Over a career of a few thousand trades, a run of 10 consecutive losses is not bad luck — it is statistically expected (about once every 5,000 trades). Every long-term trader will live through several. The question is never *whether* the streak comes. It is what the streak *costs*.

With fixed-percentage sizing, losses compound:

- At **1% risk per trade**, 10 consecutive losses cost 1 − 0.99^10 ≈ **9.6%** of the account. Painful. Survivable. You keep trading.
- At **10% risk per trade**, the same 10 losses cost 1 − 0.90^10 ≈ **65%** of the account. You are now in recovery-asymmetry hell, needing ~186% gains to break even — and you are expected to keep executing the strategy flawlessly while staring at that hole.

Same streak. Same strategy. The 1% trader experiences a bad month; the 10% trader experiences an event they may never recover from. **Losing streaks are guaranteed. Ruin from them is a choice you make when you set your size.**

### Set your own ruin line: the custom threshold method

So far we have defined ruin as losing the whole account — U = 1 ÷ risk fraction. But nothing forces you to use zero. If ruin *for you* means a 50% drawdown, the number of risk units changes: **U = ln(1 − threshold) ÷ ln(1 − risk fraction)**. One worked line: for Example 1's trader (base 0.6111, 2% risk) with ruin defined as a 50% drawdown, U = ln(0.5) ÷ ln(0.98) ≈ 34.3 risk units, so ruin = 0.6111^34.3 — a few millionths of a percent, effectively zero. The method matters more than the answer: pick the drawdown that would end *your* trading, plug it in, and let the formula tell you what your personal survival odds really are.

## How Trading Costs Quietly Raise Your Risk of Ruin

Every rupee of cost — brokerage, STT, exchange charges, GST, funding — comes out of your average win and gets added to your average loss. That shrinks Rp, which shrinks A, which raises ruin. Costs do not sit next to your edge; they eat it from the inside.

A concrete illustration for Indian options traders. Suppose a strategy averages a ₹650 win and a ₹500 loss (Rp = 1.3, positive expectancy). Our cost breakdown found that a single NIFTY lot round trip costs roughly ₹63 in total charges — paid whether you win or lose. Net of costs, the average win is really ₹587 and the average loss is really ₹563: Rp falls to about 1.04, and expectancy drops to 0.45 × 587 − 0.55 × 563 ≈ **−₹45 per trade**. A strategy that looked borderline-positive is now mathematically doomed — negative expectancy, certain eventual ruin — and the trader never changed a thing about their entries.

The key facts on Indian options costs, effective **1 April 2026** under Budget 2026: **STT on selling option premium is 0.15%**, and **STT on exercised in-the-money options is 0.15% of intrinsic value**. If you see a lower exercised-STT rate quoted anywhere, it is outdated. For the full rupee-by-rupee breakdown, read our [complete guide to options trading charges in India](/articles/options-trading-charges-in-india-the-complete-cost-breakdown/).

Crypto traders face the same mechanism through funding: on perpetual futures, funding is charged on your full notional position, every 8 hours, win or lose — a recurring drag on expectancy that compounds with holding time and leverage. Our [crypto futures funding guide](/articles/crypto-futures-funding-rate-explained-what-it-really-costs-t/) works through exactly what that costs.

The practical rule: **always compute expectancy and risk of ruin on net-of-cost numbers.** Gross edge is a fantasy; the market pays you in net.

## Kelly Criterion: The Academic Answer to "How Much Should I Risk?"

If risk of ruin tells you the odds of dying at a given size, the Kelly criterion attacks from the other direction: it computes the bet size that maximizes long-run logarithmic growth of capital. The formula:

**f\* = (b × p − q) / b**

- **b** — net payoff ratio: average win ÷ average loss (the same Rp as above)
- **p** — probability of winning (your win rate W)
- **q** — probability of losing (1 − p)
- **f\*** — the fraction of capital to risk

Example: p = 0.45, b = 2 → f\* = (2 × 0.45 − 0.55) / 2 = 0.35 / 2 = **17.5%** of capital per trade.

Before anyone risks 17.5% on anything: **full Kelly is notoriously aggressive.** It maximizes growth *on paper*, assuming your edge is known exactly and never changes — and it produces drawdowns most human traders cannot psychologically survive. This is why the practitioner convention is **fractional Kelly: betting one-quarter to one-half of the full Kelly fraction.** Half-Kelly on the example above is 8.75%; quarter-Kelly is about 4.4% — well above what most disciplined traders actually risk, which tells you how wide the gap is between academic optimality and tradable reality.

Kelly connects directly back to this article's machinery: full-Kelly sizing sits at the peak of the growth-vs-ruin curve, and sizing *above* it increases both drawdowns and long-run risk of ruin — more pain for less growth. That is why fractional Kelly and the 1% rule are the same instinct expressed two ways: both deliberately step back from the mathematical maximum to buy survival.

The standard textbook treatment follows Thorp's "The Kelly Criterion in Blackjack, Sports Betting, and the Stock Market" (2006, a chapter in the Handbook of Asset and Liability Management, revisiting his earlier work). The honest summary: Kelly is a ceiling for thinking, not a number to trade. It tells you the largest size that can be justified; wisdom lives well below it.

## Survival Rules From Traders Who Didn't Blow Up

Everything below is **practitioner convention** — habits passed down by traders who survived — not mathematical law. The math gives you probabilities; these rules are how people live inside them.

- **The 1% rule.** Risk no more than ~1% of your account on any single trade. As the table above showed, at 1% risk, ruin for a strategy with a solid edge rounds to zero — but thin edges are the exception: a 40% win rate at 1.55:1 still has positive expectancy yet carries ~3.8% ruin even at 1% risk. The 1% rule makes survival likely, not certain. It is a convention, not a theorem — but it is a convention with the exponential table behind it.
- **Daily loss limits.** Many traders stop for the day after losing ~3% of the account. The logic is not mathematical; it is psychological. Large losing days are when discipline breaks and revenge trading starts, and revenge trading has no win rate worth computing.
- **Know when to stop trading the strategy, not just the day.** A circuit breaker like "pause after a 15–20% drawdown and revalidate the edge before resuming" is a convention some traders use. The point: drawdowns beyond your plan are evidence your inputs (win rate, payoff) may no longer describe reality — and the formula is only as honest as its inputs.
- **Pick your acceptable ruin deliberately.** Some traders use **<1% risk of ruin as a personal threshold** — one trader's line in the sand, not a universal standard. There is no officially "correct" number. The right question is: *what probability of losing this account can you genuinely accept?* Compute your number, compare it to your threshold, and size down until the two agree.

## What the Formula Cannot Tell You: Honest Limitations

> **Read this before you trust any number in this article.**
>
> - **Stationary, known inputs are assumed.** The formula treats your win rate and payoff ratio as fixed, known constants. In reality you *estimate* them from past trades, and past performance does not guarantee future results. Garbage inputs, garbage probability.
> - **Trades are assumed independent and identical (iid).** Real trading violates this constantly: markets regime-shift, volatility clusters, your execution degrades after losses, and correlated positions can lose together. The formula sees none of that.
> - **No account top-ups are modeled.** Adding fresh capital mid-drawdown resets the math in ways the formula does not capture — which also means "I'll just add funds" is not a risk management plan, it is a confession that you don't have one.
> - **This is an unbounded-sequence probability — "ever", not "within N trades."** The formula answers "what are the odds of *eventual* ruin over an infinite sequence?" It does not tell you your odds of ruin within your next 100 trades. For that, you need Monte Carlo simulation — running thousands of simulated trade sequences and counting how many die. For a quick version in your own spreadsheet: generate a few hundred random trade sequences with RAND(), count how many hit your ruin line, and divide by the total — that fraction is your finite-horizon estimate.
> - **Fixed-percent compounding vs fixed-cash risk differ.** This article's U assumes fixed-*fractional* sizing (risk X% of current equity, so losses compound). If you risk fixed rupees per trade instead, the math changes — fixed-cash risk is harsher in drawdowns because each loss takes a bigger percentage bite as the account shrinks.
> - **Always sensitivity-check.** Re-run your numbers with your win rate knocked down 5–10 percentage points and your payoff ratio trimmed. If your survival depends on your most optimistic estimates being exactly right, you do not have a margin of safety — you have a hope.

## Make It Personal: Estimating Your Own Risk of Ruin

You need about fifty to a hundred of your own trades — live or paper — with honest records. Then:

1. **Compute your win rate (W)** and loss rate (L = 1 − W).
2. **Compute your average win and average loss, net of all costs** — brokerage, STT, funding, everything. Divide: Rp = average win ÷ average loss.
3. **Compute expectancy** = (W × average win) − (L × average loss). If it is not positive, stop here — you have a strategy problem, not a sizing problem.
4. **Compute A** = (W × Rp − L) / (W × Rp + L), and **U** = 1 ÷ your risk fraction per trade.
5. **Compute ((1 − A) / (1 + A))^U.** That is your risk of ruin.
6. **Sensitivity-check it:** knock 5–10 points off your win rate, trim Rp, and recompute. Done once, on Example 1's trader: with W knocked from 0.45 to 0.40 and Rp trimmed from 2 to 1.8, A = 0.0909, the base becomes 0.8333, and 0.8333^50 ≈ 0.011% — still effectively zero, so the strategy passes the pessimistic test. If the pessimistic number came back at 5%+, you'd size down until it clears your threshold.

50 trades gives a rough sketch, not a measurement — this is exactly why the pessimistic rerun is the real number you should trade on.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is risking 1% per trade enough?

For a strategy with a genuine, solid positive edge, 1% per trade drives the mathematical risk of ruin to effectively zero — the formula's U becomes 100, and the base raised to the 100th power vanishes. But the qualification matters: thin edges survive less comfortably. A 40% win rate at 1.55:1 still has positive expectancy (+0.02 per unit risked) yet carries ~3.8% ruin even at 1% risk. The 1% rule makes survival likely, not certain. The caveats: your expectancy must be real and net of costs, your inputs must describe the future reasonably well, and 1% does not protect you from long, painful drawdowns. It protects the account's *life*, not your comfort.

### What risk of ruin is acceptable?

There is no universal answer — only deliberate ones. Some traders adopt <1% as a personal threshold; others tolerate more for small speculative accounts. Make the threshold concrete: size until your *pessimistic-case* number — ruin recomputed with your win rate knocked down and payoff trimmed — clears 1%. The honest approach: compute your number, decide what probability of losing the account you can truly accept, and reduce size until the two agree. An uncomputed risk is not a low risk; it is an unknown one.

### Does leverage change my risk of ruin?

Leverage does not change the formula, but it changes the inputs you feed it. Leverage multiplies how much account equity each price move represents, which raises your *effective* risk fraction per trade. A "2% risk" trade at 10x leverage behaves like a far larger risk fraction if your stop is wide relative to margin. Compute ruin on what you actually stand to lose as a fraction of equity — not on the notional position size.

### Do trading costs change my risk of ruin?

Yes — directly. Brokerage, STT, exchange charges, GST, and funding all reduce your average win and inflate your average loss, which lowers your payoff ratio and your expectancy. A strategy with thin edge can flip from positive to negative expectancy once costs are counted, and negative expectancy means certain eventual ruin regardless of sizing. Always run the formula on net-of-cost numbers.

### What should I do if I'm already in a deep drawdown?

First, stop and measure: compute your current drawdown and the gain needed to recover (the asymmetry table above). Second, reduce size or pause — trading full size in a deep hole is how drawdowns become ruin. Third, revalidate: recompute your expectancy on recent trades, net of costs. If the edge is gone, the problem is the strategy, not your psychology. And remember the recovery math: the deeper the drawdown, the less room you have for further experiments. This is general education, not personal advice — if losses are affecting your wellbeing or finances, step away and seek appropriate professional help.
