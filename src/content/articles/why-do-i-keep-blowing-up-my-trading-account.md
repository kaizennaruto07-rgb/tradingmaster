*By Kaizen · 30 October 2026 · Monks Of Market*

If you have blown up a trading account more than once, you have probably already asked yourself whether your strategy is broken, whether you lack discipline, or whether trading is simply not for you. This article offers a different, checkable answer: in the repeat pattern described here, the strategy is not the part that repeats. The risk behaviour is.

A fresh deposit resets the money. It does not reset the position sizes, the stop-loss habits, or the urge to win a loss back in the same session. Until those change, the same sequence produces the same result — no matter how good the next strategy looks on paper.

## The short answer: you keep blowing up because the same risk behaviour repeats after every loss

Blowing up a trading account is rarely one bad trade in isolation. It is usually a repeatable sequence: an oversized position, a loss, an attempt to recover that loss quickly, a bigger position, a bigger loss, and then a fresh deposit that starts the sequence again.

That is why changing strategies does not fix it. A new strategy traded with the old risk rules will still be oversized after a loss, still be traded without a hard stop when it matters, and still be doubled when the trader wants the money back. The fix has to happen before the next deposit, not after the next loss — because once a session is underway and a loss is already on the screen, the rules are hardest to follow.

The rest of this article names that sequence, breaks it into five mechanisms you can recognise in your own trading, gives you a short self-diagnosis, and ends with five rules to fix before you fund again.

## What "blowing up" actually means

Blowing up a trading account means the account is no longer usable as a trading account: either it has been wiped out in a sudden forced exit, or it has been ground down to a point where normal position sizes are no longer possible and every trade feels like a gamble to get back to where you started. Both forms end in the same place, but they get there differently.

### The fast blow-up: one oversized trade, forced exit, or margin call

The fast blow-up is the version most traders picture: one position far too large for the account, a sharp move against it, and a forced exit or margin call before there is time to react. No broker-specific threshold is needed to understand it — if a single trade can lose a large share of the account, one bad move is enough.

The fast blow-up is dramatic, but it is not the repeat pattern this article addresses. It is usually the ending of a slower process, not the whole story.

### The slow bleed: death by a thousand small cuts

The slow bleed is quieter. The trader loses a little, then a little more, then sizes up slightly to make it back, then loses again. No single trade looks catastrophic. But the losses compound, confidence in the plan falls, and the trader eventually takes the oversized trade that finishes the account.

The repeat pattern this article addresses is the slow bleed that ends fast. That matters because it means there are warning signs — and decision points — long before the final trade. The cycle below is where those decision points get missed.

## The repeat cycle, step by step

Here is the account blow-up cycle as it typically plays out:

1. **A loss.** It may be a normal losing trade, or the first loss after a winning run. Either way, the account is smaller than it was.
2. **The urge to recover it.** Instead of accepting the loss as one trade among many, the trader treats it as money that must be won back — quickly.
3. **Bigger size, or skipped rules.** The next position is larger than the plan allows, the stop is left out or moved, or a losing trade is added to. Here, trading psychology is mechanical: it shows up in what the trader did next, not in any diagnosis of the trader's mind.
4. **A bigger loss.** Because the position was bigger, or the stop was absent, the next loss costs more than the first one did.
5. **Shame, then a fresh deposit.** The trader stops, feels the loss, and funds the account again — with the same rules, the same sizing habit, and the same urge to recover.
6. **Repeat.** The deposit resets the balance. It does not reset the behaviour. So the cycle runs again.

The break-point in this cycle is not after step 4, when the bigger loss has already happened. It is before step 2 of the next round — in the quiet period between accounts, when the risk rules can be written down, fixed, and enforced before any new money is at stake.

## The five mechanisms: a diagnosis, not a ranked list

The five mechanisms below are a diagnosis, not a ranking. They are presented in no order of severity or frequency, and no single one is claimed to be the cause for every trader. Most blown accounts involve more than one of them, but the self-diagnosis section that follows will help you pick the one to fix first.

Each mechanism follows the same shape: what the trader did, what the maths says, and the tell-tale sign to look for in your own record.

### 1. Oversizing: risking too much of the account on one trade

**What happened.** Position size was set by how confident the trade felt, or by how much money the trader wanted to make, rather than by a fixed percentage of the account. The risk on the trade — the amount lost if the stop is hit — was never actually calculated before entry.

**The maths.** All figures in this section use one clearly stated basis: each trade risks a fixed percentage of the then-current balance, so the balance compounds down as losses accumulate. The account starts at Rs 1,00,000 in every case, and the figures are illustrative arithmetic, not a prediction for any reader's account.

| Risk per trade | After 5 consecutive losses | After 10 consecutive losses |
|---|---|---|
| 1% of then-current balance | Rs 95,099 (4.9% drawdown) | Rs 90,438.21 (9.56% drawdown) |
| 10% of then-current balance | Rs 59,049 (40.95% drawdown) | Rs 34,867.84 (65.13% drawdown) |
| 20% of then-current balance | Rs 32,768 (67.23% drawdown) | Rs 10,737.42 (89.26% drawdown) |

At 1% risk, five consecutive losses leave the trader down 4.9% and still thinking clearly. At 10% risk, the same five losses take 40.95% of the account. At 20% risk, just three consecutive losses leave Rs 51,200 — a 48.8% drawdown. Three bad trades, and half the account is gone. That is the "it happened so fast" mechanism, quantified: it was not one unusually terrible trade, it was an unusually large risk repeated a few times.

Getting back from a 65.13% drawdown would require a ~186% gain on what remains — the full asymmetry maths is covered in [Trading Drawdown: The Mathematics of Recovery](/articles/trading-drawdown-mathematics-of-recovery/).

**Tell-tale sign.** A few trades decide the whole week. If removing your two largest losing trades would turn a bad week into a good one, your size — not your strategy — is deciding your results.

### 2. Leverage-as-risk confusion: the leverage number is not your risk

**What happened.** The trader judged risk by the leverage number on the position, rather than by how much of the account would be lost if the stop — or liquidation — were hit. High leverage was treated as dangerous by definition, and low leverage as safe by definition. Neither is true on its own.

**The maths.** Risk per trade is the amount lost if the stop is hit, and position size is derived from it: position size = (account capital × risk %) ÷ stop-loss distance. That formula is the whole distinction. A large notional position with a tight stop can risk less than a small position with a wide or absent stop. A trader can be overleveraged at low leverage by risking a large share of the account on one position, and conservatively sized at high leverage by risking about 1%.

Worked pair, on the same Rs 1,00,000 basis. Trade A risks 1%: (Rs 1,00,000 × 1%) ÷ Rs 2 stop distance = 500 units, so the rupee amount at risk is 500 × Rs 2 = Rs 1,000 (1% of the account) — even though, at a Rs 500 entry price, the notional position is 500 × Rs 500 = Rs 2,50,000, or 2.5 times the account. Trade B risks 10%: (Rs 1,00,000 × 10%) ÷ Rs 50 stop distance = 200 units, so the rupee amount at risk is 200 × Rs 50 = Rs 10,000 (10% of the account) — even though, at a Rs 100 entry price, the notional position is only 200 × Rs 100 = Rs 20,000, or 0.2 times the account. The position with more than twelve times the notional value risks one-tenth as much: the leverage number alone does not define risk; the rupee amount at risk does.

Overleveraging, in other words, is not a leverage setting. It is a risk-per-trade setting. If you cannot state, before entry, the rupee amount you will lose if your stop is hit, you do not yet know your risk — whatever the leverage number says. The live [position-size tool](/tools/position-size/) exists for exactly this calculation: size from your stop, not from your feeling about the trade. The method behind it is set out in [Position Sizing](/articles/position-sizing/).

**Tell-tale sign.** You know your leverage but you cannot say, without checking, what percentage of your account your last trade risked.

### 3. Stop-loss abandonment: mental stops, moving stops, averaging down

**What happened.** The stop existed only in the trader's head, or it was moved further away when price approached it, or the trader added to a losing position to lower the average entry. In each case, the trade that started with a capped risk ended with an uncapped one.

**The maths.** Without a hard stop, the 1%, 10%, and 20% arithmetic above no longer applies at all — because there is no capped risk percentage to compound. A trade with no enforced exit can lose far more than the planned risk, which is how a slow bleed turns into a fast blow-up in a single session. Stop-loss discipline is not about being right about where price will go; it is about ensuring that being wrong costs a known, survivable amount.

**Tell-tale sign.** You have trades you cannot sleep with open, or stops you have moved mid-trade. If a stop has ever been moved to "give it room" after the trade was already losing, this mechanism is in your record.

### 4. Revenge trading and overtrading: trying to win the loss back in the same session

**What happened.** After a loss, the trader re-entered the market — often immediately, often larger — with the explicit aim of recovering that loss before the session ended. The trade was not taken because the setup met the plan; it was taken because the previous trade had lost.

**The maths — one rung only, on a clearly labelled basis.** The worked example above compounds a risk percentage of the then-current balance. This rung uses a different, labelled basis: doubling position size. After a 10% loss on a Rs 1,00,000 account, the balance is Rs 90,000. Doubling the position size to "win it back" means the next loss, at the same stop distance, costs Rs 20,000 — that is 20% of the original account, and 22.22% of the reduced balance. One rung is enough to see the mechanism: doubling size after a loss does not double the chance of recovery, it doubles the cost of the next loss.

Overtrading is the same mechanism spread across a session: more trades, taken faster, with less filtering, each one justified as the trade that will undo the earlier ones. The session ends not when the plan says to stop, but when the account forces a stop.

**Tell-tale sign.** You traded immediately after a loss with the explicit aim of recovering it. If your trade log shows entries that only exist because the previous trade lost, this is your mechanism.

### 5. Overconfidence drift after wins: size creeps up without a decision

**What happened.** After a winning streak, position size crept up — from 1% to 2% to 3% and beyond — without any conscious decision to change the risk rule. Vigilance dropped at the same time: stops were checked less carefully, entries were taken faster, and the plan was followed more loosely.

**The maths.** This mechanism needs no new arithmetic. It is the oversizing maths, entered from the other side. When size has drifted up, the losing trade that follows is not unusually bad — the position is unusually large. A normal losing streak, of the kind covered in [How Losing Streaks Affect Trading Accounts](/articles/how-losing-streaks-affect-trading-accounts/), does damage proportional to the size it meets. At drifted-up size, ordinary losses produce blow-up-sized drawdowns.

**Tell-tale sign.** Your current risk percentage is higher than the risk percentage you started the winning streak with, and you cannot say when it changed. If the size grew but no decision was ever written down, it drifted — it was not chosen.

## Self-diagnosis: which mechanism is yours?

Do not try to fix all five mechanisms at once. Look at your last blown account — or your last 10 trades, if the account survived — and answer these five questions honestly, one per mechanism.

If your stops were mental, moved, or never recorded, you can still reconstruct risk per trade from your past record. For each trade, take the entry price, the stop price if one existed or the actual worst exit price if it did not, and the position size, then multiply the price distance by the position size to get the rupee amount at risk. Use the actual exit, not the intended stop, whenever the stop was moved — the moved stop is what the trade really risked.

1. **What was the biggest percentage of the account you risked on any single trade?** If it was 5% or more, start with oversizing.
2. **Could you state, before entry, the rupee amount your last trade would lose if its stop were hit?** If not, start with mechanism 2 (leverage-as-risk confusion).
3. **Did you move a stop, use a mental stop, or add to a loser?** If yes, start with stop-loss abandonment.
4. **Did you trade after a loss specifically to recover that loss?** If yes, start with revenge trading and overtrading.
5. **Did your size grow after a winning streak without a written decision?** If yes, start with overconfidence drift.

If you cannot answer question 1 or question 2 because you never calculated risk per trade before entry, that itself is the answer: start with mechanism 2 (leverage-as-risk confusion), and learn to calculate size from your stop. If more than one answer is yes, pick the one that appears earliest in your last blow-up — the mechanism that started the sequence — and fix that one first. A single fixed mechanism breaks the cycle more reliably than five half-fixed ones.

## The fix: five non-negotiable rules before the next deposit

These are editorial rules for this article — practical defaults to adopt before funding again, not a proven optimum and not a guarantee. Each rule gets one line on how to enforce it, because a rule you cannot enforce in the moment is not a rule.

### Rule 1: Fix your risk % outside the session — 1% by default, 0.5% after 3 consecutive losses

The range most professional sources recommend is 0.5–2% per trade, stated here as a widely repeated practitioner guideline, not as a measured optimum. This article's default is 1% per trade, dropping to 0.5% after 3 consecutive losses, until the streak ends.

**How to enforce it:** write the percentage down before the session starts, and calculate every position size from it. The percentage is never decided mid-trade, and never raised because a trade feels certain.

### Rule 2: A hard stop on every trade, placed with the order

No mental stops. The stop is placed at the same time as the entry order, at a level decided before entry, and it is not moved to give a losing trade room.

**How to enforce it:** size is calculated from the stop before entry, using the [position-size tool](/tools/position-size/). If you cannot place the stop, you do not place the trade.

### Rule 3: A daily loss cap — your personal stop for the session

Decide, before the session, the maximum you are willing to lose in one day, and stop trading when you hit it. No funded-account figures are imported here: this is your own ceiling, chosen in advance, in rupees or as a percentage of your account — whichever you will actually obey. As a labelled illustrative starting point only — example, choose your own: a personal daily cap of 2% of the account, which is Rs 2,000 on a Rs 1,00,000 account.

**How to enforce it:** write the cap next to your risk percentage before the session. When the cap is hit, the session is over, even if a "perfect" setup appears afterwards.

### Rule 4: Never add to a loser

A losing trade gets smaller, never bigger. Adding to a loser converts a capped loss into an oversized position, and it reopens every mechanism above in a single decision.

**How to enforce it:** if you are tempted to add, close the trade instead and review it after the session. Averaging down is not a separate strategy; it is oversizing with a delay.

### Rule 5: No recovery-motivated re-entry in the same session

This rule bans only one thing: re-entering the market in the same session because you want to recover a loss. A trade taken because the plan calls for it is not banned, even if it comes later in the same session — planned trades later in the same session are NOT banned; only recovery-motivated re-entries are. After a recovery-motivated trade, or once the daily cap is hit, the session ends.

**How to enforce it:** after a recovery-motivated trade, or once the daily loss cap is hit, step away. The next session starts with a clean plan, not with a debt to the previous one.

## What NOT to do next

Three moves feel like fixes but repeat the cycle:

- **Do not fund a bigger account to "absorb" the same behaviour.** A bigger account with the same risk percentage and the same habits loses more rupees, not fewer. Size absorbs nothing; rules do.
- **Do not switch strategies.** The strategy was rarely the cause. A new strategy traded with oversized positions, moved stops, and revenge re-entries will blow up in exactly the same sequence, just with different entry signals.
- **Do not buy a course or signal service as the fix.** No purchased system changes what size you trade or whether you move your stop. Those are your decisions, made before entry, and they are fixed by the five rules above — not by a new set of signals.

## When to stop and reset

Do not fund the next account until you can do two things: state your risk rule in one sentence, and show your last 20 trades obeying it. If you cannot do both, you are not ready to size up — you are ready to practise the rules at small size, or on paper, until the record proves the behaviour has changed.

This article stops here deliberately: its subject is fixing the rules before the next deposit. What comes after — the rebuild itself — belongs to a separate article, After the Blow-Up: A Recovery Protocol, mentioned here in plain text only. No rebuild timeline or re-entry protocol is set out here, because the point of this article is the step before it: break the cycle first, then talk about rebuilding.

## Risk and limitations of this diagnosis

These rules reduce the chance of repeating the same sequence. They cannot eliminate trading losses, guarantee survival, or turn a losing approach into a profitable one. All worked figures in this article are illustrative arithmetic on a Rs 1,00,000 account, not a prediction for any reader's account. Trading costs add to the drag on top of losses. Psychology in this article is described mechanically — in terms of what the trader did — not as a diagnosis of any individual's mind. Nothing here is a stock pick, a trade call, or a claim that a particular setup works.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does it mean to blow up a trading account?

It means the account can no longer function as a trading account: either it has been wiped out by a sudden forced exit or margin call (the fast blow-up), or it has been ground down by repeated losses until normal position sizes are no longer possible (the slow bleed). The repeat pattern this article addresses is the slow bleed that ends fast.

### Why do I keep blowing up my trading account?

Because the risk behaviour repeats after every loss while only the money is reset. The cycle runs loss, urge to recover, bigger size or skipped rules, bigger loss, fresh deposit, repeat. A new deposit with the same sizing habits, stop habits, and revenge re-entries produces the same result. Breaking the cycle means changing the risk rules before the next deposit, not after the next loss.

### Is it my strategy or my risk management that causes blow-ups?

In repeat blow-ups, it is usually the risk management — the risk per trade, the stop discipline, and the response to losses — rather than the strategy. Switching strategies does not fix oversizing, moved stops, or revenge trading, because those behaviours travel with the trader to the new strategy. Diagnose the behaviour first, using the checklist above, before concluding the strategy is at fault.

### How much should I risk per trade so I don't blow up?

No risk percentage guarantees you will not blow up, but this article's editorial rule is 1% of the account per trade by default, dropping to 0.5% after 3 consecutive losses. The range most professional sources recommend is 0.5–2% per trade, stated as a practitioner guideline, not a measured optimum. The key is not just the number: it must be fixed outside the session, and every position size must be calculated from it and from the stop before entry.

### Does leverage cause account blow-ups?

Leverage alone does not define your risk. What matters is how much of the account is lost if the stop — or liquidation — is hit. A trader can be overleveraged at low leverage by risking a large share of the account on one position, and conservatively sized at high leverage by risking about 1%. Judge every trade by its risk per trade, calculated from its stop, not by its leverage number.

### Why do I revenge trade after a loss, and how do I stop?

Revenge trading happens when a loss is treated as money that must be won back immediately, so the next trade is taken to recover it rather than because the plan called for it. It is usually taken larger and faster than planned, which is why the next loss costs more. The remedy is structural, not willpower: Rule 5 (no recovery-motivated re-entry in the same session — planned trades later in the same session are not banned) plus Rule 3 (a daily loss cap decided before the session). After a recovery-motivated trade, or once the daily cap is hit, the session ends.

### How many losing trades in a row does it take to blow up an account?

There is no universal number — it depends entirely on risk per trade. On a Rs 1,00,000 account, risking 1% of the then-current balance each trade, 5 consecutive losses produce only a 4.9% drawdown. At 10% risk, 10 consecutive losses produce a 65.13% drawdown. At 20% risk, just 3 consecutive losses produce a 48.8% drawdown. That is why the same losing streak destroys one account and barely dents another: the streak is the same, the size is different. For the consecutive-loss context behind these figures, see [How Losing Streaks Affect Trading Accounts](/articles/how-losing-streaks-affect-trading-accounts/).

### What are the warning signs I'm about to blow up my account?

Risking more than 5% of the account on a single trade; trades you cannot sleep with open; a few trades deciding the whole week; stops moved mid-trade; size that has grown after a winning streak without a written decision; and any trade taken immediately after a loss to recover it. Any one of these is a reason to stop, check the checklist above, and fix the mechanism it points to before trading again.

### Can I recover after blowing up my trading account?

Recovery, at the level this article covers, starts with fixing the rules before the next deposit: a fixed risk percentage, a hard stop on every trade, a daily loss cap, no adding to losers, and no recovery-motivated re-entries. The five rules above are the recovery starting point. The fuller rebuild that follows is covered separately in After the Blow-Up: A Recovery Protocol, mentioned here in plain text only.

### Should I stop trading after blowing up, or fund a new account?

Do not fund a new account until the risk rules are fixed and demonstrable. Funding first repeats the cycle: the same behaviour with fresh money produces the same result. Stop, and start with the self-diagnosis on your last blown account — it points to the one mechanism to fix first. Pausing is not quitting — it is the break-point in the cycle.
