## The Short Answer: Yes, Some Traders Make Money — Most Don't, and Income Is Nothing Like a Salary

Yes, you can make money from trading. Some traders do it consistently, year after year, and treat it as a business.

But here is the number that should sit next to that yes: in India, SEBI's study found that over 91% of individual traders in equity futures and options lost money in FY2024-25, with aggregate net losses of about ₹1,05,603 crore. A separate SEBI study found 93% lost money across FY2022 to FY2024.

So the honest answer has three parts, and this article covers all three with numbers:

- Making money from trading is possible, not typical.
- Whether you make it is decided by two numbers — expectancy and risk of ruin — not by how good your predictions are.
- Trading income arrives in lumps, with losing months in between. It never behaves like a salary.

## What SEBI's Data Actually Says About Making Money From Trading

Before strategies, indicators, or "best setups," look at the base rate. The base rate is the percentage of people like you who already tried and what happened to them.

### The F&O numbers are brutal

- In FY2024-25, over 91% of individual equity F&O traders in India lost money after costs.
- Their combined net losses were about ₹1,05,603 crore, up roughly 41% from ₹74,812 crore the year before.
- Across FY2022 to FY2024, 93% of individual F&O traders lost money, with total losses above ₹1.8 lakh crore.

These are not survey guesses. SEBI's studies cover broker-level records of millions of individual traders, which makes them the most reliable picture available of what actually happens to retail traders in India.

### The cash-market picture is better, but not kind

SEBI also found that 7 out of 10 individual intraday traders in the equity cash segment made losses in FY2023. Intraday cash trading is less destructive than leveraged F&O, yet the majority still loses.

The pattern is consistent across segments: the shorter the holding period and the higher the leverage, the worse the average outcome.

### What this means for you

The base rate does not say you will lose. It says losing is the default outcome, and beating it requires something specific: a tested edge, strict position sizing, and enough capital to survive the learning period.

Anyone who tells you trading income is easy is asking you to ignore the largest dataset ever collected on the subject.

## Why "Can You Make Money From Trading" Is the Wrong Question

Most beginners ask whether trading can pay. The better question is whether you can survive long enough to find out.

Consider a hypothetical trader, Rohan, who starts with ₹1,00,000 and risks 10% of his account on every trade. Five straight losses — an ordinary event for any strategy — leave him with about ₹59,000. That is a 41% drawdown from five trades.

Now consider the same trader risking 1% per trade. Ten straight losses leave him with about ₹90,400, a drawdown under 10%. Same strategy, same losing streak, completely different outcome.

The difference is not skill. It is survival math, and it is the single most underrated factor in trading income.

### The two numbers that decide everything

- **Expectancy:** how much each trade makes or loses you on average, over many trades.
- **Risk of ruin:** the probability that a losing streak wipes out your account before your edge pays off.

A trader with positive expectancy but reckless sizing still goes broke. A trader with modest expectancy and disciplined sizing can compound slowly for years. If you want to check your own numbers, the [risk of ruin calculator](/tools/risk-of-ruin/) on this site runs the same math, and the [risk of ruin guide](/articles/risk-of-ruin-trading/) explains it in full.

## The Only Math That Decides Whether You Make Money: Expectancy

Forget win rate for a moment. Win rate alone tells you almost nothing, and this is where most beginners get fooled.

### The formula, in one line

**Expectancy = (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss).**

Measure wins and losses in R, where 1R is the amount you risked on the trade. If you risk ₹1,000 and win ₹2,000, that win is 2R. If you lose ₹1,000, that loss is 1R.

### Two traders, opposite win rates, opposite results

Consider a trader who wins 70% of the time but wins only 0.5R while losing 1R. Her expectancy is (0.70 × 0.5) − (0.30 × 1) = 0.35 − 0.30 = +0.05R per trade. Barely positive, despite a 70% win rate.

Now consider a trader who wins just 30% of the time but wins 3R while losing 1R. His expectancy is (0.30 × 3) − (0.70 × 1) = 0.90 − 0.70 = +0.20R per trade. Four times better, with less than half the win rate.

This is the contrarian truth generic articles skip: a high win rate can still bleed you dry, and a low win rate can pay you well. What matters is the combination of win rate and payoff, never either number alone.

### Compute your own expectancy in four steps

- Step 1: Export your last 30 to 50 completed trades from your broker statement into a spreadsheet.
- Step 2: For each trade, record the profit or loss divided by the amount you risked. That gives you its R value.
- Step 3: Count your winners and losers to get your win rate, and average the R values of wins and losses separately.
- Step 4: Apply the formula. If the result is positive across at least 30 trades, you have evidence of an edge. If it is negative, no indicator or "better setup" fixes it — the strategy itself must change.

Do not trust an expectancy computed from fewer than 30 trades. Small samples lie, and a lucky week can make a losing strategy look brilliant.

### A worked example with rupees

Suppose your records show 100 trades: 45 winners averaging ₹2,000 and 55 losers averaging ₹1,000. Your expectancy is (0.45 × ₹2,000) − (0.55 × ₹1,000) = ₹900 − ₹550 = ₹350 per trade.

At 20 trades a month, that edge is worth about ₹7,000 a month before costs — and costs are not trivial, as the section on mistakes below shows. At 100 trades a month, the same edge is worth about ₹35,000 before costs.

Notice what this example proves: income follows from expectancy multiplied by frequency, minus costs. There is no step in that chain where motivation, confidence, or screen time appears.

## Position Sizing: The Rule That Keeps You Alive Long Enough to Profit

Expectancy decides whether you should make money. Position sizing decides whether you are still in the game when it happens.

### The 1% rule, stated plainly

Risk no more than 1% of your account on any single trade. With a ₹2,00,000 account, that means a maximum loss of ₹2,000 per trade. With a ₹50,000 account, it means ₹500.

This rule feels slow, and that is the point. It converts a normal losing streak from an account-ending event into an annoyance.

### What different risk levels actually cost you

The table below shows what a losing streak does to ₹1,00,000 at different risk levels. The math assumes you reduce position size as the account shrinks, which is what disciplined traders do.

- Risking 1% per trade: 10 straight losses leave about ₹90,400. You are down under 10% and still trading normally.
- Risking 2% per trade: 10 straight losses leave about ₹81,700. Painful, but recoverable.
- Risking 5% per trade: 10 straight losses leave about ₹59,900. Recovery now needs a 67% gain just to break even.
- Risking 10% per trade: 5 straight losses leave about ₹59,000. Recovery needs a 69% gain.

Recovery math is cruel and asymmetric: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain to break even, and a 40% loss needs a 67% gain. Every extra percent of risk makes the hole deeper and the climb steeper.

### How to size a position in practice

- Step 1: Decide your risk amount. Account size × 1%. On ₹2,00,000, that is ₹2,000.
- Step 2: Decide your stop-loss distance before you enter. If your stop is ₹20 below your entry price, your risk per share is ₹20.
- Step 3: Divide risk amount by risk per share. ₹2,000 ÷ ₹20 = 100 shares. That is your position size.
- Step 4: If the calculation gives you a size you cannot afford, skip the trade. Never widen the stop to fit a bigger position.

If you want this done automatically, the position-size tool on this site applies the same steps, including costs. The principle stays the same either way: the stop decides the size, never the other way round.

## What Monthly Trading Income Really Looks Like

Trading income is not a salary with a different name. A salary arrives every month whether the market cooperates or not. Trading income does not.

### Income follows capital, not effort

A trader with a genuine edge earning an illustrative 2% a month makes very different money depending on capital:

- ₹1,00,000 capital at 2% a month = about ₹2,000 a month.
- ₹5,00,000 capital at 2% a month = about ₹10,000 a month.
- ₹5,00,000 capital at 3% a month = about ₹15,000 a month.
- ₹10,00,000 capital at 2% a month = about ₹20,000 a month.

These are arithmetic illustrations, not promises. A 2% monthly return is not guaranteed, and many skilled traders have flat or losing months.

The uncomfortable implication: with small capital, even good trading pays little. A ₹50,000 account earning 3% a month produces about ₹1,500 — real money, but not rent money. Anyone promising a living from a tiny account is selling you a story, not a plan.

### The lumpiness nobody warns you about

Consider a hypothetical trader with a genuine edge averaging 2% a month over a year. Her actual months might look like this: +5%, −3%, +1%, −2%, +8%, 0%, +4%, −4%, +6%, −1%, +3%, +7%.

The yearly total is positive and respectable. But four of those twelve months are negative, and one is flat. A trader who needs every month to pay bills cannot survive that pattern — and the pressure to "make the month" is exactly what pushes traders into oversized, desperate trades.

This is why experienced traders keep living expenses separate from trading capital. Trading income funds goals over quarters and years, not groceries next week.

## Can Beginners Make Money From Trading?

Beginners can make money from individual trades from day one. Making money consistently is a different achievement, and it takes much longer than most courses admit.

### A realistic timeline, not a motivational one

- Months 1–3: Learn the mechanics. Place trades in a practice account, learn order types, and read your broker statements until you understand every charge line. Expect no income. The goal is to stop making unforced errors.
- Months 4–6: Trade tiny real capital — an amount whose complete loss would not change your life. Journal every trade. Your only target is a clean record of 30 to 50 trades with rules you actually followed.
- Months 7–12: Compute your expectancy from that journal. If it is positive, scale size slowly. If it is negative, fix the strategy before adding capital. Most beginners who survive this long are roughly break-even, and that is a good result.
- Year 2 and beyond: Only now does "income" become a sensible word. Traders who reach consistent profitability usually do so after a year or more of deliberate practice, and many never get there.

Anyone selling a shortcut around this timeline is selling the timeline itself as the product.

### The beginner's edge is small size, not big predictions

A beginner's advantage is that small size makes mistakes cheap. A ₹500 loss teaches the same lesson as a ₹50,000 loss, but only one of them ends the journey.

Trade the smallest size your broker allows until your journal proves you can follow rules under real-money pressure. Paper trading teaches mechanics; only real money, in small doses, teaches emotional control.

## Trading vs a Regular Job: An Honest Comparison

Trading and a job solve different problems, and comparing them honestly helps you decide what trading is for.

### What a job gives you that trading does not

- A job pays a fixed amount on a fixed date, including in bad months.
- A job usually includes benefits — paid leave, health cover, retirement contributions — that trading income must fund separately.
- A job's worst month is rarely a month where you pay your employer for the privilege of working.

Trading gives you none of these. Every rupee of benefits, every tax payment, and every losing month comes out of the same account.

### What trading gives you that a job does not

- No ceiling tied to a salary band. A trader with a large account and a real edge can earn multiples of a salary.
- Full control over hours, location, and strategy.
- Skills that compound: risk management, probability thinking, and emotional discipline transfer to business and investing.

The honest summary: a job is income first and growth second. Trading is growth first and income eventually, maybe. Treat trading as a replacement for your job only when your trading record — not your hope — covers your expenses for at least six consecutive months, with living expenses saved separately.

### When to keep the job

Keep the job if any of these are true: you need monthly income to survive, you have less than a year of trading records, your expectancy is unproven or negative, or your trading capital is money you cannot afford to lose. None of these are failures. They are the conditions under which trading is most likely to destroy wealth instead of building it.

## Five Mistakes That Turn Winning Traders Into Losers

Most losing traders do not have terrible strategies. They have good strategies ruined by five repeatable mistakes.

### 1. Oversizing after a win or a loss

A trader doubles size after a winning streak to "press the advantage," or after a losing streak to "win it back." Both moves raise risk of ruin exactly when discipline matters most. The fix is mechanical: size comes from the 1% rule, never from how you feel about the last trade.

### 2. Trading without a stop-loss

A trade without a stop is not a trade with more patience. It is an open-ended loss waiting for a bad day. Consider a hypothetical options buyer who skips the stop on a volatile expiry-day position: a single adverse gap can erase weeks of small wins. Every trade gets a stop before entry, without exception.

### 3. Overtrading to "make the day"

Taking ten trades when your plan called for two is not working harder. Each extra trade adds costs and decision fatigue, and the weakest trades of the day are usually the ones taken out of boredom. Set a maximum number of trades per day in advance, and stop when you hit it — win or lose.

### 4. Ignoring costs, which quietly eat edges

Costs are the silent partner in every trade, and they take their share whether you win or lose. The locked Indian figures to keep in mind:

- Delivery trades in equity attract STT of 0.1% on both the buy and sell sides.
- Futures trades attract STT of 0.05% on the sell side.
- Options trades attract STT of 0.15% on the sell side, including exercised in-the-money options.
- NSE's equity-options charge is 0.03553% per side, all-in.

Brokerage, exchange charges, GST, and SEBI charges sit on top of these. A strategy that looks profitable before costs can be a losing strategy after them — which is one reason SEBI's data counts losses after costs, and why your own expectancy must be computed net of every charge line.

### 5. Treating a hot streak as proof

Five winning weeks do not prove an edge, and five losing weeks do not disprove one. Short runs are mostly noise. Judge a strategy only on 30-plus trades with consistent rules, and re-check expectancy every quarter. The traders who last are the ones who distrust their own recent results the most.

## A Realistic 12-Month Plan to Find Out Whether Trading Can Pay You

This plan has one purpose: to give you an honest answer about your own trading within a year, without risking money you need.

- Month 1: Open a practice account. Learn order types, stops, and how to read a contract note line by line. Write your first one-page trading plan: markets, setup, entry, stop, target, maximum trades per day.
- Months 2–3: Trade the practice account under your written rules. Journal every trade, including the ones you skipped and why. Review weekly for rule breaks, not just profits.
- Month 4: Fund a small real account with money you can afford to lose completely. Trade the smallest size allowed. Your goal is emotional data, not profit.
- Months 5–8: Keep trading small. Build your journal to 50 trades. Compute expectancy net of costs. If it is negative, change one variable at a time and re-test.
- Months 9–10: If expectancy is positive, increase size in small steps — never more than doubling at once, and only after a clean month of rule-following.
- Months 11–12: Review the full year. Positive expectancy plus disciplined sizing plus a full year of records is the minimum evidence for calling trading a real income source. Anything less is a hypothesis, not an income.

If at any point the process stops being enjoyable or starts threatening money you need, pause. The market will still be there when you return, and your capital will be too — which is more than can be said for traders who kept going on tilt.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can beginners make money from trading?

Beginners can make money on individual trades, but consistent profit is rare in the first year. The realistic beginner goal is not income but a clean journal of 30 to 50 rule-following trades, from which expectancy can be computed.

Most beginners lose money early because they risk too much per trade, trade without stops, and judge strategies on a handful of trades. Small size and strict rules are what turn early losses into tuition instead of ruin.

### Is trading a good way to make money?

Trading is a good way to make money only for people who treat it as a skill-based business with measurable edges and strict risk control. For everyone else, it is one of the fastest ways to lose money, as SEBI's data shows.

It is a poor way to make urgent money. If you need income this month, a job or freelance work pays with certainty that trading cannot offer. Trading rewards patience and capital; it punishes urgency.

### Can you make a living from trading?

Yes, some traders make a living from trading, but the conditions are demanding. You need a proven positive expectancy over many trades, capital large enough that a realistic monthly return covers your expenses, and living expenses saved separately from trading capital.

A practical test: six consecutive months where trading profits cover your monthly expenses, with losing months absorbed without touching living savings. Until your record passes that test, trading is a side income at best, not a living.

### How much do traders earn per month?

There is no honest single number, because monthly trading income depends on capital, expectancy, and market conditions. Arithmetic gives the shape: ₹5,00,000 of capital earning an illustrative 2% a month produces about ₹10,000, while ₹10,00,000 at the same rate produces about ₹20,000.

Beware of anyone quoting an "average trader income." Averages hide the fact that most traders lose money, and the minority who profit earn wildly different amounts. Your own expectancy multiplied by your capital is the only monthly figure that means anything.

### Do traders make money every day?

No. Even consistently profitable traders have losing days and losing months. A trader averaging 2% a month can easily post a −3% month followed by a +8% month.

Daily profit targets are dangerous because they pressure traders into taking trades their plan does not justify. Professionals judge results over quarters, not days, and a trader who claims to profit every day is either lying or has too short a record to know better.

### [Why do most traders lose money?](/articles/why-do-most-traders-lose-money-in-india/)

Most traders lose money for a short list of repeatable reasons: they risk too much per trade, trade without stop-losses, overtrade out of boredom or revenge, ignore costs, and judge strategies on tiny samples.

SEBI's data adds a structural reason: in leveraged F&O, costs and leverage compound small mistakes into large losses, which is why over 91% of individual F&O traders lost money in FY2024-25. The market is not rigged against retail traders, but the combination of leverage, costs, and undisciplined sizing produces the same result as if it were.

### Is trading better than a regular job?

Trading is better than a regular job only once it reliably pays more, with less stress, than the job it replaces — measured over at least six months of real records, not one good month. Before that point, a job is better in almost every way: fixed pay, benefits, and no risk of paying to work.

The smartest arrangement for most people is both: a job for certainty and trading as a growing side skill. Let the trading record earn the right to replace the job, rather than quitting first and hoping the record follows.

## The Bottom Line

You can make money from trading, but the path runs through survival first and income second. Compute your expectancy from real trades, risk no more than 1% per trade, keep living expenses far away from trading capital, and judge yourself over quarters, not days.

If you do that, you give yourself the one thing most traders never get: enough time for a real edge to show up in your results. If you skip it, you join the base rate — and the base rate, as SEBI's data shows, is not kind.
