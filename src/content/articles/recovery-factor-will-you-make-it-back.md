*By Kaizen · 29 October 2026 · Monks Of Market*

Will you make it back? That question returns after every drawdown, and it is a fair question, but on its own it has no answer. Recovery is not a matter of hope or patience. It depends on four things you can actually check: how much gain your smaller, post-drawdown balance needs, how long that gain takes at a pace you can honestly assume, whether your strategy still has an edge after costs, and what size you trade from here.

This article gives you that verdict. It is not a calculator walkthrough and not a re-teach of the recovery-factor metric. It is a decision guide: work through the numbers, judge the levers, and reach one of four outcomes — keep, resize, pause-and-prove, or stop-and-reset.

## The Direct Answer: Gain, Pace, Edge, and Size — Not Hope

Here is the direct answer. You have a plausible path back only if all four of these hold: the gain required from your trough is reachable, the pace you assume is plausible rather than wished for, your edge after costs is still positive, and your size is survivable while the recovery plays out. If any one of them fails, the honest answer may be "probably not at this size, like this" — and that is not the same as saying the strategy is worthless. It means the recovery plan, as currently set up, does not add up.

That distinction matters. Traders in a drawdown often treat the verdict as a judgement on themselves or their system. It is a judgement on neither. It is arithmetic plus three honest judgements, and you can change the arithmetic by changing the size, the edge, or the plan — but you cannot change it by wanting the recovery to be faster.

The rest of this article works through that verdict step by step, using one worked example throughout, and ends with a checklist you can apply to your own drawdown.

## The Verdict in One Worked Example: Rs 2,00,000 Down to Rs 1,60,000

Take an account that peaked at Rs 2,00,000 and now sits at Rs 1,60,000. That is a 20% drawdown — Rs 40,000 down from the peak.

To return to Rs 2,00,000, the account does not need a 20% gain. It needs Rs 40,000 earned on the smaller Rs 1,60,000 balance, which is a 25% gain. That single shift — measuring from the trough, not the peak — is the whole reason drawdown recovery feels harder than the drawdown itself felt. The money you lost is measured against the bigger number; the money you must make is measured against the smaller one.

## The Two Numbers That Decide Recovery: How Much Gain, and How Long at a Stated Pace

The verdict rests on two numbers, in this order:

1. **How much gain is required**, measured from the trough balance.
2. **How long that gain takes** at a monthly pace you state openly as an assumption.

The second number is where most recovery talk goes wrong. A time figure without its assumption attached sounds like a promise. It is not. Every time figure in this article is an illustration under a stated assumption — steady gains at the stated pace, with no further losses — never a forecast of what will happen to your account.

### How Much Gain Is Required — and Why It Is Measured From the Trough

The required gain is the drawdown divided by what remains: D ÷ (1 − D). The gain is earned on the reduced trough balance, not on the original peak, so the required percentage is always larger than the drawdown percentage. For the worked example, a 20% drawdown needs 25%: 0.20 ÷ 0.80 = 0.25.

That is the only formula this article needs. It tells you the size of the climb before you ask how fast you might climb it.

### How Long That Gain Takes at 2% and 3% a Month — Under a Stated Assumption

Now add a pace, stated as an assumption and nothing more. At a steady 3% a month, the 25% gain needed in the worked example takes about 7–8 months — and that figure holds only under steady gains with no further losses; it is an illustration, not a forecast. At a steady 2% a month, the same 25% gain takes about 11 months — again, only under steady gains with no further losses, and again as an illustration, not a forecast.

Notice what changed between those two sentences: nothing about the drawdown, only the pace. A single percentage point of monthly pace moves the recovery by roughly three to four months in this example. That sensitivity is exactly why the pace must be chosen honestly, from what the strategy has actually produced after costs, rather than from the pace that gives the most comforting answer.

## Recovery Factor as One Short Check — Not the Whole Verdict

One more check belongs in the verdict, and it is deliberately kept short. The recovery factor is net profit divided by maximum drawdown. It asks a simple question about a strategy's history: how much profit did it make for each unit of its worst fall?

Read it in plain English:

- **Below 1** means the strategy's net profit has not yet covered its own worst drawdown — on that episode, it is still underwater.
- **A low value** warns that the system spends much of its life climbing out of holes rather than making new ground.
- **A higher value** suggests the profits have comfortably outweighed the worst fall.

As a rule of thumb from backtest-reporting practice — not a verified universal standard, since different sources use different bands — a recovery factor above 5 is often described as healthy, while one below 2 warns. Treat those bands as a rough guide, not a pass mark: they are a way to spot a warning, not a verdict on their own.

That is all this article needs from the ratio. For the full explainer, including how to read it across a backtest, see [Recovery Factor](/articles/recovery-factor/). The verdict itself still comes from the gain required, the pace, the edge, and the size — the ratio is one supporting check, not the decision.

## Depth Is Not Duration: A Shallow-but-Long Drawdown Tells a Different Story Than a Deep-but-Short One

Two drawdowns can look similar on a single number and mean completely different things. **Depth** is how far an account fell from its peak, in percent. **Duration** is how long the account stayed below its previous peak before recovering to it.

- **A shallow-but-long drawdown** — say, a modest fall that drags on for months without recovering — points to a stalled system. The edge may have faded, the market regime may have changed, or the strategy may be treading water. The damage is not dramatic, but the account is not going anywhere either, and time spent stalled is time the recovery never starts.
- **A deep-but-short drawdown** — a sharp fall followed by a quick return toward the peak — points to a shock the system absorbed. The fall hurt, but the recovery machinery worked. The risk here is different: a system that survives sharp shocks can still be undone by the next one if size is too large.

Judge both together. A deep drawdown that recovers quickly and a shallow one that never recovers are not the same problem, and they do not call for the same decision. Depth tells you how big the climb is; duration tells you whether the climb is actually happening.

## The Three Levers That Change the Answer: Edge, Size, and Time

Measurement tells you where you stand. These three levers are what you can actually judge and act on, and each comes with an honest trade-off.

### Edge: Is It Still Positive After Costs?

This is the gating lever, and it comes first for a reason. Recovery to the previous peak is only possible if the strategy's edge remains positive after costs — brokerage, taxes, slippage, and every other cost of actually trading. A zero or negative after-cost edge cannot compound an account back to its peak, however long it is given. Time does not fix a negative edge; it only extends the period over which it operates.

So ask the question directly, and answer it from evidence rather than from the period when the strategy worked best: after all costs, is this strategy still making money on recent trades? If the honest answer is no, or "I don't know," then the recovery verdict is not about patience — it is about whether the edge needs fixing before any recovery plan makes sense. That is what the pause-and-prove path in the decision section is for.

### Size: Smaller Size Slows the Bleeding — and Slows the Recovery

Reducing size is the most common recovery move, and it is usually the right instinct — but it is a trade-off, not a free lunch. Smaller size means any further loss is smaller, which protects the trough from deepening. It also means any gain is smaller, which stretches the recovery.

Take the worked example. If halving size roughly halves the monthly pace — an illustrative proportional-scaling assumption, not a measured fact — then the recovery stretches from about 7–8 months to about 15 months at the halved 3% pace, and from about 11 months to about 22 months at the halved 2% pace. Each of those figures still holds only under steady gains with no further losses, and each is an illustration, not a forecast.

That is the trade: halved size buys survival and costs time. For many traders that is exactly the right trade — a slower recovery that actually happens beats a fast one that blows up halfway. But go in with your eyes open: if you halve size, do not keep expecting the original recovery date.

If resizing is your verdict, the [Position Size Calculator](/tools/position-size/) can help you work out the new size from your risk budget rather than from feel.

### Time: Recovery Time Grows Faster Than Drawdown Depth

The third lever is time itself, and it punishes depth disproportionately. At a fixed monthly pace, a deeper drawdown needs a disproportionately longer recovery, because two things work against the trader at once: the required gain is larger, and it must be earned on a smaller compounding base. Double the drawdown does not double the recovery time — it more than doubles it.

That is why early action matters more than it seems. Every additional point of drawdown does not just add to the loss; it lengthens the climb by more than the last point did. A drawdown left to deepen while a trader waits for it to "come back on its own" is a drawdown whose recovery is quietly getting much longer.

## The Decision: Keep, Resize, Pause-and-Prove, or Stop-and-Reset

The verdict resolves into four paths. Each fits a different combination of the numbers above.

- **Keep trading as you are.** This fits when the required gain is reachable, the pace assumption is honest and based on actual after-cost results, the edge is still positive after costs, the size is survivable, and the drawdown is within the triggers you set in advance. Keep does not mean ignore — it means the plan still adds up, so you follow it.
- **Resize — down, not up.** This fits when the edge is intact but the size makes the drawdown unbearable, or when a further loss at current size would deepen the trough past your trigger. You accept a longer recovery in exchange for survival. See the size trade-off above before choosing this path.
- **Pause-and-prove.** This fits when you are unsure the edge is still positive after costs, or when the drawdown has lasted long enough to look like a stalled system rather than a shock. Pause live trading, test the strategy on recent data, and return only when the evidence — not the hope — says the edge is back. Pausing is not quitting; it is refusing to pay live tuition for an unproven edge.
- **Stop-and-reset.** This fits when the after-cost edge is no longer positive, when depth or duration has passed your pre-written trigger, or when repeated attempts to trade out of the drawdown have only deepened the trough. Reset means stepping back, rebuilding the plan from scratch, and starting again with a size and strategy you can defend. It is the honest verdict, not a failure of discipline.

### Set Your Triggers Before the Next Drawdown, Not in the Middle of One

None of these decisions should be made at the worst moment. Before the next drawdown — ideally now, while you are clear-headed — write down the conditions that would move you from one path to the next: the depth at which you resize, the duration after which you pause, the evidence that would make you stop and reset. Decide them as rules, not feelings, and write them somewhere you will actually see them.

A trigger decided mid-drawdown is not a trigger; it is a negotiation with your own stress. The whole point of pre-written triggers is that they decide for you when you least trust your own judgement.

### The Trap to Name Plainly: Sizing Up to Make It Back Faster

The most dangerous move in a drawdown is also the most tempting: increase position size to recover faster. Name it for what it is — a risk-amplifying move, not a shortcut.

Larger size also enlarges any further loss, and a further loss on the already-reduced base raises the required recovery gain further. The trough deepens, the climb gets longer, and the account that needed a 25% gain can suddenly need far more. Sizing up does not shorten the recovery; it gambles the remaining capital on the recovery happening quickly, and if it does not, there is less capital left to recover with. If the verdict points anywhere, it points to resizing down or pausing — never to sizing up out of impatience.

## Risks and Limits of This Verdict

This verdict is only as good as its assumptions, and its limits deserve stating plainly.

- **Every pace in this article is an assumption, not a forecast.** Real recovery is uneven: it includes flat months and further losses, so actual recovery times will differ from the illustrations — often by a lot. A month of further losses does not just pause the clock; it deepens the trough and raises the required gain.
- **The halved-size figures assume returns scale proportionally with size.** That is a simplification used for illustration, not a measured fact. Real halved-size results will differ.
- **The recovery-factor bands are a rule of thumb from backtest-reporting practice**, not a verified universal standard. Different sources use different bands, so use them as a warning light, not a pass mark.
- **This article is a decision framework, not advice for your specific account.** It cannot know your costs, your strategy, or your risk tolerance, and it is no substitute for working through your own numbers.
- **Trading involves risk of loss of capital.** Leverage amplifies both gains and losses, so a leveraged account can lose more, and faster, than an unleveraged one. Past recovery patterns — yours or anyone else's — do not guarantee future recovery, and no outcome described in this article is guaranteed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it take to recover from a trading drawdown?

It depends on three things: how deep the drawdown is, what monthly pace you can honestly assume, and whether your edge after costs is still positive. There is no single answer, and anyone giving you one without those three inputs is guessing. As an illustration only: a 20% drawdown needs a 25% gain, which takes about 7–8 months at a steady 3% a month or about 11 months at a steady 2% a month — each figure only under steady gains with no further losses, and neither is a forecast. If the edge is gone, the answer is not a longer time — it is that recovery at the current setup will not happen.

### How much gain do I need to recover from a 20% / 30% / 50% loss?

Because the gain is earned on the smaller trough balance, not the original peak, the required gain is always larger than the loss: a 20% loss needs a 25% gain, a 30% loss needs 42.9%, and a 50% loss needs 100%. The one-line reason is D ÷ (1 − D), where D is the drawdown. For the full reasoning, see [Why a 50% Loss Needs a 100% Gain: The Asymmetry of Drawdown](/articles/why-a-50-loss-needs-a-100-gain-the-asymmetry-of-drawdown/).

### Should I increase my position size to recover faster?

No — not as a shortcut. Increasing size also increases the size of any further loss, and a further loss on the already-reduced base raises the required recovery gain further. It is a risk-amplifying move that can turn a recoverable drawdown into a much deeper one. The honest options are to keep trading at a survivable size, resize down, or pause-and-prove until the edge is confirmed.

### What is the difference between drawdown depth and drawdown duration?

Depth is how far an account fell from its peak, measured in percent. Duration is how long the account stayed below its previous peak before recovering to it. Both must be judged together: a shallow-but-long drawdown suggests a stalled system, while a deep-but-short one suggests a sharp shock that was absorbed quickly. Depth alone cannot tell you which problem you have.

### What is a good recovery factor, and what does a low one warn me about?

The recovery factor is net profit divided by maximum drawdown. Below 1, net profit has not yet covered the strategy's worst drawdown. A low value warns that the system spends much of its life climbing out of holes rather than making new ground. As a rule of thumb from backtest-reporting practice — not a verified universal standard — above 5 is often described as healthy and below 2 as a warning. For the full explainer, see [Recovery Factor](/articles/recovery-factor/).

### When should I stop trying to recover and reset instead?

Reset when the verdict points there: your after-cost edge is no longer positive, the drawdown's depth or duration has passed the trigger you set in advance, or repeated attempts to trade out of it have only deepened the trough. Resetting is not admitting defeat. It is the decision to stop paying for a recovery plan that the numbers say will not work, and to rebuild from a plan you can defend.

## Your Verdict Checklist: Apply It to Your Own Drawdown

Work through these five steps with your own numbers, in order:

1. **Required gain:** Work out your drawdown from peak to trough, then the gain required on the trough balance (D ÷ (1 − D)). That is the size of your climb.
2. **Pace assumption:** Pick a monthly pace based on what the strategy has actually produced after costs — stated as an assumption, with the steady-gains, no-further-losses caveat attached. Work out how long the climb takes at that pace, and ask whether that time is acceptable.
3. **Edge check:** Is the after-cost edge still positive on recent evidence? If not, or if you do not know, the recovery plan waits until it is proven.
4. **Size decision:** Is your current size survivable through the recovery, including a further loss? If not, resize down — accepting the longer recovery — rather than sizing up.
5. **Trigger check:** What pre-written trigger would change your decision — a deeper trough, a longer duration, a failed edge check? If a trigger has already fired, act on it now, not after the next loss.

If all five steps check out, keep trading and follow the plan. If any one of them fails, the verdict is resize, pause-and-prove, or stop-and-reset — and making that call honestly is what gives the account its best chance of ever making it back.
