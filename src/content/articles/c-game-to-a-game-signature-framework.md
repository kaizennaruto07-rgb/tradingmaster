> **Educational disclaimer:** This article is for education only and is not financial advice. Trading involves substantial risk of loss. The framework below is a behavioural safeguard — it is designed to keep you trading your plan instead of your emotions, not to produce profits.

Your A-game and your C-game both have a signature. Only one of them is on file with you.

You know your peak self on the charts. You know the focused version of you — patient, planned, out at the stop without drama. But the version of you that takes five extra trades after a loser, the version that doubles size on a "sure thing," the version that re-enters a position you just exited out of sheer frustration — that version has a signature too. A pattern. A set of tells. And you have never written it down.

This week you mapped your leaks: the giveback circuit, the revenge circuit, the overtrading circuit. This is the capstone chapter. It answers the question the week was always leading to: *I've seen my failure circuits — how do I build the trader who doesn't spring them?*

## The short answer: a personal signature system that keeps you trading your plan, not your emotions

The system has two parts, and both are written, not willpower-based.

**Part one is a map.** Two index-card-sized documents in your own words: your **A-game card** (what peak-you actually does — the behaviours, the mental state, the setup discipline) and your **C-game card** (your five personal early-warning signals — the specific mistakes that tell you the bad version of you has taken the terminal). Nobody else can write these for you. A guru's morning routine is not your signature.

**Part two is an operating protocol.** A pre-market traffic-light state audit (green = trade the full plan, yellow = reduced size, red = no trading today). An in-session physical interrupt for the moment your C-game tells appear. A post-session process score that grades execution, not rupees. And a weekly inchworm review that measures one thing only: is this month's C-game less bad than last month's?

Everything below unpacks those two parts — the map, the protocol, and the counter-intuitive law underneath them: stop chasing more A-game days; make your C-game days less bad. That is the entire game.

## The five faces of one C-game: naming your emotional trading mistakes

Giveback, revenge trading, overtrading, tilt, FOMO — the week treated them as separate chapters because each needed its own article. But they are not five separate problems. They are five faces of **one** C-game: the state in which you stop trading your tested plan and start trading on feeling, urgency, or necessity.

- **Giveback** is the *win-side* face. A winning streak floods you with the urge to keep playing, and you hand the morning's profits back to the afternoon. This week's first chapter mapped it in full: [why traders give back profits](/articles/why-traders-give-back-profits/).
- **Revenge trading** is the *loss-side* face. A red day becomes a debt the market "owes" you, and the recovery trades get bigger and sloppier. This week's second chapter built the loss protocol for it: [how to stop revenge trading after a loss](/articles/revenge-trading/).
- **Overtrading** is what C-game *looks like on the trade log*. When the emotions are hard to see in real time, the broker statement shows them: too many trades, sizes that drift, setups that weren't setups. [The overtrading chapter](/articles/overtrading/) is your same-week sibling — one line of map, not a re-teach.
- **Tilt** is the *broad framework* face — the full emotional-decision spiral, of which revenge is one branch. It gets [its own framework article](/articles/tilt-in-trading/) next; for today, it is enough to know it wears your C-game face.
- **FOMO** is the *chasing face* — the compulsion to enter moves that are already gone. The fifth face gets its own chapter tomorrow; for today, note that it, too, is C-game with a different mask.

Each article in this week's curriculum owns its circuit. This article owns what sits above them: the identity system — your signature — that short-circuits whichever face appears.

## The signature map: write your own A-game card and C-game card

Here is why borrowed routines fail: your tells are personal. Another trader's red flags — caffeine and late nights, maybe — may never be yours. The C-game card only works if it reads like *you* wrote it, because you did. Same for the A-game card: peak-you has specific habits, a specific calm, a specific discipline. Describe that, and you have a target your protocol can protect.

Take an actual index card or one page in your journal for each. The instructions are below; the example fills are written in the reader's voice — yours should sound like you, not like this article.

### Card one: your A-game card

Describe peak-you. Not aspirational-you, not a trader you admire — the version of you that shows up on your best days. Be concrete and observable: behaviours, mental state, setup discipline. Ask yourself: *what was I doing differently on the days I traded my plan perfectly?*

An example A-game card, in the reader's voice:

> **MY A-GAME CARD**
>
> 1. I write my plan before the market opens — levels, bias, and max loss — and I don't touch the terminal until it's done.
> 2. I sleep before midnight and skip trading days after bad sleep. Tired-me makes C-game decisions.
> 3. I take only my two highest-conviction setups. If neither appears, I close the laptop at 11:30 without guilt.
> 4. I size exactly per plan — never more because I "feel good," never less because the last trade hurt.
> 5. I exit at the stop or the target the moment price touches it. No "let me see one more candle."

### Card two: your C-game card — five early-warning signals

Now the other card. List **your five personal early-warning signals** — the specific errors that tell you the bad version of you is at the controls. The diagnostic question: *what mistakes would I never make on a good day?* Pull from your journal's worst sessions — the pattern is always there. Re-entering after exiting, size mistakes, plan abandonment: whatever yours are, name them in first person, present tense.

An example C-game card, in the reader's voice:

> **MY C-GAME CARD — when I see any of these, the interrupt protocol starts**
>
> 1. I re-enter a position within minutes of exiting it.
> 2. I increase size after a loss to "make it back faster."
> 3. I stop looking at my stop-loss level and watch only the P&L number.
> 4. I take a trade that wasn't in my morning plan because the market is moving without me.
> 5. I say "just one more trade" after I've already hit my daily loss budget.

### How to personalize your cards

1. **Mine your journal.** Open your last two weeks of trades (or your broker statement if you don't journal yet). Your C-game signals are the mistakes that repeat. Circle them; they go on the card.
2. **Write in "I" statements, present tense.** "I double size after losses" beats "avoid overtrading after losses." The card is a mirror, not a rulebook.
3. **Keep it to five.** More than five becomes a poster you stop reading. Five fits on a card, and five real signals catch everything — C-game always announces itself.
4. **Put both cards where you can see them.** Taped to the monitor, as your phone lock screen, inside the journal cover. The map only works if it's visible before the trade, not after.
5. **Update them at your weekly review.** When a new tell appears — or an old one stops appearing — edit the card. Your signature evolves; the cards keep up.

## The inchworm law: raise your C-game floor with the trading inchworm concept

Here is the load-bearing idea of this chapter, and it runs against everything trading social media sells you.

Picture your performance as a bell curve. On the right edge sits your A-game — your best days, the ones you post about. In the middle, your B-game — ordinary days, plan followed, nothing dramatic. On the left edge, your C-game — your worst days, the ones where the plan goes out the window.

The instinct is to chase the right edge: more A-game days, bigger A-game days. But pulling the front of a bell curve forward without fixing the back end just stretches the curve wider. The range widens, the lows get lower, and eventually the whole thing snaps back. That is what traders call a blow-up: not a failure of talent, a failure of range.

The improvement move is the opposite. It is called the inchworm law: **don't chase more A-game days — make your C-game days less bad.** Pull the *back* of the bell curve forward. Inchworms move by dragging the rear forward, not by stretching the front.

Three rules make this law operational:

**Rule 1: Compare C-game to C-game, never A-game to C-game.** Looking at your worst day next to your best day only produces shame, which produces worse decisions. The only comparison that matters: *is this month's C-game less bad than last month's?* Compare your C-game across 1, 3, 6, and 12 months. If your worst days are getting less bad, you are moving forward. If they aren't, you haven't found what causes your C-game yet — and finding it is the work.

**Rule 2: C-game → B-game, never forced A-game.** Trying to jump straight from a C-game spiral back into peak flow in a single session is rare, and attempting it is dangerous — the frustration of a failed "reset" is exactly how C-game becomes revenge trading ([the loss protocol article](/articles/revenge-trading/) covers that circuit). The realistic move is C-game to **B-game**: regain control, stop the bleeding, trade the rest of the day mechanically or walk away. A-game returns when its conditions are rebuilt — sleep, calm, a fresh session — not when it is forced.

**Rule 3: Make the fix small and weekly.** When a C-game mistake appears, the job is not to eliminate it in one heroic session. Pile on too much and you injure the thing you're trying to fix — confidence and motivation crack under overhaul. Make it slightly less bad this week than last week. One signal, one fix, seven days. Boring, consistent — and because it survives contact with reality, it compounds.

## The daily operating system: how to stay in your A-game

The signature cards are the map. This is the map in motion: a four-part daily and weekly protocol that keeps you in A-game conditions and short-circuits C-game the moment a tell appears.

### Pre-market: the traffic-light state audit

Before charts, audit the trader. Ask five questions — sleep, stress (your state about the session ahead), recent P&L feeling, life stress (pressures off the desk), physical state — and score honestly. Then set the light:

- **🟢 Green — full plan.** Slept well, calm, no emotional baggage from yesterday. Trade your plan exactly as written, normal planned size.
- **🟡 Yellow — reduced size.** One or two warnings: poor sleep, a frustrating or euphoric day behind you, life stress bleeding in. Trade at most half your normal size — a protocol many experienced traders use — or skip the riskier setups.
- **🔴 Red — do not trade.** Three or more warnings, or any single screaming one: exhausted, furious, heartbroken, euphoric after a huge win. The most profitable decision today may be no position at all.

Two things that surprise traders about this audit. First: **euphoria is a warning, not a green light.** The day after your biggest win is one of the most dangerous days of your month — the giveback circuit from [this week's first chapter](/articles/why-traders-give-back-profits/) starts with feeling invincible. A common practitioner rule is to halve size when euphoric — a convention, not a law, and worth trying. Second: keep the whole audit under five minutes. Some traders score it 1–10 on paper; the traffic-light framing above is our version of the same idea.

### In-session: the physical interrupt protocol

Your C-game card lists your tells. The moment you catch one *in the session* — the re-entry, the size creep, the P&L-staring — the protocol is physical, not mental: **stand up, walk away from the terminal, no trading until the state is rebuilt.**

This works because of what is happening in your head. Popular psychology calls it an amygdala hijack — treat that as a metaphor, not neuroscience: under emotional flooding, the part of your brain that reasons gets drowned out by the part that reacts. Logic doesn't reach a hijacked brain. Your body does. Standing up and physically leaving the desk breaks the stimulus loop in a way that arguing with yourself never will.

This is a protocol many traders use — not a clinical prescription, and not therapy. Walk for ten minutes. Drink water. Come back only when you can read your C-game card without flinching. If you can't, the session is over, and that is a *successful* decision, not a failed day.

### Post-session: the process-P&L score

At the end of every session, grade the day on execution — not rupees. Process P&L, a practitioner convention:

- **Followed the plan and lost money = a good trade.** The market paid you nothing today, but your process is intact. That is a green day on the score that matters.
- **Broke the plan and won money = a bad trade.** The market just rewarded your worst habit. That is a red day wearing a green mask — and it is how C-game learns to trust itself.

Score the session in one line in your journal: *plan followed? yes/no. Result irrelevant.* Over a month, this single line is the most honest mirror you own. It is also the raw material for the weekly review below.

### Weekly: the inchworm review

Once a week — Sunday evening, or whatever day closes your trading week — run the inchworm review. It has two questions and one decision:

1. **Which C-game face appeared this week?** Name it from the week's map: giveback, revenge, overtrading, tilt, FOMO. No judgment; a name.
2. **Is this month's C-game less bad than last month's?** The inchworm measurement rule. Compare your worst day this month to your worst day last month. Less bad = progress. Same or worse = you haven't found the cause yet.
3. **One fix for next week.** Exactly one. Not "fix my psychology" — something like "if I see tell #3 on my C-game card, I stand up immediately." One fix, seven days, then re-measure.

That is the whole review. Ten minutes, and it is the engine of the inchworm law: the back end moves forward one week at a time.

## C-game costs rupees: price one deviation afternoon

The charges are not why C-game days lose money — the bad decisions are. The charges are just the receipt.

One unplanned NIFTY-option round trip costs ~₹62.53 in total charges — the worked figure from our net-expectancy chapter ([how to calculate real expectancy](/articles/how-to-calculate-real-expectancy/)).

Now price the deviation *afternoon* as a whole. A classic C-game afternoon — three revenge re-entries, two oversized FOMO chases, one "just one more" after the loss budget — is five or six unplanned round trips. That's **₹300–₹375 in guaranteed charges alone**, paid before the market moves a single point in your favour. One afternoon. Guaranteed drag. The decisions caused the loss; the charges just documented it. Price it once and the inchworm math gets personal.

The week has already given you the tools to see the full picture, one line each. C-game days are the fastest path to ruin — run them through the [risk of ruin](/articles/risk-of-ruin-trading/) lens (or the site's [risk-of-ruin calculator](/tools/risk-of-ruin/), one sobering input at a time). C-game trades are the unmodelled leak in [your real expectancy](/articles/how-to-calculate-real-expectancy/). The [Monte Carlo fan chart](/articles/monte-carlo-simulation-for-traders/) modelled your *plan's* trades — your C-game trades are the trades the simulation never saw (the site's [What-If simulator](/tools/what-if-simulator/) shows this the moment you add unplanned trades). Your [position size](/articles/position-sizing/) must interact with your traffic-light readiness level, not just your account. And the honest self-grading behind all of this shares its ethos with [the honest protocol](/articles/paper-trading-vs-real-trading/).

## Limitations: what this framework cannot do

Honesty first — this framework is a behavioural safeguard, not a profit strategy. It will not make a bad strategy profitable, it will not produce returns, and it will not fix a plan that has no edge. What it does is narrower and more valuable: it keeps the worst version of you from destroying the work of the best version of you.

Three more caveats. The traffic-light size rules, the euphoria size cut, the physical interrupt, and process P&L are **practitioner conventions** — habits many experienced traders report using — not laws, and not medical or psychological advice. Second, fee figures change: STT itself changed in April 2026, and exchange schedules move. The ₹62.53 worked figure cited above was verified as of October 2026; re-check before you rely on it. Third, the charges section priced the *cost* of deviation trades — never mistake that for the *cause* of C-game losses. The cause is the behaviour; the charges are just the receipt.

## The identity upgrade: survive to the level of your C-game

Here is the week's payoff, and it is an identity claim, not a tip.

Amateurs try harder. They promise themselves discipline, they download someone else's morning routine, they swear this month will be different — and their worst days stay exactly as bad as ever, because nothing in the system changed.

Professionals build a system where their worst days are survivable and their best days compound. Two written cards. A five-minute audit. A physical interrupt. A process score. A ten-minute weekly review. None of it is dramatic. All of it moves the back of the bell curve forward, one week at a time.

You don't rise to the level of your A-game. You survive to the level of your C-game.

The week ends with a trader who has a map, an audit, and an interrupt protocol — not just five warnings. ## Frequently asked questions

**What is my C-game in trading?**
Your C-game is the bottom of your performance bell curve — the specific mistakes you make when you're stressed, tired, or tilted: abandoning the plan, trading on feeling, re-entering right after exiting, sizing errors (too big from overconfidence, too small from fear). It is not bad luck. It is a pattern with a signature — which is why this article's whole point is writing it down.

**How do I know when I am playing my C-game?**
Check your tell list. The classic signs: making errors you'd never make on a good day ("I know I shouldn't do this"), re-entering a position soon after exiting, size mistakes, losing interest after a big mistake. Then match what you see against your C-game card's five tells. Your early-warning signals are personal, which is exactly why you map them yourself instead of borrowing someone else's list.

**Can I jump straight from C-game back to A-game in one session?**
Rarely — and forcing it usually makes things worse. The frustration of a failed "reset" is precisely how C-game becomes revenge trading. The realistic move is C-game → B-game: regain control, stop the bleeding, trade mechanically or walk away. A-game returns when its conditions are rebuilt — rest, calm, a fresh session — not when it is forced.

**How do I improve my A-game?**
Counter-intuitively: don't chase the front — pull the back. The inchworm improves by raising the C-game floor; stretching the A-game without fixing the back end just widens your range and ends in snap-back. Making your worst days slightly less bad, week after week, is what actually advances you.

**How do I measure whether I am getting better?**
Never compare your A-game to your C-game — that only produces shame. Compare your current C-game to your C-game 1, 3, 6, and 12 months ago: are your worst days less bad? If yes, you're moving forward. If no, you haven't found what causes your C-game yet, and finding it is the work.

**What should my pre-market routine include?**
Run the traffic-light state audit — the five checks (sleep, stress (your state about the session ahead), recent P&L feeling, life stress (pressures off the desk), physical state) scored green/yellow/red in under five minutes — then size the day to the light. Full plan on green, reduced size on yellow, no trading on red. That is the whole routine; consistency beats duration.

**How do I assess my emotional state before trading?**
Run a readiness audit: sleep quality, stress (your state about the session ahead), your feeling about recent P&L (frustration *and* euphoria are both warnings), life stress (pressures off the desk), physical state. That is the traffic-light audit from this chapter — five checks, under five minutes: multiple red flags means don't trade; one or two means trade at reduced size. Be honest — your account depends on it.

**How should I grade a trading day?**
On process P&L, not money: followed the plan and lost = a good trade; broke the plan and won = a bad trade. Disciplined execution is what compounds improvement over time, not outcomes on any single day. Grade the execution, ignore the rupees, and the journal line stays honest.
