> **Educational disclaimer:** This article is for education only and is not financial advice. Trading involves substantial risk of loss, including the possible loss of more than your initial margin in leveraged positions. Nothing here guarantees profits.

Most Indian traders do not lose money because they cannot pick stocks. They lose it in small, repeated, entirely avoidable ways — a position slightly too large, three extra trades a day, an option quietly decaying to zero, charges on every round trip — and those small leaks add up faster than any single bad call ever could.

That is the whole story in one paragraph. The rest of this article proves it with SEBI's own numbers, names the nine causes in order of damage, and gives each one a specific fix with exact numbers you can apply to your very next trade.

## The 90% Number, Stated Correctly

Before anything else, the most-quoted statistic in Indian trading needs correcting, because misquoting it is itself one of the errors this article is about.

### What SEBI actually measured

The famous "90%" figure comes from SEBI's studies of **individual traders in the equity F&O (futures and options) segment**. It is not a statistic about all traders, all investors, or everyone who buys shares.

- In FY22, about 90% of individual F&O traders incurred net losses.
- In each year from FY23 to FY25, roughly 91% did.
- In FY26, per SEBI's study released in August 2026, 87.7% incurred net losses, with aggregate net losses of about ₹91,685 crore and an average loss of about ₹1.17 lakh per trader.
- Across FY22 to FY24 combined, about 93% of individual F&O traders lost money, with aggregate losses above ₹1.8 lakh crore.
- Options accounted for about 92% of individual losses in FY26.

### A different segment, a different number

SEBI's study of the equity **cash** segment found something different: **7 out of 10 individual intraday traders** made losses in FY2023. That is an intraday cash figure, not an F&O figure, and the two must never be swapped.

This distinction matters for one practical reason. When someone says "90% of traders lose money" without the F&O qualifier, a beginner hears "trading itself is a lottery" and either avoids markets entirely or — worse — concludes that since everyone loses, risk controls do not matter. The truth is narrower and more useful: the losses concentrate where leverage, short time frames, and high frequency combine.

That is exactly where the fixes in this article operate.

### What the number does not mean

The SEBI figures do not say that 90% of people who buy shares and hold them lose money. They do not say that every active trader is doomed. They say that a specific population — individuals trading leveraged derivatives, mostly options, mostly short-dated — loses at a very high rate.

The question worth asking is not "is trading a scam?" It is "what exactly are those traders doing wrong, and what would the surviving minority be doing differently?" SEBI's own data points at the answer: costs turned a large number of traders who were profitable before charges into net losers, and losses concentrated in options and in high-frequency activity. Those are mechanics, not destiny.

## The Uncomfortable Truth: Most Losing Traders Already Have a Strategy

Here is the contrarian insight most articles miss. Ask a losing trader why they lost and they will usually say their strategy failed, or their stock pick was wrong. But watch their actual trading for a month and a different picture emerges.

- Their entries are often reasonable. Their exits are not.
- Their strategy might win 45% of the time at 1.5:1 reward-to-risk — a workable edge on paper.
- Yet their account bleeds, because they risked 5% per trade instead of 1%, took 12 trades instead of 3, paid charges on every round trip, and held three options positions into expiry week while time decay ate the premium.

The strategy was never the main problem. **Position sizing, costs, and trade frequency were.** This is death by a thousand cuts: no single cut looks fatal, but a thousand of them drain any account, no matter how good the entry signal is.

That reframes the entire fix. Instead of hunting for a better entry indicator — the thing most struggling traders spend their time on — the highest-return work is plugging the leaks: smaller positions, fewer trades, a daily loss limit, and respect for what options decay and charges do to a small account. Every section below follows that logic: cause, then the exact fix.

## Cause 1: Position Size Too Large

This is the single biggest leak, and it is pure arithmetic.

### How oversized positions kill accounts

Consider a trader with a ₹1,00,000 account who risks ₹10,000 per trade — 10% of capital. Three consecutive losses, an utterly ordinary event, cut the account to about ₹72,900. Recovery from a 27% drawdown needs a 37% gain, earned on the smaller remaining base.

The same three losses at 1% risk per trade cost about ₹3,000 total. The account sits at ₹97,000, the trader is calm, and the strategy still has room to work. Same strategy, same losses, completely different outcome.

The difference was never the market. It was the bet size.

### The fix: the 1% rule, applied mechanically

- **Step 1.** Risk no more than 1% of your account on any single trade. On ₹1,00,000, that is ₹1,000. On ₹50,000, it is ₹500.
- **Step 2.** Compute your position from your stop-loss, not from how confident you feel. Position size = risk amount ÷ (entry price − stop-loss price). If the stop is ₹10 away and your risk budget is ₹1,000, you buy 100 shares. No more.
- **Step 3.** Never widen a stop to justify a larger position. If the stop distance forces a tiny position, take the tiny position or skip the trade.
- **Step 4.** Use a calculator instead of mental math. The [position size calculator](/tools/position-size/) turns your risk budget and stop distance into an exact share count.

Read the full reasoning in [position sizing](/articles/position-sizing/) and the mathematics of account death in [risk of ruin](/articles/risk-of-ruin-trading/). If you adopt only one fix from this article, adopt this one.

## Cause 2: No Daily Loss Limit, Then Revenge Trading

The second leak is emotional, but its fix is mechanical too.

### How one bad morning becomes a blown week

A trader loses twice before noon. The plan says stop. Instead, the urge to "win it back today" takes over.

Position sizes double, setups that were never in the plan get traded, and by market close a controlled 2% down day has become a 12% disaster.

This is revenge trading, and it has a recognizable signature: trades taken immediately after a loss, larger than usual, in instruments the trader had not planned to touch. It is also the direct cousin of tilt — the state where frustration, not analysis, drives decisions. Both are covered in depth in [revenge trading](/articles/revenge-trading/) and [tilt in trading](/articles/tilt-in-trading/).

### The fix: a daily loss limit with a real off-switch

- **Step 1.** Set a daily loss limit of 2–3% of your account. On ₹1,00,000, that is ₹2,000–₹3,000. Write it down before the market opens.
- **Step 2.** The moment the limit is hit, stop trading for the day. Not "one more trade to recover." Stop.
- **Step 3.** Make the rule enforceable. Many brokers offer a kill-switch feature that blocks further orders for the session — turn it on. A rule that depends on willpower at the worst moment of the day is not a rule.
- **Step 4.** After any stop-out, wait at least 15–30 minutes before the next trade, and write one line in your journal about what happened. The pause breaks the emotional chain; the journal entry makes the pattern visible over weeks.

A trader who never has a catastrophic day does not need a miraculous strategy. They need a ceiling on bad days, enforced automatically.

## Cause 3: Overtrading — Too Many Trades

Frequency is a cost multiplier, and most struggling traders trade far more often than their edge can support.

### Why more trades usually means more losses

Every trade carries two penalties: charges, and a fresh chance to be wrong. A trader taking 12 trades a day pays charges 12 times and needs their edge to survive 12 independent outcomes. A trader taking 3 trades pays charges 3 times and gives their best setups room to matter.

Worse, trade quality falls as frequency rises. The first two or three trades of the day are usually the planned ones. Trades eight, nine, and ten are usually boredom, FOMO, or revenge in disguise.

The full breakdown is in [overtrading](/articles/overtrading/).

### The fix: a hard cap on trades per day

- **Step 1.** Cap yourself at 3 trades per day to start. If you cannot find 3 genuine setups that match your written plan, take fewer. Fewer is fine.
- **Step 2.** Define a "setup" in writing before the market opens: instrument, trigger, entry, stop, target. If a trade does not match the written definition, it does not count as a setup — it counts as a violation.
- **Step 3.** Track your trades per day for a month. Most traders who do this discover that their profit comes from their first few trades and their losses come from everything after. The data, not discipline lectures, is what convinces them to cut frequency.
- **Step 4.** If you trade intraday, decide in advance whether you are an intraday trader at all. Holding a position overnight without a plan is not a strategy change — it is refusing to accept a loss.

## Cause 4: Buying Options Like Lottery Tickets

Options account for about 92% of individual F&O losses in SEBI's FY26 data. That concentration deserves its own section, because the mechanics of buying options punish exactly the behaviour beginners bring to them.

### How time decay quietly wins

When you buy an option, you are buying time as well as direction. Every day that passes, the option loses a little value to time decay — even if the underlying index does not move at all. A trader can be right about direction, roughly right about timing, and still lose money, because the premium bled away while waiting.

The expiry-day version is worse. Cheap, same-day-expiry options look like lottery tickets: small outlay, huge possible payoff. But the probability structure is brutal — most expire worthless, and the few winners rarely cover the long run of small, repeated losses.

Short-dated options concentrate all of this into hours.

There is also an asymmetry beginners miss. Option sellers collect the decay; option buyers pay it. Buying options means paying a daily rent for the privilege of being right quickly.

If your analysis is slow, vague, or "sometime this week," that rent eats you.

### The fix: rules for options buyers

- **Step 1.** Never buy an option without knowing its expiry and what happens if the underlying stays flat. If you cannot explain the decay cost, do not take the trade.
- **Step 2.** Avoid same-day-expiry speculation as a default activity. If you trade expiry day at all, treat it as a small, capped experiment — a fixed tiny budget, not your main strategy.
- **Step 3.** Size options positions by premium at risk, using the same 1% rule: the entire premium paid is the risk if the option expires worthless. A ₹5,000 premium on a ₹1,00,000 account is already 5% risk.
- **Step 4.** Learn the full charge picture before trading options seriously. [Options trading charges in India](/articles/options-trading-charges-india/) walks through every component.
- **Step 5.** Paper-trade any options approach for at least 20 trades before committing real money. Twenty trades is enough for decay and expiry effects to show up in your results; fewer is not.

## Cause 5: Costs and Taxes Quietly Eating the Edge

A strategy can be profitable before charges and unprofitable after them. SEBI's data shows exactly this: a large number of traders who were profitable before transaction costs ended up as net losers once charges were included.

### The charges that actually apply

Only verified figures are used here. On the sell side of options trades, Securities Transaction Tax (STT) is 0.15% of the premium, including exercised in-the-money options. The NSE equity-options charge is 0.03553% per side, all-in.

For futures, STT is 0.05% on the sell side. For delivery trades in equity, STT is 0.1% on both sides.

Worked example, options sell side: selling options premium worth ₹50,000 attracts STT of ₹75 (0.15%) and an NSE charge of about ₹17.77 (0.03553%) on that side — before brokerage and other statutory charges, which vary and are not estimated here. On a small account taking many trades, these per-trade amounts compound into a serious monthly drag.

### The fix: count costs before you count profits

- **Step 1.** Before adopting any strategy, calculate its cost per round trip using the figures above plus your broker's actual schedule. If the expected profit per trade is smaller than the cost per trade, the strategy cannot work, no matter how good the signals look.
- **Step 2.** Reduce round trips before chasing better entries. Fewer trades, held longer, mechanically cut the cost drag. This is the same frequency fix as Cause 3, now justified by arithmetic rather than discipline.
- **Step 3.** Review a monthly charges statement, not just profit and loss. Many traders discover their gross trading profit was positive and their net result negative — the difference is the leak this section describes.
- **Step 4.** Be especially careful with high-frequency scalping on a small account. When the profit target per trade is tiny, charges take a proportionally larger bite out of every win.

## Cause 6: No Exit Plan — Holding Losers, Averaging Down

The most expensive sentence in trading is "it will come back." Sometimes it does. Often it does not, and the trader who waited has converted a small, planned loss into a large, unplanned one.

### How holding losers destroys the math

A 10% loss needs an 11.11% gain to recover. A 20% loss needs 25%. A 50% loss needs 100%.

The recovery formula is required gain = drawdown ÷ (1 − drawdown), and it steepens brutally: the deeper the hole, the harder the climb, because the recovery is earned on the smaller remaining base.

Now consider averaging down — buying more as the price falls to "lower the average." Each addition increases exposure to a position that is already proving the trader wrong. If the position eventually recovers, averaging down looks brilliant. If it does not, it converts a survivable loss into an account-threatening one.

The full recovery mathematics is in [trading drawdown mathematics of recovery](/articles/trading-drawdown-mathematics-of-recovery/), and the psychology of losing streaks is in [how losing streaks affect trading accounts](/articles/how-losing-streaks-affect-trading-accounts/).

### The fix: decide the exit before the entry

- **Step 1.** Every trade gets a stop-loss level decided before entry, based on the chart or the strategy's rules — never on how much loss "feels acceptable" after the trade is already open.
- **Step 2.** Place the stop as a live order in the system, not as a mental note. A mental stop fails exactly when it is needed most: in fast moves, in volatile opens, and in the trader's own hesitation.
- **Step 3.** Never average down on a losing intraday or short-term trade unless the averaging was part of the written plan before entry, with its own size limit. "I will add once, with half the original size, only if price reaches X" is a plan. "It has fallen a lot, let me buy more" is not.
- **Step 4.** Judge exits by process, not by what happened next. A stop that triggered and then price recovered was still the right decision if it followed the plan — because the same rule, followed consistently, is what prevents the one catastrophic hold.

## Cause 7: Leverage and Margin — Losses Larger Than Your Capital

This is the section most beginners skip, and it answers one of the most important questions in trading.

### Cash versus leveraged positions

In a plain cash delivery trade bought with your own money, the most you can lose is what you put in. A ₹50,000 delivery purchase that goes to zero loses ₹50,000. It cannot lose ₹60,000, because no borrowed money is involved.

Leveraged positions work differently. In futures and options, and in margin-funded cash trades, you control a large exposure with a smaller amount of margin. If the market moves against you beyond your margin, your losses can exceed the money you posted.

The broker will demand additional margin (a margin call), and if it is not provided, the position can be squared off — possibly at the worst moment, locking in a loss larger than the original deposit.

This is not a theoretical edge case. It is the standard mechanics of leveraged trading, and it is why position sizing matters even more in F&O than in cash: leverage multiplies both the win and the loss, but the loss side is what ends accounts.

### The fix: treat leverage as a risk dial, not a buying-power boost

- **Step 1.** Before any leveraged trade, calculate the loss if the position moves to your stop — and if it gaps past your stop. Size the position so that even the gap scenario stays within your 1% risk budget where possible.
- **Step 2.** Keep free margin well above the minimum requirement. Trading at maximum margin leaves no room for normal adverse movement and guarantees forced exits at bad prices.
- **Step 3.** Never use leverage to "make up" for a small account. A small account needs smaller positions and more time, not more leverage. Leverage accelerates the timeline to both profit and ruin, and ruin arrives first for most beginners.
- **Step 4.** Understand the product before trading it. Futures, options buying, and options selling have different loss profiles — options buyers risk the premium paid, while futures traders and options sellers face potentially much larger exposures. Do not trade a product whose worst case you cannot state in one sentence.

## Cause 8: Copying Tips Instead of Understanding Trades

Social media is full of screenshots of winning trades and very few of losing ones. Following tips feels like a shortcut. In practice, it transfers someone else's entry without their exit plan, their position size, or their risk tolerance — which are the parts that actually determine the outcome.

### Why tips fail even when the tipster is good

Consider a trader who copies a tip to buy a stock, but does not know the tipster's stop-loss, target, or time horizon. When the stock falls 4%, the copier has no rule for what to do. The tipster may have exited at a 2% stop hours ago; the copier is still holding, hoping.

The entry was identical. The outcome is different, because the trade was never really the same trade.

There is also an execution lag problem. By the time a recommendation is posted, discussed, and acted on by followers, the price has often moved — so followers routinely get worse entries than the person they are copying. The pattern is documented in [FOMO trading](/articles/fomo-trading/).

### The fix: a tip is research, never a trade

- **Step 1.** Treat every tip as an idea to investigate, not an instruction. Before acting, answer in writing: what is the setup, where is the stop, what is the target, and what would prove the idea wrong?
- **Step 2.** If you cannot answer those four questions, do not take the trade. "Someone I trust said so" is not an answer to any of them.
- **Step 3.** Track copied trades separately in your journal for a month. Most traders find the copied set performs worse than their own planned trades — and that evidence ends the habit more effectively than any advice.
- **Step 4.** Unfollow sources that post only wins. A source that never shows a loss is not teaching trading; it is marketing.

## Cause 9: Expecting Monthly Income From Day One

The final cause is an expectation problem. A beginner who needs trading to pay monthly bills from the first month will take trades they should not take, size them larger than they should, and refuse to stop on bad days — because stopping feels like losing income.

### Why the income expectation is so destructive

Trading income is irregular even for skilled traders. Winning months and losing months both occur, and the distribution is lumpy. A trader who must produce a fixed monthly amount will force trades in conditions where their strategy has no edge, simply to "make the month." Forced trades are where most of the other eight causes pile up at once: oversized, overfrequent, revenge-driven, and held without exits.

The honest framing is different. The first year of trading is mostly education with real stakes, and the tuition is paid in small, controlled losses. Traders who survive that year with their capital and their discipline intact are the ones who eventually have a chance at consistency.

Traders who demand income immediately usually pay much higher tuition, much faster.

### The fix: separate learning capital from living expenses

- **Step 1.** Trade only with money you can afford to lose completely without affecting rent, EMIs, or family expenses. If losing the account would change your life, the account is too large — or trading should wait.
- **Step 2.** Set a learning budget, not an income target, for the first year: for example, a written rule to risk at most ₹2,000 a month while learning, and to judge performance on whether the rules were followed, not on profit.
- **Step 3.** Measure the right scoreboard. For the first 50 trades, track rule-following rate (how many trades matched the written plan) alongside profit and loss. A trader following rules at 90% with small losses is learning; a trader at 50% with a lucky profit is not.
- **Step 4.** Study risk of ruin before increasing size. [Risk of ruin](/articles/risk-of-ruin-trading/) shows, with numbers, why surviving is a precondition for compounding — and why impatience is the most expensive trait in trading.

## The Survival Checklist: 7 Rules That Keep You in the Game

If the nine causes above feel like a lot, here is the entire article compressed into seven rules. A trader who follows these seven rules will not be guaranteed profits — no honest article can promise that — but they will have removed the leaks that destroy most accounts.

1. **Risk 1% per trade, no exceptions.** Compute position size from your stop-loss, not from confidence.
2. **Set a daily loss limit of 2–3% and stop when it hits.** Use the broker kill-switch so the rule enforces itself.
3. **Cap trades at 3 per day.** Planned setups only; boredom is not a setup.
4. **Never buy an option you cannot explain the decay cost of.** Avoid same-day-expiry speculation as a default activity.
5. **Count costs before counting profits.** If cost per trade exceeds expected profit per trade, the strategy cannot work.
6. **Place every stop as a live order before entry.** Never average down on a losing trade unless the plan said so in advance.
7. **Trade only with money you can afford to lose.** Judge your first 50 trades on rule-following, not on profit.

Tape this list next to your screen. The traders who survive are not the ones with the cleverest indicators. They are the ones whose bad days are small, whose costs are counted, and whose accounts are still alive when their edge finally gets enough trades to show up.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do 90% of traders lose money?

Because the "90%" figure describes individual traders in the equity F&O segment, where leverage, short-dated options, and high frequency combine. SEBI's studies found roughly 90–91% of individual F&O traders incurred net losses in each year from FY22 to FY25, easing to 87.7% in FY26. The main drivers are oversized positions, overtrading, options time decay, and transaction costs — not a lack of entry signals.

The fix is the survival checklist above: 1% risk per trade, a daily loss limit, capped trade frequency, and counted costs.

### Why do beginners lose money in trading?

Beginners lose mainly because they trade before they have rules. They size positions by gut feel instead of stop-loss math, trade without a daily loss limit, copy tips without an exit plan, and buy short-dated options without understanding time decay. They also expect income too early, which forces trades in conditions where they have no edge.

The remedy is unglamorous: write the rules down, risk 1% per trade, cap trades at 3 per day, and judge your first 50 trades on rule-following rather than profit.

### Is options trading riskier than stocks?

Yes, for most retail traders, and for specific mechanical reasons. Options involve leverage, time decay, and expiry: a bought option loses value every day even if the underlying does not move, and it can expire worthless, losing the entire premium. Plain cash delivery stock purchases, bought with your own money, cannot lose more than the amount invested and have no expiry clock.

SEBI's FY26 data reflects this concentration of risk: options accounted for about 92% of individual F&O losses. Options are not inherently bad instruments, but buying short-dated options as a beginner's default activity is one of the fastest ways to lose capital.

### Can you lose more money than you invest?

It depends on the product. In a plain cash delivery trade bought with your own money, no — the maximum loss is the amount invested. In leveraged positions such as futures, options selling, or margin-funded trades, yes — losses can exceed the margin you posted, because you control a much larger exposure than your deposit.

In that case the broker can issue a margin call demanding more funds, or square off the position, possibly locking in a loss larger than your original deposit. This is why leveraged position sizing must be even stricter than cash position sizing.

### Does overtrading cause losses?

Yes, through two channels. First, every trade adds transaction costs — STT, exchange charges, and brokerage — so frequency directly increases the cost drag on the account. Second, trade quality falls as frequency rises: the first few trades of the day are usually the planned ones, while later trades are typically driven by boredom, FOMO, or revenge after a loss.

Capping trades at 3 planned setups per day, and tracking which trades actually make money, is the practical fix. Most traders who measure this find their profit comes from their early trades and their losses from everything after.

### How can traders control their losses?

Control losses with rules decided before trading, not with willpower during trading. Risk no more than 1% of your account per trade, computed from your stop-loss distance. Set a daily loss limit of 2–3% and stop for the day when it hits, ideally enforced by your broker's kill-switch feature.

Place every stop-loss as a live order, never as a mental note. Cap trades at 3 per day. Avoid averaging down on losing trades unless the written plan allowed it in advance.

Review your trades weekly and cut whatever pattern — a time of day, an instrument, a setup — produces the largest losses. Loss control is a system, not a personality trait.

### Is intraday trading profitable in India?

It can be, but the data says it is difficult, and it is hardest for the traders who need it most. SEBI's equity cash-segment study found 7 out of 10 individual intraday traders made losses in FY2023. Intraday trading compresses every cause in this article into a few hours: more trades, more costs, faster emotional decisions, and no overnight recovery time.

It becomes viable only with the full discipline stack — 1% risk per trade, a daily loss limit, capped frequency, counted costs, and a written plan followed for at least 50 trades before judging results. A beginner who cannot yet follow rules on slower time frames should not expect to follow them intraday, where decisions are fastest and emotions run highest.
